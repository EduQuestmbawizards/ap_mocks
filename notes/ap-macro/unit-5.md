## 5.1 Fiscal and Monetary Policy Actions in the Short Run

### 5.1.1 Recap: Stabilization Policy
**Stabilization policy** = demand-side policy used to smooth the business cycle and close output gaps.
- **Fiscal policy:** changes in **G and T** (Congress/government).
- **Monetary policy:** changes in the **money supply and interest rates** (central bank).

| | **Recessionary gap** (Y < Yf, high unemployment) | **Inflationary gap** (Y > Yf, high inflation) |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal** | Expansionary: **G ↑, T ↓** | Contractionary: **G ↓, T ↑** |
| **Monetary** | Expansionary: **buy bonds, lower rates, MS ↑** | Contractionary: **sell bonds, raise rates, MS ↓** |
| **AD** | Shifts **right** | Shifts **left** |

### 5.1.2 Multi-Market Effects of Each Policy
**Expansionary fiscal policy (financed by borrowing):**
- **AD-AS:** AD ↑ (by multiplier × ΔG, less crowding out) → Y ↑, PL ↑, U ↓.
- **Loanable funds:** government borrows → **demand for loanable funds ↑ → real interest rate ↑** → **private investment ↓ (crowding out).**
- **Money market:** higher real GDP and prices → **money demand ↑ → nominal interest rate ↑.**
- **Foreign exchange:** higher interest rates attract **capital inflows** → **currency appreciates** → **net exports ↓** (partly offsetting the AD gain).

**Expansionary monetary policy:**
- **Money market:** MS ↑ → **nominal interest rate ↓** (real rate ↓ in the short run).
- **AD-AS:** investment ↑ and net exports ↑ → **AD ↑** → Y ↑, PL ↑, U ↓.
- **Foreign exchange:** lower interest rates → capital outflows → **currency depreciates** → **net exports ↑** (reinforcing the AD gain).
- **Loanable funds:** the lower real rate encourages more borrowing for investment (movement along demand).

**Contractionary policies work in the opposite directions.**

### 5.1.3 Combining Fiscal and Monetary Policy (Policy Mix)
| Policy mix | Effect on AD | Effect on interest rates | Effect on investment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expansionary fiscal + expansionary monetary** | **Strong increase** (AD ↑↑) | **Ambiguous** (fiscal pushes up, monetary pushes down) | Ambiguous |
| **Contractionary fiscal + contractionary monetary** | **Strong decrease** (AD ↓↓) | **Ambiguous** | Ambiguous |
| **Expansionary fiscal + contractionary monetary** | **Ambiguous** | **Rises sharply** (↑↑) | **Falls** (heavy crowding out); currency appreciates |
| **Contractionary fiscal + expansionary monetary** | **Ambiguous** | **Falls sharply** (↓↓) | **Rises** (promotes investment/growth); currency depreciates |

> **Exam rule:** when two policies push a variable in opposite directions, the outcome for that variable is **indeterminate** unless the question states relative sizes.

### 5.1.4 Policy Dilemmas
- **Stagflation (supply shock):** fighting unemployment worsens inflation (and vice versa).
- **Time lags:** by the time policy takes effect, the economy may have already self-corrected.
- **Expectations:** if households and firms anticipate policy, effects can be muted.
- **Short-run vs. long-run trade-off:** policies that help in the short run (deficit spending, easy money) can have **long-run costs** (debt, inflation, crowding out), which is the core theme of this unit.

## 5.2 The Phillips Curve

### 5.2.1 Short-Run Phillips Curve (SRPC)
The **short-run Phillips curve** shows an **inverse (negative) relationship between the inflation rate and the unemployment rate** in the short run.
- **Vertical axis:** **inflation rate (%)**. **Horizontal axis:** **unemployment rate (%)**.
- **Downward sloping:** lower unemployment goes with **higher** inflation (tight labor market → wages rise → prices rise), and vice versa.
- Reflects the **short-run trade-off** between inflation and unemployment.

### 5.2.2 Connection to the AD-AS Model
| AD-AS event | Phillips curve movement |
|---|---|
| **AD increases** (Y ↑, PL ↑, U ↓) | **Movement UP and LEFT along the SRPC** (higher inflation, lower unemployment) |
| **AD decreases** (Y ↓, PL ↓, U ↑) | **Movement DOWN and RIGHT along the SRPC** |
| **SRAS decreases** (negative supply shock: Y ↓, PL ↑, U ↑) | **SRPC shifts UP/RIGHT** (stagflation: higher inflation **and** higher unemployment) |
| **SRAS increases** (positive supply shock) | **SRPC shifts DOWN/LEFT** |

> **Key distinction:** **demand-side changes move you ALONG the SRPC; supply-side changes (and changes in expected inflation) SHIFT the SRPC.**

### 5.2.3 Long-Run Phillips Curve (LRPC)
- The **LRPC is a vertical line at the natural rate of unemployment (NRU).**
- In the long run, there is **no trade-off** between inflation and unemployment: unemployment returns to the natural rate whatever the inflation rate.
- The LRPC corresponds to the **vertical LRAS** at potential output (Yf).
- **LRPC shifts:** if the **natural rate changes** (e.g., due to changes in labor-market structure, unemployment benefits, demographics, technology), the LRPC (and LRAS) shifts. NRU ↓ → LRPC shifts **left**; NRU ↑ → shifts **right.**

### 5.2.4 Role of Expected Inflation
The **position of the SRPC depends on expected inflation.**
- **Expected inflation ↑ → SRPC shifts UP (and right).** Workers demand higher nominal wages to protect purchasing power, raising costs and shifting SRAS left.
- **Expected inflation ↓ → SRPC shifts DOWN (and left).**
- At the point where **actual inflation = expected inflation, unemployment = the natural rate** (the SRPC crosses the LRPC at the natural rate).

### 5.2.5 Why the Trade-off Disappears in the Long Run (Worked Scenario)
Assume the NRU = 5% and expected inflation = 2% (SRPC₁ crosses the LRPC at U = 5%, inflation = 2%).

1. **Policymakers use expansionary policy to reduce unemployment to 4%.** AD ↑ → movement along SRPC₁ to **U = 4%, inflation = 4%.**
2. **Workers observe higher inflation (4%)** → expect it to continue → **demand higher nominal wages.**
3. **SRAS shifts left** (higher costs) → output falls back to Yf → **U returns to 5%** at the **higher inflation of 4%.**
4. **SRPC shifts up** (SRPC₂ with expected inflation 4%) → the economy is at **U = 5%, inflation = 4%** (a point on the LRPC).
5. To push U back to 4% again would need **even higher inflation** (accelerating inflation).

**Conclusion:** attempts to keep unemployment **below the natural rate** only produce **permanently higher inflation.** In the long run, **expansionary policy raises prices, not real output.**

### 5.2.6 Disinflation and the Cost of Reducing Inflation
- To **reduce** inflation, the central bank uses **contractionary policy**, causing **temporary higher unemployment** (movement down the SRPC) until expectations adjust (SRPC shifts down).
- **Sacrifice ratio:** the output (or employment) lost per percentage-point reduction in inflation.
- **Credibility** of the central bank and **rational expectations** (people form expectations using all available information) can reduce the cost: if people believe the policy, expected inflation falls quickly and **SRPC shifts down faster**.
- **Adaptive expectations:** people base expectations on past inflation (slow adjustment).

### 5.2.7 Historical Example
- **1960s:** apparent stable trade-off.
- **1970s:** **oil shocks** → **stagflation**: SRAS shifted left; SRPC shifted up/right; high inflation and high unemployment, undermining the idea of a stable trade-off.
- **Early 1980s:** the Fed's contractionary policy brought inflation down at the cost of a deep recession (high unemployment) → **disinflation.**

## 5.3 Money Growth and Inflation

### 5.3.1 The Quantity Theory of Money
**M × V = P × Y**
(M = money supply, V = velocity, P = price level, Y = real GDP; **P × Y = nominal GDP**)

In growth-rate form: **%ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY**

If **V is constant** and **Y grows at a rate determined by real factors** (long run):
**Inflation rate (%ΔP) = Money growth (%ΔM) − Real GDP growth (%ΔY)**

**Example:** M grows 8%, real GDP grows 3%, V constant → **inflation = 5%.**
**Example 2:** M grows 12%, Y grows 2%, V constant → inflation = 10%.

### 5.3.2 Long-Run Money Neutrality
- In the long run, **changes in the money supply affect only nominal variables** (price level, nominal wages, nominal interest rates, exchange rates) and **not real variables** (real GDP, real wages, real interest rates, unemployment).
- **Sustained inflation is always a monetary phenomenon**: it is caused by money growth that exceeds real output growth.
- **Short run:** with sticky prices/wages, changes in money supply **do** affect real GDP and unemployment.

### 5.3.3 Hyperinflation and the Inflation Tax
- **Hyperinflation** (inflation over about 50% per month) usually arises when a government **finances large deficits by printing money (monetizing the debt).**
- **Inflation tax:** inflation acts as a tax on **holders of money** (their money loses value), transferring resources to the government (**seigniorage**).
- Hyperinflation destroys the value of money, savings, and planning; economic activity collapses (Zimbabwe, Weimar Germany, Venezuela).

### 5.3.4 Money Growth, Inflation and Interest Rates (Fisher Effect)
- **Nominal interest rate ≈ Real interest rate + Expected inflation.**
- **Higher sustained money growth → higher expected inflation → higher nominal interest rates** in the long run, but the **real interest rate is unchanged** (determined by saving and investment in the loanable funds market).
- **Short run:** an increase in MS **lowers** the nominal interest rate (liquidity effect); **long run:** nominal rates **rise** with higher inflation expectations.

### 5.3.5 Central Bank Credibility
- Central banks with **independence** and a **credible inflation target** (e.g., 2%) can **anchor inflation expectations**, making inflation control less costly.
- **Time inconsistency:** governments may be tempted to inflate for short-term gains; independence and rules (inflation targeting) counter this.

## 5.4 Government Deficits and the National Debt

### 5.4.1 Definitions
- **Budget deficit** (a **flow**): in one year, **government spending (G + transfers + interest payments) > tax revenue (T).**
- **Budget surplus:** T > spending. **Balanced budget:** T = spending.
- **National (public) debt** (a **stock**): the **total accumulated** amount the government owes, i.e., the sum of past deficits minus past surpluses.
- **Deficits add to the debt; surpluses reduce it.** The **deficit is not the same as the debt.**

### 5.4.2 Types of Deficits
- **Cyclical deficit:** caused by the **business cycle** (recessions reduce tax revenue and raise transfers; automatic stabilizers). It tends to disappear when the economy returns to full employment.
- **Structural deficit:** the deficit that would exist **even at full employment** (a permanent imbalance between spending and revenue).
- **Primary deficit:** the deficit **excluding interest payments** on existing debt.

### 5.4.3 Financing a Deficit
1. **Borrowing** (selling government bonds) to the public, banks, foreigners → increases demand for loanable funds (crowding out).
2. **Printing money / monetizing the debt** (central bank buys the bonds) → increases the money supply → **inflation** (inflation tax).
3. **Raising future taxes or cutting future spending.**

### 5.4.4 Debt-to-GDP Ratio
**Debt-to-GDP ratio = National debt ÷ GDP (nominal) × 100.** It measures the **burden** relative to the economy's ability to pay.

**Example:** Debt = ₹20 trillion; GDP = ₹25 trillion → **ratio = 80%.**
Next year: deficit = ₹0.3 trillion → debt = ₹20.3 trillion; nominal GDP grows 3% to ₹25.75 trillion → ratio = 20.3 ÷ 25.75 = **78.8%** (the ratio **falls** even with a deficit, because GDP grows faster than the debt).

**Rule of thumb:** the debt-to-GDP ratio **rises** if the interest rate on debt exceeds the growth rate of GDP (**r > g**) and the government runs a primary deficit; it can fall when **g > r.**

### 5.4.5 Who Holds the Debt?
- **Internally held debt (to domestic citizens):** payments of interest are transfers within the country (but still involve taxes and distortions).
- **Externally held debt (to foreigners):** interest payments transfer **real resources abroad**; a larger burden on national income.

### 5.4.6 Consequences and Burden of the Debt
- **Higher interest payments** crowd out other government spending or require higher taxes.
- **Crowding out of private investment** (higher real interest rates) → lower capital stock → **lower long-run growth.**
- **Burden on future generations:** higher taxes or lower public services; but if borrowing finances **productive investment** (infrastructure, education, R&D) whose return exceeds the interest cost, future generations may benefit.
- Risk of **default, loss of confidence, higher risk premiums, currency depreciation** (if externally financed).
- Possible **inflation** if monetized.
- **Counterpoint:** in a **deep recession**, deficits stabilize the economy; **Ricardian equivalence** argues households save more in anticipation of future taxes, offsetting the stimulus.

### 5.4.7 Deficits and Trade (Twin Deficits)
A **budget deficit** can raise real interest rates → attracts **foreign capital inflows** → **currency appreciates** → **net exports fall** → larger **trade (current-account) deficit.** This link is the "**twin deficits**" idea (Unit 6).

## 5.5 Crowding Out

### 5.5.1 Definition
**Crowding out** is the **reduction in private investment (and interest-sensitive consumption)** caused by **higher interest rates resulting from government borrowing.**

### 5.5.2 Mechanism
Government deficit → **demand for loanable funds shifts right** → **real interest rate ↑** → **private investment ↓** → lower **capital accumulation** → lower **future potential output (LRAS grows more slowly).**

### 5.5.3 Numerical Example
(Using the loanable funds example from Unit 4)
- Initial: r = 6%, funds = 400 (all private investment).
- Government borrows 100 at any rate → new equilibrium: **r = 7%**, funds = 450; government takes 100, so **private investment = 350 (down from 400)**.
- **Crowding out = 50** (partial: total spending still increases by the government's 100, while private investment falls by 50).

### 5.5.4 Effects in Other Markets
- **AD-AS:** the **rightward AD shift from fiscal expansion is smaller** than the simple multiplier suggests, because higher interest rates reduce investment (and attract capital → appreciation → NX falls).
- **Foreign exchange:** higher real rates lead to **appreciation** of the currency, reducing net exports ("crowding out" of net exports).
- **Long run:** lower investment = lower capital stock = **slower growth.**

### 5.5.5 When Is Crowding Out Smaller?
- In a **deep recession with idle resources and low interest rates** (or at the zero lower bound), borrowing needs don't push up rates much; the central bank may accommodate (monetary policy keeps rates low).
- If the government spending is **productive** (infrastructure), future gains may offset it (**"crowding in"**).
- If **foreign savers** supply funds (capital inflows) to finance the deficit.
- If **higher incomes** stimulate private saving and optimism ("crowding in").

### 5.5.6 Deficit Reduction Effects
Reducing the deficit (or running a surplus) → supply of loanable funds ↑ (public saving ↑) or demand ↓ → **real interest rate ↓** → **investment ↑** → higher long-run growth. In the **short run**, deficit reduction reduces AD (contractionary).

## 5.6 Economic Growth

### 5.6.1 Definition and Measurement
**Economic growth** is the **increase in an economy's capacity to produce goods and services over time.**
- **Measured by:** the growth rate of **real GDP**, and especially **real GDP per capita** (for living standards).
- **Growth rate = (Real GDP₂ − Real GDP₁) ÷ Real GDP₁ × 100.**
- On the graphs: **PPC shifts outward; LRAS shifts right.**

### 5.6.2 Rule of 70
**Years to double ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate (%).**
- 2% growth → 35 years; 3% → ≈ 23 years; 4% → 17.5 years; 7% → 10 years.
- **Small differences in growth rates compound into huge differences in living standards over decades.**

### 5.6.3 Productivity
**Labor productivity = Real GDP ÷ hours worked (output per worker).** The main driver of rising living standards. Growth of real GDP per capita comes mainly from **productivity growth.**

### 5.6.4 Determinants (Sources) of Growth
**Production function: Y = A × F(K, L, H, N)** (A = technology/total factor productivity; K = physical capital; L = labor; H = human capital; N = natural resources).

1. **Physical capital (K):** more machines, factories and infrastructure per worker (**capital deepening**), financed by **saving and investment.**
2. **Human capital (H):** education, training, health, experience.
3. **Technological progress (A):** innovation, R&D, new methods, entrepreneurship. Often the **most important long-run source**.
4. **Natural resources (N).**
5. **Quantity of labor (L):** population growth, labor-force participation, immigration (affects total GDP; not necessarily per capita).
6. **Institutions:** property rights, rule of law, low corruption, political stability, sound money, competitive markets, openness to trade and investment.

### 5.6.5 Diminishing Returns to Capital and Convergence
- Adding **more capital per worker** (with other inputs fixed) yields **diminishing returns**, so growth from capital accumulation alone slows.
- **Conditional convergence ("catch-up" growth):** poorer countries with similar institutions tend to **grow faster** because they can adopt existing technology and have higher returns to capital; but many poor countries don't catch up because of weak institutions.
- **Sustained growth requires technological progress.**

### 5.6.6 Costs and Limits of Growth
- **Opportunity cost:** more saving/investment means **less current consumption.**
- **Environmental degradation** and resource depletion (GDP growth doesn't account for it).
- **Inequality** may increase; **adjustment costs** (structural unemployment from technological change).
- **Business cycles** cause short-run deviations from trend growth.

## 5.7 Public Policy and Economic Growth

### 5.7.1 Policies to Promote Long-Run Growth (Supply-Side)
| Policy | How it raises growth |
|---|---|
| **Education and training; health** | Raises **human capital** and productivity |
| **Support for R&D** (patents, subsidies, research grants, tax credits) | Promotes **technology**; addresses the **positive externality** of knowledge |
| **Infrastructure investment** (roads, ports, power, broadband) | Raises productivity of private capital |
| **Incentives for saving and investment** (tax breaks, stable financial system, lower deficits) | Shifts **loanable funds supply right** → lower real rate → more investment |
| **Free trade, openness, foreign investment** | Larger markets, technology transfer, competition, specialization |
| **Strong institutions:** property rights, rule of law, contract enforcement, anti-corruption | Encourages investment and innovation |
| **Competition policy / deregulation (where efficient)** | Reduces barriers, spurs efficiency |
| **Stable macroeconomy** (low inflation, manageable debt) | Reduces uncertainty |
| **Immigration policies** | Increase labor force and skills |

### 5.7.2 Supply-Side Policies in the AD-AS Model
- Successful supply-side policies shift **LRAS (and SRAS) to the right** → **higher real GDP, lower price level.**
- **Supply-side tax cuts** (cutting marginal rates) aim to increase incentives to work, save and invest. Critics note **revenue loss and larger deficits**; evidence on the **Laffer curve** suggests tax cuts raise revenue only at very high tax rates.

### 5.7.3 Policy Trade-offs
- **Short-run stabilization vs. long-run growth:** large persistent deficits can **crowd out investment**, lowering long-run growth.
- **Equity vs. efficiency:** policies that raise growth may widen inequality.
- **Growth vs. environment.**
- **Consumption vs. investment today.**

### 5.7.4 Summary: Long-Run Consequences of Stabilization Policies
| Policy action | Short-run effect | Long-run consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Expansionary monetary policy** | AD ↑, Y ↑, U ↓, interest rate ↓ | **Only inflation** rises (money neutrality); SRPC shifts up if sustained |
| **Expansionary fiscal policy (deficit-financed)** | AD ↑, Y ↑, U ↓ | **Debt ↑, crowding out, real interest rate ↑**, lower investment and growth |
| **Contractionary policies** | AD ↓, Y ↓, U ↑, inflation ↓ | Lower inflation expectations; SRPC shifts down |
| **Supply-side policies** | Raises SRAS/LRAS | **Higher potential growth** |
| **Continued attempts to keep U below natural rate** | U ↓ temporarily | **Accelerating inflation**, no change in real output |

## Unit 5 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- **M × V = P × Y;** **Inflation ≈ Money growth − Real GDP growth** (V constant).
- **Real interest rate ≈ Nominal − Expected inflation.**
- **Debt-to-GDP = Debt ÷ GDP.**
- **Growth rate = ΔY ÷ Y × 100; Rule of 70 (doubling time).**
- **Phillips curve:** SRPC downward sloping; **LRPC vertical at NRU**.

**Key terms glossary:** stabilization policy, policy mix, Phillips curve (short-run, long-run), natural rate of unemployment, expected inflation, adaptive/rational expectations, disinflation, sacrifice ratio, stagflation, quantity theory of money, velocity, money neutrality, hyperinflation, inflation tax, seigniorage, budget deficit/surplus, national debt, cyclical/structural/primary deficit, debt-to-GDP ratio, monetizing debt, Ricardian equivalence, crowding out, twin deficits, economic growth, productivity, human capital, physical capital, technology, conditional convergence, institutions, supply-side policies.

**Top exam traps**
1. **AD changes → movement along SRPC; SRAS changes (or expected inflation) → shift the SRPC.**
2. **LRPC is vertical at the natural rate**, no long-run trade-off.
3. **Negative supply shock → SRPC shifts right/up** (stagflation).
4. **Deficit ≠ debt** (flow vs. stock).
5. **Crowding out lowers private investment** via higher **real** interest rates (not by "using up" money).
6. **Money growth in excess of real output growth causes inflation** in the long run; **money is neutral** in the long run.
7. **Mixed policy combos → indeterminate effects** on the variables pulling in opposite directions.
8. **Rule of 70** uses the **growth rate in percent**.
9. **Growth = LRAS/PPC shift**; recoveries from recessions are **not** growth.
10. Expansionary fiscal policy → **currency appreciates** (higher rates); expansionary monetary policy → **depreciates**.

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** An increase in aggregate demand will cause, along the short-run Phillips curve:
(A) A rise in inflation and a fall in unemployment (B) A fall in inflation and a rise in unemployment (C) A shift of the curve to the right (D) A shift of the curve to the left
**Answer: A.** Movement up and left along the SRPC.

**2.** The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at:
(A) Zero inflation (B) The natural rate of unemployment (C) Zero unemployment (D) The inflation target
**Answer: B.**

**3.** A negative supply shock (e.g., an oil-price spike) will:
(A) Shift the SRPC to the left (B) Shift the SRPC to the right (C) Shift the LRPC left (D) Move the economy along the SRPC
**Answer: B.**

**4.** If the money supply grows 9%, real GDP grows 4%, and velocity is constant, inflation will be about:
(A) 4% (B) 5% (C) 9% (D) 13%
**Answer: B.** 9% − 4% = 5%.

**5.** Which statement is correct?
(A) The national debt is the annual shortfall of tax revenue (B) A budget deficit adds to the national debt (C) A budget surplus increases the debt (D) The debt and the deficit are the same
**Answer: B.**

**6.** Government borrowing to finance a deficit tends to:
(A) Lower the real interest rate and raise investment (B) Raise the real interest rate and crowd out private investment (C) Have no effect on interest rates (D) Increase the supply of loanable funds
**Answer: B.**

**7.** At a constant 2.5% annual growth rate, real GDP will double in approximately:
(A) 14 years (B) 28 years (C) 35 years (D) 70 years
**Answer: B.** 70 ÷ 2.5 = 28.

**8.** Which policy mix would most likely lower interest rates?
(A) Expansionary fiscal and contractionary monetary (B) Contractionary fiscal and expansionary monetary (C) Expansionary fiscal and expansionary monetary (D) Contractionary fiscal and contractionary monetary
**Answer: B.** Lower government borrowing reduces demand for loanable funds, and the money supply increase lowers nominal rates.

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** The economy is at long-run equilibrium with the natural rate of unemployment at 5% and expected inflation at 2%. The central bank unexpectedly expands the money supply.
(a) Draw a correctly labeled short-run and long-run Phillips curve graph showing the initial equilibrium.
(b) Show and explain the short-run effect of the policy on inflation and unemployment.
(c) Explain what happens in the long run as expectations adjust. Show it on the graph.
(d) Explain the long-run effect on the nominal interest rate and the real interest rate.

**Answer:**
(a) SRPC₁ (downward sloping) intersects the vertical LRPC at U = 5%, inflation = 2%.
(b) AD increases → **movement up/left along SRPC₁**: unemployment falls below 5% and inflation rises above 2%.
(c) Workers and firms revise expected inflation upward → **SRAS shifts left, SRPC shifts up to SRPC₂** → unemployment returns to **5% (LRPC)** at a **higher inflation rate**.
(d) **Nominal interest rate rises** (higher expected inflation, Fisher effect); the **real interest rate is unchanged** (money is neutral in the long run).

**Q2.** The government runs a budget deficit financed by borrowing from private savers. Debt = ₹10 trillion; GDP = ₹20 trillion.
(a) Calculate the debt-to-GDP ratio.
(b) If the next year's deficit is ₹0.5 trillion and nominal GDP grows to ₹21 trillion, calculate the new ratio and interpret.
(c) Draw the loanable funds market showing the effect of the deficit on the real interest rate.
(d) Explain how this affects long-run growth.

**Answer:**
(a) 10 ÷ 20 = **50%**.
(b) Debt = 10.5; GDP = 21 → **50%**: unchanged (debt grew 5%, GDP grew 5%): the burden relative to the economy is stable.
(c) **Demand for loanable funds shifts right** → **real interest rate rises**.
(d) The higher real rate **crowds out private investment** → smaller capital stock → **slower growth of potential output (LRAS)**.
