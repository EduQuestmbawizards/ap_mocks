## 6.1 Balance of Payments Accounts

### 6.1.1 What is the Balance of Payments (BOP)?
The **balance of payments** is a **systematic record of all economic transactions between the residents of one country and the rest of the world during a period (usually a year).** Every transaction has two sides, so the BOP **always balances** (apart from statistical discrepancy).

**BOP identity:**
**Current account balance + Capital and financial account balance = 0**
(Equivalently: **a current account deficit must be matched by a capital/financial account surplus**, and vice versa. Official reserve changes are included in the financial account.)

### 6.1.2 The Current Account
Records the **flows of goods, services, income and transfers.**
1. **Trade in goods and services (net exports, NX = X − M):**
   - **Exports** of goods and services (**inflow of money**, credit +).
   - **Imports** of goods and services (**outflow of money**, debit −).
   - **Balance of trade** = value of exports − value of imports (goods only is the "merchandise trade balance").
2. **Net primary income (investment/factor income):** interest, dividends, profits and wages earned abroad minus those paid to foreigners (e.g., interest paid to a foreign bondholder is an outflow).
3. **Net secondary income (unilateral transfers):** foreign aid, **remittances** sent by workers abroad, gifts, pensions.

**Current account balance = NX + net primary income + net secondary income.**
- **Current account deficit:** outflows > inflows (the country is a **net borrower** from the rest of the world).
- **Current account surplus:** inflows > outflows (net lender).

### 6.1.3 The Capital Account and the Financial Account
- **Financial account:** records **purchases and sales of financial and real assets** across borders (cross-border investment):
  - **Foreign direct investment (FDI):** building or buying factories, businesses (long-term control, e.g., Samsung builds a plant in India).
  - **Portfolio investment:** purchases of **stocks and bonds** and bank deposits.
  - **Changes in official reserve assets** (central bank holdings of foreign currencies, gold).
- **Capital account (small):** transfers of non-produced, non-financial assets (debt forgiveness, patents, migrants' transfers).
- **Net capital inflow** = foreigners' purchases of domestic assets − domestic residents' purchases of foreign assets. **A financial account surplus means money is flowing IN** (the country is selling assets/borrowing from abroad).

**Direction rules (from the home country's perspective):**
| Transaction | Where recorded | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Exports** of goods/services | Current account | + (credit) |
| **Imports** of goods/services | Current account | − (debit) |
| Interest/dividends **received** from abroad | Current account (primary income) | + |
| Interest/dividends **paid** to foreigners | Current account (primary income) | − |
| Foreign aid **given**, remittances **sent abroad** | Current account (secondary income) | − |
| **Foreigners buy** domestic stocks, bonds, property; **FDI into** the home country | Financial account | + (capital inflow) |
| **Domestic residents buy** foreign assets; **FDI out of** the home country | Financial account | − (capital outflow) |

### 6.1.4 Numerical Examples
**(a) Current account:** Exports of goods = 500; imports of goods = 600; net services = +50; net primary income = −20; net secondary income = −10.
- Trade balance (goods) = −100; **NX (goods and services) = −100 + 50 = −50.**
- **Current account = −50 − 20 − 10 = −80 (a deficit).**
- Therefore **capital/financial account = +80** (net capital inflow; foreigners acquire 80 of domestic assets, or the country borrows 80).

**(b) Classify:**
- Indian IT firm exports software services to the U.S. → **current account (+)**.
- A U.S. mutual fund buys shares on the Indian stock exchange → **financial account (+, inflow into India)**.
- An Indian worker in Dubai sends money home → India's **secondary income (+)**.
- Indian tourist spending in Paris → **import of services, current account (−)**.
- A Japanese company builds a factory in Gujarat → **FDI inflow: financial account (+)**.

### 6.1.5 Meaning of Deficits and Surpluses
- A **trade/current account deficit** doesn't automatically mean the economy is weak: it can reflect **strong domestic investment financed by foreign savings** (e.g., a fast-growing economy). But it also means the country is **accumulating foreign liabilities** (net debtor), which must be repaid or serviced.
- A **surplus** means the country is a net lender and accumulates foreign assets.

### 6.1.6 Link to Saving and Investment (Open Economy Identity)
**National saving (S) = Domestic investment (I) + Net capital outflow (NCO)**, and **NCO = NX.**
- If **S < I** (the country invests more than it saves) → **NCO < 0** → **net capital inflow** and **NX < 0** (current account deficit).
- A **government budget deficit** lowers national saving → **less capital outflow / more inflow → NX falls**: the **"twin deficits"** link (budget deficit and trade deficit).

## 6.2 Exchange Rates

### 6.2.1 Definition
The **exchange rate** is the **price of one country's currency in terms of another currency.**
- *Example:* ₹83 per $1 → **₹83/$** (the price of one dollar is ₹83); the **reciprocal** is $1/83 = **$0.012 per ₹** (the price of one rupee in dollars).

### 6.2.2 Appreciation and Depreciation
- **Appreciation:** a currency **becomes more valuable** relative to another (it buys **more** foreign currency).
- **Depreciation:** a currency **becomes less valuable** (it buys **less** foreign currency).
- **Both currencies move oppositely:** if the dollar appreciates against the rupee (₹83 → ₹90 per $), the **rupee depreciates** against the dollar.
- *Example:* Rupee rises from **₹90/$ to ₹80/$**: the rupee has **appreciated** (fewer rupees needed to buy a dollar).

### 6.2.3 Effects on Prices (Worked Examples)
**Rupee appreciation: ₹90/$ → ₹80/$**
- An Indian shirt priced **₹900**: for a U.S. buyer, **$10 → $11.25** (900/80) → **Indian exports become more expensive.**
- A U.S. gadget priced **$100**: for an Indian buyer, **₹9,000 → ₹8,000** → **imports become cheaper.**
- So **appreciation → exports ↓, imports ↑ → net exports ↓.**

**Rupee depreciation: ₹83/$ → ₹90/$**
- Indian IT services worth **$1,000** earn **₹83,000 → ₹90,000** (exporters gain in rupees); for foreigners, Indian goods are **cheaper**.
- A **$100** import costs ₹8,300 → **₹9,000** (imports dearer).
- So **depreciation → exports ↑, imports ↓ → net exports ↑.**

### 6.2.4 Exchange Rate Systems
- **Flexible (floating) exchange rate:** determined by **supply and demand** in the foreign exchange market (the central bank does not intervene, or only occasionally).
- **Fixed (pegged) exchange rate:** the government/central bank **sets the rate** and intervenes (buying/selling foreign currency, using **foreign reserves**) to maintain it.
- **Managed (dirty) float:** mostly market-determined, with occasional central bank intervention (India's rupee is a managed float).
- **Devaluation/revaluation** = deliberate downward/upward change in a **fixed** rate; appreciation/depreciation = changes in a **floating** rate.

### 6.2.5 Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) — Background
**Law of one price:** identical goods should cost the same in different countries when prices are converted at the exchange rate. In the long run, exchange rates tend to move to equalize the price of a basket of goods. *Example:* if a basket costs ₹830 in India and $10 in the U.S., the PPP exchange rate is **₹83/$.**
Countries with **higher inflation tend to see their currencies depreciate** in the long run.

## 6.3 The Foreign Exchange Market

### 6.3.1 The Model
The **foreign exchange (forex) market** is where currencies are traded. In the AP model, we analyze **one currency at a time**:
- **Vertical axis:** the **exchange rate = price of the currency in terms of the other** (e.g., **euros per dollar**, **€/$**; or **dollars per rupee, $/₹**).
- **Horizontal axis:** **quantity of that currency** (e.g., dollars) traded.

### 6.3.2 Demand for a Currency (Downward Sloping)
The demand for (say) **dollars** comes from **foreigners** who need dollars to buy:
- **U.S. exports** of goods and services,
- **U.S. financial and real assets** (stocks, bonds, property, FDI),
- and from tourists visiting the U.S.
**Why downward sloping?** A **higher exchange rate (more euros per dollar; dollar appreciates)** makes U.S. goods and assets **more expensive** for foreigners → they buy **less** → **quantity of dollars demanded falls.**

### 6.3.3 Supply of a Currency (Upward Sloping)
The supply of **dollars** comes from **Americans** who sell dollars to get foreign currency to buy:
- **Imports** of foreign goods and services,
- **Foreign assets** (stocks, bonds, FDI).
**Why upward sloping?** A **higher exchange rate (dollar appreciates)** makes foreign goods and assets **cheaper for Americans** → they buy **more imports/foreign assets** → they **supply more dollars.**

### 6.3.4 Equilibrium Exchange Rate
Where the **demand for the currency = supply of the currency.**
- If the exchange rate is **above** equilibrium → surplus of the currency (excess supply) → it depreciates toward equilibrium.
- If **below** → shortage → currency appreciates.

### 6.3.5 Two Sides of the Same Transaction
When the **demand for dollars increases** (euro holders buying U.S. goods), the **supply of euros increases** in the euro market. **One currency appreciating means the other depreciating**: the demand shift in one market is a supply shift in the other.

### 6.3.6 Graph Checklist
1. **Axes:** "Exchange rate (€ per $)" and "Quantity of dollars."
2. Label **D** (downward) and **S** (upward) curves of dollars.
3. Show shifts and label **new D/S**, the new exchange rate, and state **"appreciation"** or **"depreciation"** of the dollar.

## 6.4 Effects of Changes in Policies and Economic Conditions on the Foreign Exchange Market

### 6.4.1 Determinants (Shifters) of Currency Demand and Supply
| Change (in the home country, relative to abroad) | Effect on **demand for** home currency | Effect on **supply of** home currency | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Higher real interest rates at home** (relative) | ↑ (foreigners buy home bonds/deposits for higher returns) | ↓ (home residents invest less abroad) | **Appreciation** |
| **Lower real interest rates at home** | ↓ | ↑ | **Depreciation** |
| **Higher inflation at home (relative)** | ↓ (home goods become less competitive → fewer exports) | ↑ (cheaper foreign goods → more imports) | **Depreciation** |
| **Lower inflation at home (relative)** | ↑ | ↓ | **Appreciation** |
| **Higher foreign income** (foreign GDP ↑) | ↑ (foreigners buy more of our exports) | — | **Appreciation** |
| **Higher home income** | — | ↑ (more imports) | **Depreciation** |
| **Foreign tastes shift towards home goods** (preferences ↑) | ↑ | — | **Appreciation** |
| **Home tastes shift towards foreign goods** | — | ↑ | **Depreciation** |
| **Expectation that the currency will appreciate** | ↑ (speculators buy now) | ↓ | **Appreciation** (self-fulfilling) |
| **Expectation of depreciation** | ↓ | ↑ | **Depreciation** |
| **Home tariffs/quotas on imports** | — | ↓ (fewer imports → fewer units of home currency supplied) | **Appreciation** |
| **Foreign tariffs on our exports** | ↓ | — | **Depreciation** |
| **Greater foreign investment in home economy** | ↑ | — | **Appreciation** |

### 6.4.2 Macroeconomic Policy and the Exchange Rate
- **Expansionary monetary policy** (MS ↑) → **interest rates ↓** → capital outflows → **depreciation** (and higher inflation expectations).
- **Contractionary monetary policy** → **interest rates ↑** → capital inflows → **appreciation.**
- **Expansionary fiscal policy (deficit)** → government borrowing raises **real interest rates** → capital inflows → **appreciation** (and higher income raises imports, but the interest rate effect is the one tested).
- **Contractionary fiscal policy** → lower real interest rates → **depreciation.**
- **Trade policy:** a **tariff on imports** reduces imports → supply of home currency falls → **appreciation** (this partly offsets the tariff's effect on the trade balance).

### 6.4.3 Interaction Example
**The Fed raises interest rates** (U.S.):
- Foreign investors buy more U.S. bonds → **demand for dollars ↑**; Americans buy fewer foreign assets → **supply of dollars ↓** → **dollar appreciates.**
- U.S. exports ↓, imports ↑ → **NX ↓** → **AD ↓** (reinforcing the contractionary effect of higher interest rates).

## 6.5 Changes in the Foreign Exchange Market and Net Exports

### 6.5.1 Exchange Rates and Net Exports
| Currency movement | Exports | Imports | Net exports (NX) | AD |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Domestic currency appreciates** | **↓** (more expensive abroad) | **↑** (cheaper) | **↓** | **AD ↓** (shifts left) |
| **Domestic currency depreciates** | **↑** (cheaper abroad) | **↓** (dearer) | **↑** | **AD ↑** (shifts right) |

- Because **AD = C + I + G + NX**, a change in NX shifts **aggregate demand**: Y and PL change in the short run.
- **Marshall–Lerner condition:** a depreciation improves the trade balance only if the **demand for exports and imports is sufficiently elastic**. In the short run, the **J-curve** effect can initially worsen the trade balance because prices adjust before quantities.

### 6.5.2 Chain Effects (Typical FRQ Reasoning)
**Expansionary monetary policy:** MS ↑ → interest rates ↓ → capital outflow → supply of domestic currency ↑ → **depreciation** → **exports ↑, imports ↓** → **NX ↑** → **AD ↑ further** (reinforces the effect on AD).
**Expansionary fiscal policy:** deficit → real interest rate ↑ → capital inflow → **appreciation** → **NX ↓** → **partly offsets** the AD increase (a form of crowding out).

### 6.5.3 Winners and Losers from Exchange Rate Changes
- **Depreciation:** exporters and import-competing firms **gain**; consumers of imports and firms using imported inputs **lose**; may raise inflation (imported inflation).
- **Appreciation:** importers and consumers **gain** (cheaper imports, travel abroad); exporters and tourism **lose.**

### 6.5.4 Trade Policy and Net Exports
- **Tariffs/quotas** reduce imports (NX ↑) but cause **appreciation** (offsetting), possible **retaliation** from partners, and **higher prices** for consumers; they reduce overall efficiency (see Micro Unit 2).
- **Free trade** raises efficiency and growth through specialization (comparative advantage, Unit 1).

## 6.6 Real Interest Rates and International Capital Flows

### 6.6.1 Net Capital Outflow (NCO)
**Net capital outflow (NCO) = domestic residents' purchases of foreign assets − foreigners' purchases of domestic assets.**
- **NCO > 0:** net lender to the world (capital flows **out**). **NCO < 0:** net capital **inflow.**
- **NCO = NX** (the current account balance).

### 6.6.2 Real Interest Rates and Capital Flows
Capital moves to where the **real return is highest** (adjusting for risk):
- **Higher domestic real interest rate (relative to abroad)** → **foreign investors buy more domestic assets** and domestic investors keep more savings at home → **capital inflow increases; NCO falls** → **demand for domestic currency ↑ → appreciation.**
- **Lower domestic real interest rate** → capital flows out; NCO rises; currency depreciates.
- **The NCO curve slopes downward in the real interest rate** (higher r → lower NCO).

### 6.6.3 Open-Economy Loanable Funds Market
- **Supply:** national saving (S = private + public saving).
- **Demand:** **domestic investment (I) + net capital outflow (NCO).**
- **Equilibrium:** **S = I + NCO**, determining the **real interest rate**; it also determines the exchange rate and net exports through NCO.

**Example (government budget deficit):**
1. **Public saving falls → national saving (supply of loanable funds) shifts left.**
2. **Real interest rate rises.**
3. **Domestic investment falls (crowding out)** and **NCO falls** (more capital inflows).
4. **Currency appreciates → NX falls → trade deficit** ("twin deficits").

**Example (increase in national saving):** supply ↑ → r ↓ → investment ↑ and NCO ↑ → currency depreciates → NX ↑.

### 6.6.4 Consequences of Capital Flows
- **Benefits:** foreign capital finances domestic investment and growth (a country with high investment opportunities and low saving can borrow abroad); risk-sharing.
- **Costs:** foreign ownership of assets, vulnerability to **sudden capital flight** (financial crises, currency crashes), repayment obligations, exchange-rate volatility.
- **Policy implications:** high domestic real interest rates (from tight monetary policy or deficits) → appreciation and larger trade deficits.

### 6.6.5 Putting It All Together: An Integrated Example
**The central bank raises the policy interest rate** (domestic economy):
1. **Money market:** MS ↓ → nominal interest rate ↑ (real rate ↑ in the short run).
2. **Capital flows:** foreign demand for domestic bonds ↑ → **capital inflow ↑, NCO ↓.**
3. **Foreign exchange:** demand for domestic currency ↑, supply ↓ → **appreciation.**
4. **Net exports:** exports ↓, imports ↑ → **NX ↓.**
5. **AD-AS:** investment ↓ and NX ↓ → **AD shifts left** → **real GDP ↓, price level ↓ (inflation slows), unemployment ↑.**

## Unit 6 Quick Reference

**Key identities and formulas**
- **Current account + Capital/financial account = 0.**
- **Current account = NX + net primary income + net secondary income.**
- **NCO = NX;** **S = I + NCO.**
- **Exchange rate (price of A in terms of B) = 1 ÷ (price of B in terms of A).**
- **Currency appreciation → exports ↓, imports ↑, NX ↓, AD ↓.**
- **Currency depreciation → exports ↑, imports ↓, NX ↑, AD ↑.**
- **Higher real interest rate → capital inflow → currency demand ↑ → appreciation.**

**Key terms glossary:** balance of payments, current account, capital account, financial account, net exports, trade deficit/surplus, primary and secondary income, FDI, portfolio investment, net capital outflow, exchange rate, appreciation, depreciation, flexible/fixed/managed exchange rate, devaluation, purchasing power parity, foreign exchange market, J-curve, twin deficits, capital flight, tariff, quota.

**Top exam traps**
1. **BOP always balances**: a current account deficit = financial account surplus.
2. **Capital inflow = financial account surplus** (foreigners buying domestic assets).
3. **When the home currency appreciates, the other currency depreciates** (two sides of the same market).
4. **Appreciation hurts exports** and **depreciation helps exports.**
5. **Higher domestic interest rates → appreciation** (higher demand for the currency).
6. **Higher domestic inflation → depreciation.**
7. **Tariffs reduce imports → appreciation** (partially offsetting NX).
8. **Expansionary monetary policy → depreciation → NX ↑**; **expansionary fiscal policy → appreciation → NX ↓.**
9. **A trade deficit is not automatically "bad"**; it is financed by capital inflows.
10. Always **label the axes with currency units** (e.g., "€ per $"), and say which currency appreciates.

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** Which of the following is recorded in the **financial** account of the U.S. balance of payments as a capital inflow?
(A) A U.S. company exports aircraft (B) A Japanese investor purchases U.S. Treasury bonds (C) A U.S. tourist visits Japan (D) A U.S. citizen sends money to relatives in Japan
**Answer: B.**

**2.** A country has a current account deficit of ₹100 billion. Which must be true?
(A) The financial/capital account has a surplus of ₹100 billion (B) The country has a budget surplus (C) The currency must be depreciating (D) Exports exceed imports
**Answer: A.**

**3.** If the Indian rupee depreciates against the U.S. dollar, then:
(A) More rupees are needed to buy one dollar (B) Indian exports become more expensive for Americans (C) Fewer rupees are needed to buy one dollar (D) Indian imports become cheaper
**Answer: A.**

**4.** A rise in real interest rates in the United States relative to other countries will most likely cause the dollar to:
(A) Depreciate because of capital outflow (B) Appreciate because of capital inflow (C) Remain unchanged (D) Depreciate because of lower exports
**Answer: B.**

**5.** An increase in the U.S. price level relative to the price level in Europe will most likely:
(A) Appreciate the dollar (B) Depreciate the dollar (C) Raise net exports (D) Have no effect
**Answer: B.**

**6.** A depreciation of a country's currency will most likely lead to:
(A) A fall in exports and rise in imports (B) A rise in exports and fall in imports; AD shifts right (C) A fall in AD (D) No change in net exports
**Answer: B.**

**7.** The central bank undertakes expansionary monetary policy. In the foreign exchange market the domestic currency will:
(A) Appreciate, reducing NX (B) Depreciate, increasing NX (C) Appreciate, increasing NX (D) Not change
**Answer: B.**

**8.** An increase in income in Europe will most likely cause, in the market for U.S. dollars:
(A) Demand for dollars to rise and the dollar to appreciate (B) Supply of dollars to rise (C) Demand for dollars to fall (D) No change
**Answer: A.** Europeans buy more U.S. exports, needing more dollars.

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** Assume the economy of Country A is initially at full employment with a floating exchange rate. The central bank of Country A lowers its policy interest rate.
(a) Draw a correctly labeled graph of the foreign exchange market for Country A's currency (the "crown") showing the effect of the rate cut on the exchange rate.
(b) Explain the effect on the crown's exchange rate.
(c) Explain the effect on Country A's net exports.
(d) Explain the effect on aggregate demand, real GDP and the price level in the short run.

**Answer:**
(a) Axes: "Exchange rate ($ per crown)" and "Quantity of crowns." Lower interest rates reduce foreign demand for crown assets → **demand for crowns shifts left (D₂)**; domestic residents buy more foreign assets → **supply of crowns shifts right (S₂)**; new equilibrium at a **lower exchange rate.**
(b) The crown **depreciates.**
(c) Country A's goods are **cheaper abroad**, foreign goods are **dearer**: **exports ↑, imports ↓ → net exports ↑.**
(d) Lower interest rates raise investment and NX → **AD shifts right** → **real GDP ↑, price level ↑** (inflationary gap if above Yf), unemployment ↓.

**Q2.** A country's data (in billions): exports of goods and services = 400; imports = 450; net primary income = +10; net secondary income = −5.
(a) Calculate the current account balance.
(b) What must be the balance in the capital/financial account? Explain.
(c) The country also runs a large government budget deficit. Explain, using the loanable funds market, how this could be related to the trade deficit and what happens to the exchange rate.

**Answer:**
(a) NX = 400 − 450 = −50; current account = −50 + 10 − 5 = **−45 (a deficit of 45).**
(b) The BOP must balance → **financial/capital account = +45** (net capital inflow: foreigners buy 45 more of the country's assets than it buys of theirs).
(c) A budget deficit lowers national saving → **supply of loanable funds shifts left** → **real interest rate rises** → capital inflows increase (NCO falls) → **demand for the domestic currency rises → appreciation** → exports ↓, imports ↑ → **NX falls** (trade deficit widens): the "twin deficits."
