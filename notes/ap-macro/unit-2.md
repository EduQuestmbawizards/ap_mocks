## 2.1 Circular Flow and Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

### 2.1.1 The Circular Flow Model
A model showing how **goods, services, resources and money** flow among sectors of the economy.

**Basic two-sector model (households and firms):**
- **Product (goods and services) market:** households **buy** goods and services from firms (money flows to firms as **consumption spending/revenue**; goods flow to households).
- **Factor (resource) market:** households **sell** factors of production (labor, land, capital, entrepreneurship) to firms (money flows to households as **wages, rent, interest, profit = income**).
- **One person's spending is another person's income**, so **total spending = total output = total income** in the economy.

**Extended model adds:**
- **Government:** collects **taxes (T)** from households and firms; spends on goods and services (**G**) and **transfer payments** (pensions, unemployment benefits).
- **Financial sector (banks and financial markets):** households **save (S)**; funds are lent to firms for **investment (I)**.
- **Foreign sector (rest of the world):** **exports (X)** bring money in; **imports (M)** send money out.

**Leakages and injections**
| Leakages (withdrawals from the flow) | Injections (additions to the flow) |
|---|---|
| **Saving (S)** | **Investment (I)** |
| **Taxes (T)** | **Government spending (G)** |
| **Imports (M)** | **Exports (X)** |

When **injections = leakages (I + G + X = S + T + M)**, the flow is stable; if injections exceed leakages, the economy expands, and vice versa.

### 2.1.2 What is GDP?
**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** is the **market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders during a given period (usually one year or one quarter).**

Key words explained:
- **Market value:** goods are added using their **prices**, so different goods can be summed in monetary terms.
- **Final goods:** goods sold to the **end user**. **Intermediate goods** (inputs used to make other goods, e.g., flour sold to a bakery) are **not** counted separately, to avoid **double counting**; their value is already included in the final price.
- **Produced:** counts **current production only**.
- **Within a country's borders:** location matters, **not the nationality of the producer.** (A Japanese-owned car plant in India adds to **India's** GDP.)
- **Given period:** GDP is a **flow**.

### 2.1.3 What is NOT Included in GDP
1. **Intermediate goods** (double counting).
2. **Used (second-hand) goods** (counted when first produced; but the **dealer's commission/service** is included).
3. **Financial transactions:** buying stocks and bonds, bank deposits (only transfers of ownership).
4. **Transfer payments:** pensions, unemployment benefits, scholarships, welfare (no good or service is produced in exchange).
5. **Non-market activities:** household work, volunteer work, DIY repairs.
6. **Underground (illegal/unreported) economy.**
7. **Foreign production** by residents abroad (this belongs to GNP, not GDP).

### 2.1.4 GDP vs. GNP
- **GDP:** production **within** the country's borders (regardless of owner nationality).
- **GNP (GNI):** production **by the country's residents anywhere** in the world. **GNP = GDP + net income from abroad.**
- **Net Domestic Product (NDP) = GDP − depreciation** (consumption of fixed capital).

### 2.1.5 Measuring GDP: Three Approaches
**1. Expenditure approach (most important for AP):**
**GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)** [also written GDP = C + I + G + NX]

| Component | What it includes | What it excludes |
|---|---|---|
| **C: Consumption** (≈ 2/3 of GDP in many economies) | Household spending on **durable goods** (cars, appliances), **non-durable goods** (food, clothing) and **services** (haircut, tuition, healthcare) | Purchase of a new house (this is investment) |
| **I: Gross private domestic investment** | **Business spending on capital** (machinery, equipment, structures), **new residential construction (houses)**, and **changes in business inventories** | **Financial investment** (buying stocks/bonds) is **not** investment in the GDP sense |
| **G: Government purchases** | Spending by federal/state/local governments on **goods and services** (roads, defense, teachers' salaries) | **Transfer payments** (not G) |
| **NX: Net exports (X − M)** | **Exports** (foreign spending on domestic goods) minus **imports** (domestic spending on foreign goods) | — |

- **Imports are subtracted** because C, I and G include spending on imported goods that were not produced domestically.
- **Net investment = gross investment − depreciation.**

**2. Income approach:** GDP = total income earned by factors: **wages + rent + interest + profit** (= national income) **+ depreciation + indirect business taxes (and statistical adjustments).**

**3. Value-added approach:** sum of the **value added** at each stage of production (value of output − value of intermediate inputs). Total value added = value of final goods.

### 2.1.6 Numerical Examples
**(a) Expenditure approach:** C = 700, I = 200, G = 300, X = 100, M = 150.
**GDP = 700 + 200 + 300 + (100 − 150) = ₹1,150.**

**(b) Value added (bread):**
| Stage | Sale price | Intermediate inputs | Value added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farmer (wheat) | ₹30 | 0 | 30 |
| Miller (flour) | ₹50 | 30 | 20 |
| Baker (bread, final) | ₹90 | 50 | 40 |
| **Total** | | | **90 = final price** |
Only the **₹90** final sale counts in GDP (not 30 + 50 + 90 = 170).

### 2.1.7 Per Capita GDP
**GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population.** Used for comparing average living standards.

## 2.2 Limitations of GDP

GDP is the best-known measure of economic output but is **not a perfect measure of well-being**:
1. **Excludes non-market activities** (household production, volunteer work, childcare by family).
2. **Excludes the underground economy** (illegal activities and unreported cash transactions).
3. **Ignores leisure time:** a country that works longer hours has higher GDP but not necessarily higher well-being.
4. **Ignores environmental degradation and resource depletion:** pollution and deforestation may even raise GDP (cleanup spending).
5. **Ignores the distribution of income:** two countries with the same GDP per capita may differ widely in inequality.
6. **Ignores quality improvements and new products** (a ₹20,000 smartphone today does much more than a ₹20,000 phone 10 years ago).
7. **Ignores the composition of output:** GDP counts weapons, medicine and junk equally.
8. **Doesn't reflect quality of life:** health, safety, happiness, freedom, education.
9. **Cross-country comparisons** are difficult because of **exchange rates** (economists use **purchasing power parity (PPP)** adjustments).
10. **Negative externalities** (e.g., traffic jams) are not subtracted.

> **Remember:** a higher GDP generally correlates with higher living standards (life expectancy, literacy), but GDP is **not the same as welfare.**

## 2.3 Unemployment

### 2.3.1 Labor Force Definitions
- **Working-age (adult civilian non-institutional) population:** people aged 16 and over, **not** in the military or in institutions (prisons, hospitals).
- **Employed:** has a job (including part-time) during the survey week.
- **Unemployed:** **not employed, but actively looking for work** (in the last four weeks) **and available to work.**
- **Labor force = employed + unemployed.**
- **Not in the labor force:** full-time students, retirees, stay-at-home parents, **discouraged workers** (stopped looking because they believe no jobs are available), and others not seeking work.

### 2.3.2 Key Formulas
**Unemployment rate = (Number of unemployed ÷ Labor force) × 100**
**Labor force participation rate (LFPR) = (Labor force ÷ Working-age population) × 100**

**Numerical example (millions):**
Working-age population = 200; employed = 140; unemployed = 10; not in labor force = 50.
- Labor force = 140 + 10 = **150**.
- **Unemployment rate = 10 ÷ 150 = 6.67%.**
- **LFPR = 150 ÷ 200 = 75%.**

**If 4 million unemployed become discouraged and stop looking:** labor force = 146; unemployed = 6; **unemployment rate = 6 ÷ 146 = 4.1%** (falls although no one found a job), **LFPR = 146 ÷ 200 = 73%** (falls).

### 2.3.3 Types of Unemployment
| Type | Cause | Example | Policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frictional** | **Normal job search**: people moving between jobs, new entrants or re-entrants; lack of information | A graduate looking for a first job; a worker who quit to find a better job | Better job information, job boards, shorter search times |
| **Structural** | **Mismatch between workers' skills/location and available jobs**; technology, changes in industry composition, trade | Coal miners when the coal industry declines; factory workers replaced by robots | Retraining, education, relocation assistance |
| **Cyclical** | **Downturn in the business cycle** (deficient aggregate demand) | Layoffs during a recession | Expansionary fiscal/monetary policy |
| **Seasonal** *(sometimes treated as part of frictional)* | Seasonal changes | Ski instructors in summer | — |

### 2.3.4 Natural Rate of Unemployment (NRU) and Full Employment
- **Natural rate of unemployment = frictional + structural unemployment** (the unemployment rate when the economy is at **full employment**; **cyclical unemployment = 0**).
- **Full employment does NOT mean zero unemployment.**
- **Cyclical unemployment = actual unemployment rate − natural rate.**
- When actual unemployment > natural rate → **recession (negative output gap)**; when actual < natural rate → **economy overheating (positive output gap)**.
- The natural rate can change because of demographics, labor-market policies, technology and unemployment benefits.

### 2.3.5 Limitations and Costs
**Limitations of the unemployment rate:**
- **Excludes discouraged workers** → understates the true unemployment.
- **Counts part-time workers who want full-time jobs (underemployed) as employed.**
- **Doesn't capture the quality of jobs** or workers in jobs below their skill level.

**Costs of unemployment:**
- **Economic:** lost output (below potential GDP), lower income, lower tax revenue, higher transfer payments, human capital loss.
- **Social/personal:** stress, health problems, crime, family strain, lost skills.

## 2.4 Price Indices and Inflation

### 2.4.1 Definitions
- **Price level:** the average level of prices in an economy.
- **Inflation:** a **sustained increase in the general price level** over time (the purchasing power of money falls).
- **Deflation:** a sustained **decrease** in the general price level (negative inflation).
- **Disinflation:** a **fall in the rate of inflation** (prices still rising but more slowly).

### 2.4.2 The Consumer Price Index (CPI)
The **CPI** measures the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a **fixed market basket** of goods and services.

**Steps:**
1. Select the **market basket** (typical consumer purchases).
2. Find the **prices** each year.
3. Compute the **cost of the basket** in each year.
4. Choose a **base year** (CPI = 100).
5. **CPI = (Cost of the basket in the current year ÷ Cost of the basket in the base year) × 100.**

**Numerical example:** Basket = 10 pizzas, 5 books, 40 gallons of gas.

| | Pizza | Book | Gas | Cost of basket |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Base year prices** | ₹10 | ₹20 | ₹3 | 100 + 100 + 120 = **₹320** |
| **Current year prices** | ₹12 | ₹22 | ₹4 | 120 + 110 + 160 = **₹390** |

**CPI = 390 ÷ 320 × 100 = 121.9** → prices are **21.9% higher** than in the base year.

### 2.4.3 Calculating the Inflation Rate
**Inflation rate = [(CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year] × 100**

*Example:* CPI rises from 121.9 to 128.0 → inflation = (128.0 − 121.9) ÷ 121.9 = **5.0%**.

**Rule of 70:** the number of years for the price level (or any variable) to **double** ≈ **70 ÷ annual growth/inflation rate.** At 5% inflation, prices double in about 14 years.

**Converting nominal to real values:** **Real value = (Nominal value ÷ CPI) × 100.**
*Example:* Nominal wage = ₹20/hour, CPI = 125 → **real wage = 20 ÷ 125 × 100 = ₹16** (in base-year rupees).

### 2.4.4 The GDP Deflator
**GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100.** It measures the price level of **all domestically produced** final goods and services.

| | **CPI** | **GDP deflator** |
|---|---|---|
| Basket | **Fixed** basket of consumer goods | **Changing** — everything produced domestically (consumption, investment, government, exports) |
| Includes imports? | **Yes** (consumers buy imports) | **No** (only domestic production) |
| Used for | Cost-of-living adjustments, inflation target | Adjusting GDP for price changes |

### 2.4.5 Problems and Biases in the CPI (Overstatement of Inflation)
1. **Substitution bias:** consumers switch to cheaper goods when prices rise, but the fixed basket doesn't change.
2. **Quality changes:** a price rise may reflect better quality, not pure inflation.
3. **New products** (not in the basket initially).
4. **Outlet (shopping-location) bias:** people switch to discount stores.
Overall, the CPI **tends to overstate** inflation.

### 2.4.6 Types of Inflation (Preview of Unit 3)
- **Demand-pull inflation:** caused by an **increase in aggregate demand** (too much spending chasing too few goods).
- **Cost-push inflation:** caused by an **increase in production costs** (oil prices, wages) that shifts aggregate supply left.

## 2.5 Costs of Inflation

### 2.5.1 Anticipated vs. Unanticipated Inflation
- **Anticipated (expected) inflation:** people foresee it and adjust (wage contracts, interest rates).
- **Unanticipated inflation:** higher (or lower) than expected; it **redistributes wealth** arbitrarily and creates uncertainty.

### 2.5.2 Costs of Inflation
1. **Shoe-leather costs:** the time and effort spent to **reduce holdings of money** (extra trips to the bank) because cash loses value.
2. **Menu costs:** the cost to firms of **changing prices** (reprinting menus, catalogs, re-labeling).
3. **Distortion of the tax system:** taxes are based on **nominal** income and gains, so inflation can push people into **higher tax brackets** (**bracket creep**) and taxes **nominal capital gains**.
4. **Redistribution of wealth (unanticipated inflation):**
   - **Borrowers gain** and **lenders lose** (loans are repaid with money that is worth less).
   - People on **fixed incomes** (pensioners) and **savers** lose.
   - **Workers with long-term fixed-wage contracts** lose; employers gain.
   - Holders of **real assets** (property, stocks) often gain.
5. **Uncertainty and reduced investment:** harder to plan and make long-term contracts.
6. **Reduced international competitiveness:** if domestic inflation exceeds foreign inflation, exports become more expensive (Unit 6).
7. **Hyperinflation** (typically defined as >50% per month): destroys the usefulness of money, causes economic collapse.

### 2.5.3 Nominal vs. Real Interest Rates (Fisher Effect)
- **Real interest rate ≈ Nominal interest rate − Expected inflation rate.**
- **Nominal interest rate ≈ Real interest rate + Expected inflation.**
- *Example:* nominal rate 7%, expected inflation 3% → expected real rate **4%**. If actual inflation turns out to be 5%, the **actual (realized) real rate = 2%** → **borrowers gain, lenders lose** (unanticipated inflation).

### 2.5.4 Costs of Deflation
- **Debt burden rises** (borrowers repay with more valuable money).
- **Consumers delay purchases** expecting lower prices → lower demand → lower output → job losses (**deflationary spiral**).
- Wages are sticky downward → unemployment.
- Central banks therefore target **low, positive inflation (≈2%)** rather than zero.

## 2.6 Real vs. Nominal GDP

### 2.6.1 Definitions
- **Nominal GDP:** GDP measured using **current-year prices**. It can rise because of **higher output, higher prices, or both.**
- **Real GDP:** GDP measured using **constant (base-year) prices**; it **removes the effect of price changes**, so changes in real GDP reflect only changes in **output**.

### 2.6.2 Formulas
- **Real GDP = Nominal GDP ÷ (GDP deflator ÷ 100)**
- **GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100**
- **Real GDP growth rate = [(Real GDP₂ − Real GDP₁) ÷ Real GDP₁] × 100**
- **Real GDP per capita** = Real GDP ÷ population (best measure of change in living standards).

### 2.6.3 Worked Example (One-Good Economy)
Base year = Year 1.

| | Price (₹) | Quantity | Nominal GDP (P × Q) | Real GDP (Base price × Q) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Year 1** | 5 | 200 | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| **Year 2** | 6 | 220 | 1,320 | 5 × 220 = **1,100** |

- **Nominal GDP growth** = (1,320 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = **32%** (mix of price and output growth).
- **Real GDP growth** = (1,100 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = **10%** (output only).
- **GDP deflator (Year 2)** = 1,320 ÷ 1,100 × 100 = **120** → price level rose **20%**.

### 2.6.4 Worked Example (Two Goods)
| | Pizza price | Pizzas | Movie price | Movies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **2023 (base)** | ₹10 | 100 | ₹8 | 50 |
| **2024** | ₹12 | 105 | ₹10 | 55 |

- **Nominal GDP 2023** = 10×100 + 8×50 = **1,400**; **2024** = 12×105 + 10×55 = **1,810**.
- **Real GDP 2024 (2023 prices)** = 10×105 + 8×55 = 1,050 + 440 = **1,490**.
- **Real growth** = (1,490 − 1,400) ÷ 1,400 = **6.4%**.
- **Deflator 2024** = 1,810 ÷ 1,490 × 100 = **121.5**.

### 2.6.5 Why Real GDP Matters
Policy and comparisons of growth over time should use **real GDP** (or real GDP per capita), because nominal GDP can increase merely because of inflation.

## 2.7 The Business Cycle

### 2.7.1 Definition
The **business cycle** is the **recurring fluctuation of real GDP** around its long-run growth trend (potential output). The cycle is irregular in length and size.

### 2.7.2 Phases of the Business Cycle
1. **Expansion (recovery):** real GDP rises; unemployment falls; incomes and consumer spending increase; inflation may rise.
2. **Peak:** the **highest point** of real GDP in the cycle; the expansion ends; unemployment is typically at its lowest; inflation pressures are highest.
3. **Contraction (recession):** real GDP **falls**; unemployment rises; incomes and spending fall; inflation tends to fall.
4. **Trough:** the **lowest point** of real GDP; the contraction ends and a new expansion begins.

> **Recession:** a significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy lasting more than a few months (the official definition by the NBER in the U.S.). The popular rule: **two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP.** A **depression** is a very severe, prolonged recession.

### 2.7.3 Long-Run Trend and Output Gaps
- **Potential GDP (full-employment output)** grows over time with resources and technology (the **long-run trend**; same as the PPC/LRAS).
- **Recessionary gap (negative output gap):** actual real GDP < potential real GDP; **unemployment > natural rate**.
- **Inflationary gap (positive output gap):** actual real GDP > potential real GDP; **unemployment < natural rate**; inflation pressure.

### 2.7.4 Behavior of Key Variables During the Cycle
| Variable | Expansion | Contraction |
|---|---|---|
| **Real GDP** | Rising | Falling |
| **Unemployment rate** | Falling | Rising (**cyclical unemployment**) |
| **Inflation** | Often rises | Often falls (disinflation/deflation) |
| **Consumer/business confidence** | High | Low |
| **Investment** | Rising (most volatile component) | Falling sharply |
| **Interest rates** | Tend to rise | Tend to fall |

### 2.7.5 Economic Indicators
| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading indicators** | **Change before** the economy changes (predict turning points) | Stock market indexes, new building permits, consumer expectations/confidence, new orders for capital goods, interest-rate spread |
| **Coincident indicators** | **Change at the same time** as the economy | Real GDP, employment, industrial production, personal income |
| **Lagging indicators** | **Change after** the economy changes | **Unemployment rate**, CPI/inflation, average duration of unemployment, interest rates, corporate profits |

### 2.7.6 Causes of Business Cycles (Preview)
- **Changes in aggregate demand** (consumer/business confidence, interest rates, government spending, exports).
- **Supply shocks** (oil price changes, natural disasters, pandemics).
- **Financial crises**, credit crunches and asset bubbles.
- **Expectations and "animal spirits"**, policy mistakes, technology shocks.
(Unit 3 uses the AD-AS model to explain these; Unit 5 covers stabilization policy.)

1. **Transfer payments, used goods, stocks and bonds, intermediate goods are NOT in GDP.**

4. **Discouraged workers are NOT in the labor force** (so the unemployment rate understates the problem).

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** Which of the following is counted in the current year's GDP?
(A) A used car sold by a dealer (B) Shares of a company purchased on the stock exchange (C) A new house completed this year (D) A government pension payment
**Answer: C.** (The dealer's commission on A would count, but not the used car's value.)

**2.** If C = 600, I = 150, G = 200, exports = 80 and imports = 100, GDP equals:
(A) 930 (B) 1,030 (C) 950 (D) 1,130
**Answer: A.** 600 + 150 + 200 + (80 − 100) = 930.

**3.** A person who has stopped looking for work because they believe no jobs are available is:
(A) Unemployed (B) Employed (C) Not in the labor force (D) Cyclically unemployed
**Answer: C.** (A discouraged worker.)

**4.** A worker loses a job because the economy is in a recession. This is:
(A) Frictional unemployment (B) Structural unemployment (C) Cyclical unemployment (D) Natural unemployment
**Answer: C.**

**5.** The CPI rises from 200 to 210. The inflation rate is:
(A) 2.5% (B) 5% (C) 10% (D) 21%
**Answer: B.** (210 − 200) ÷ 200 = 5%.

**6.** Which group benefits from unanticipated inflation?
(A) Lenders with fixed-rate loans (B) Borrowers with fixed-rate loans (C) Pensioners on a fixed income (D) Savers holding cash
**Answer: B.**

**7.** Which of the following is a leading economic indicator?
(A) Unemployment rate (B) Building permits (C) CPI (D) Real GDP
**Answer: B.**

**8.** Nominal GDP is ₹2,400 and the GDP deflator is 120. Real GDP is:
(A) ₹2,000 (B) ₹2,400 (C) ₹2,880 (D) ₹120
**Answer: A.** 2,400 ÷ 1.20 = 2,000.

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** An economy has a working-age population of 200 million, of which 150 million are employed and 10 million are unemployed.
(a) Calculate the labor force, the unemployment rate and the labor force participation rate.
(b) Suppose 5 million of the unemployed become discouraged and stop looking for work. Recalculate the unemployment rate and LFPR, and explain the result.
(c) Identify the type of unemployment experienced by a recent graduate searching for a first job and a factory worker replaced by automation.

**Answer:**
(a) Labor force = 150 + 10 = **160 million**. Unemployment rate = 10 ÷ 160 = **6.25%**. LFPR = 160 ÷ 200 = **80%**.
(b) Labor force = 155 million; unemployed = 5 million. Unemployment rate = 5 ÷ 155 = **3.2%**; LFPR = 155 ÷ 200 = **77.5%**. The unemployment rate **falls** even though no one found a job, because discouraged workers are excluded from the labor force; the measure understates labor-market weakness.
(c) Graduate: **frictional**; factory worker replaced by automation: **structural**.

**Q2.** A country produces only bread. Year 1 (base year): price = ₹5, output = 200. Year 2: price = ₹6, output = 220.
(a) Calculate nominal GDP for both years.
(b) Calculate real GDP for Year 2 and the real GDP growth rate.
(c) Calculate the GDP deflator for Year 2 and the inflation rate.
(d) Explain why nominal GDP growth overstates economic growth.

**Answer:**
(a) Year 1 = 5 × 200 = **₹1,000**; Year 2 = 6 × 220 = **₹1,320**.
(b) Real GDP Year 2 = 5 × 220 = **₹1,100**; growth = (1,100 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = **10%**.
(c) Deflator = 1,320 ÷ 1,100 × 100 = **120** → inflation (price-level rise) = **20%**.
(d) Nominal GDP grew 32%, but part of that came from the 20% price rise, not extra output; real GDP removes price changes and shows true output growth of 10%.
