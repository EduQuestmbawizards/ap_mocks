## 1.0 Introduction to Macroeconomics

### 1.0.1 Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics
- **Microeconomics** studies individual decision-makers (households, firms) and individual markets (the market for wheat, the labor market for engineers).
- **Macroeconomics** studies the **economy as a whole**: total output, the overall price level, total employment, interest rates, the money supply, government policy and international trade/finance.

| Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|
| Price of a single good | **General price level** (inflation) |
| Output of one firm/industry | **Total output (GDP)** |
| Employment at one firm | **Unemployment rate** for the economy |
| Individual markets | **Aggregate** markets (AD-AS, money market, loanable funds, foreign exchange) |

### 1.0.2 Major Macroeconomic Goals
1. **Economic growth** (rising real GDP and living standards).
2. **Full employment** (low unemployment).
3. **Price stability** (low, stable inflation).
4. **A sustainable balance of trade/payments and stable exchange rates.**
5. **Equitable distribution of income** and financial stability.

### 1.0.3 Core Macroeconomic Concepts Previewed
- **Gross Domestic Product (GDP):** the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year (Unit 2).
- **Unemployment rate and inflation rate** are the two main indicators of economic health.
- **Nominal vs. real values:** *nominal* values are measured in current prices; *real* values are adjusted for price changes (inflation).
- **Stock vs. flow:** a **stock** is measured at a point in time (money supply, national debt, wealth); a **flow** is measured over a period (GDP, income, a budget deficit).
- **Short run vs. long run (macro):** in the short run, some prices and wages are **sticky** (slow to adjust); in the long run, they are **flexible** and the economy returns to its potential output.

### 1.0.4 Positive vs. Normative Economics
- **Positive statements** describe what **is** and can be tested with data ("An increase in the money supply increases inflation").
- **Normative statements** involve **value judgments** about what **should be** ("The central bank should lower interest rates to reduce unemployment").

### 1.0.5 Ceteris Paribus and Models
Economists use **models** (simplified representations) and the assumption of **ceteris paribus** ("all other things being equal") to isolate the effect of a single change. A model's value lies in its **usefulness**, not in perfect realism.

## 1.1 Scarcity, Choice and Opportunity Cost

### 1.1.1 Scarcity
**Scarcity** is the condition of **limited resources and unlimited wants.** It is a permanent feature of every economy. It forces societies to make **choices**. (Do not confuse it with a **shortage**, which is a temporary market situation where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price.)

### 1.1.2 Factors of Production
- **Land (natural resources)** → rent.
- **Labor** → wages.
- **Capital (physical capital: machines, factories, tools; not money)** → interest.
- **Entrepreneurship** → profit.
- **Human capital:** skills, education and training of workers. Investment in human capital raises productivity and long-run growth.

### 1.1.3 The Three Basic Economic Questions
Every society must decide **what** to produce, **how** to produce, and **for whom** to produce.

### 1.1.4 Opportunity Cost
**Opportunity cost** is the value of the **next best alternative forgone** when a choice is made. Every choice involves a **trade-off**.
- **Explicit costs:** out-of-pocket payments. **Implicit costs:** the value of forgone alternatives from using one's own resources (e.g., forgone salary).
- **Sunk costs** (already paid, unrecoverable) should be **ignored** in decision-making.

### 1.1.5 Macroeconomic Examples of Opportunity Cost
- A government that spends more on **defense** has fewer resources for **education or health** ("guns vs. butter").
- A country that devotes more resources to **capital goods** (factories) now gets **fewer consumer goods** today but more output in the future.
- Using ₹1 lakh crore of government funds on a highway means those funds cannot be used for another project.

### 1.1.6 Marginal Analysis
Decisions are made **at the margin.** Compare **marginal benefit (MB)** and **marginal cost (MC)**. Do more of an activity as long as **MB > MC**; stop where **MB = MC**.

### 1.1.7 Incentives
**Incentives** (rewards and penalties) change behavior. Macro policy often works by changing incentives: a lower interest rate encourages borrowing and investment; higher income taxes may reduce the incentive to work.

## 1.2 Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)

### 1.2.1 Definition
The **PPC** (production possibilities frontier) shows the **maximum possible combinations of two goods or categories of goods** an economy can produce with its **limited resources and given technology**, when all resources are **fully and efficiently employed.**

In macroeconomics, the two "goods" are often **consumer goods vs. capital goods**, or **guns vs. butter**, or **education vs. defense**.

### 1.2.2 Points on and off the PPC
| Point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **On the PPC** | **Efficient**; full employment of resources; maximum output (the economy is at its **potential output**) |
| **Inside the PPC** | **Inefficient**; unemployment or underutilized resources (a **recession**); actual output < potential |
| **Outside the PPC** | **Unattainable** with current resources and technology |

**Macro connection:** the PPC represents **potential (full-employment) output**. Being **inside** the PPC corresponds to a **recession** with high unemployment (a **negative output gap**). Moving from inside the curve toward the curve = **economic recovery**.

### 1.2.3 Opportunity Cost and the Shape of the PPC
- **Bowed-out (concave)** PPC → **increasing opportunity cost** (resources are not perfectly adaptable between uses).
- **Straight-line** PPC → **constant opportunity cost** (resources are perfectly substitutable).
- **Opportunity cost** of one more unit of Good X = units of Good Y forgone (the slope).

**Example:**
| Point | Capital goods | Consumer goods |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 100 |
| B | 10 | 90 |
| C | 20 | 70 |
| D | 30 | 40 |
| E | 40 | 0 |

Opportunity cost of capital goods: A→B: 1 consumer good per capital good; B→C: 2; C→D: 3; D→E: 4 → **increasing opportunity cost.**

### 1.2.4 Economic Growth: Shifts of the PPC
**Outward shift (growth)** caused by:
1. **Increase in the quantity of resources** (labor force growth, immigration, discovery of resources, capital accumulation/investment).
2. **Increase in the quality of resources** (education, training, health = human capital).
3. **Technological advancement** (more output from the same inputs).

**Inward shift:** destruction or loss of resources (war, natural disasters, depletion, emigration of skilled workers).

**Types of shifts:**
- **Parallel shift** (both goods): general improvement (labor, technology applied to all).
- **Pivot** (one good): improvement specific to one good (e.g., a better crop variety increases only agricultural output).

**What does NOT shift the PPC:** a recession or unemployment (that moves the economy **inside** the PPC); a change in consumer demand; changes in prices; movement along the curve.

### 1.2.5 Trade-off Between Present and Future (Growth)
A country that produces **more capital goods now** (investing) experiences **greater economic growth** (outward PPC shift) in the future, but gives up **current consumption**. A country producing mostly consumer goods grows more slowly.
- This is why **investment** and **saving** are central to long-run growth (Unit 5).

### 1.2.6 Link to Long-Run Aggregate Supply
In Unit 3, **long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)** will represent **potential output** — the same idea as the PPC. **Economic growth shifts LRAS right**, exactly like an outward PPC shift.

## 1.3 Comparative Advantage and Trade

### 1.3.1 Absolute vs. Comparative Advantage
- **Absolute advantage:** the ability to produce **more of a good with the same resources** (or the same output with **fewer resources**) than another producer.
- **Comparative advantage:** the ability to produce a good at a **lower opportunity cost** than another producer.
- **Trade is based on comparative advantage**, not absolute advantage. Even a country with an absolute advantage in everything gains from trade.

### 1.3.2 Calculating Opportunity Cost
- **Output approach** (units produced per hour/resources): Opp. cost of Good A = (units of B forgone) ÷ (units of A gained).
- **Input approach** (hours needed per unit): Opp. cost of Good A = (hours per unit of A) ÷ (hours per unit of B).
**Lower opportunity cost = comparative advantage.**

### 1.3.3 Worked Example (Input Approach)
Hours needed to produce **one unit**:

| | Shirts | Phones |
|---|---|---|
| **Country 1** | 2 hours | 3 hours |
| **Country 2** | 6 hours | 4 hours |

**Absolute advantage:** Country 1 produces **both** with fewer hours (2 < 6; 3 < 4).

**Opportunity costs:**
| | 1 Shirt costs | 1 Phone costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Country 1** | 2/3 phone | 3/2 = **1.5 shirts** |
| **Country 2** | 6/4 = **1.5 phones** | 4/6 = **2/3 shirt** |

- Country 1 has the **lower opportunity cost of shirts (2/3 < 1.5)** → **comparative advantage in shirts**.
- Country 2 has the **lower opportunity cost of phones (2/3 < 1.5)** → **comparative advantage in phones**.

### 1.3.4 Terms of Trade
The price of one good in terms of the other must lie **between the two countries' opportunity costs** for both to gain.
- For **shirts**: between **2/3 and 1.5 phones per shirt**.
- For **phones**: between **2/3 and 1.5 shirts per phone**.
- If the terms equal one party's opportunity cost, that party gains nothing; outside the range, a party loses.

### 1.3.5 Gains from Trade (Numerical)
Suppose each country has 120 hours.
- Country 1 alone: 60 shirts **or** 40 phones. Country 2 alone: 20 shirts **or** 30 phones.
- **Specialize:** Country 1 makes **60 shirts**; Country 2 makes **30 phones**.
- **Trade 24 shirts for 24 phones** (1:1, inside the range).
  - Country 1 ends with **36 shirts + 24 phones**: producing these on its own would take 36×2 + 24×3 = **144 hours > 120** → **beyond its PPC** ✔.
  - Country 2 ends with **24 shirts + 6 phones**: 24×6 + 6×4 = **168 hours > 120** → **beyond its PPC** ✔.
- Both countries **consume outside their PPC** thanks to trade. **Trade does not shift the PPC**; it lets countries **consume beyond** their production possibilities.

### 1.3.6 Macro Relevance of Trade
- Trade increases total output, lowers prices for consumers and raises living standards.
- Trade barriers (tariffs, quotas) reduce efficiency; trade changes the **balance of payments** (Unit 6).
- Costs: some workers/industries lose from import competition (structural unemployment), adjustment costs.

## 1.4 Economic Systems and the Role of Government

### 1.4.1 What is an Economic System?
The set of institutions, rules and methods a society uses to answer **what, how and for whom** to produce. Systems differ in **who owns resources** and **who makes decisions.**

### 1.4.2 Types of Economic Systems
| System | Who decides? | Ownership | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** | Custom, tradition | Often communal | Stability, social cohesion | Slow growth, low innovation |
| **Command (centrally planned)** | Government planners | Mostly state | Can mobilize resources; can reduce inequality | Inefficiency, shortages/surpluses, weak incentives, little innovation |
| **Market (capitalist)** | Households and firms through prices | Private | Efficiency, innovation, consumer choice | Inequality, market failures, instability |
| **Mixed** | Both market and government | Both | Combine market efficiency with government correction | Government failure risks, trade-offs |

Almost all modern economies are **mixed economies**.

### 1.4.3 Features of Market Systems
- **Private property rights**, **freedom of choice**, **self-interest**, **competition**, **prices as signals**, **limited government**.
- **Adam Smith's "invisible hand":** self-interested individuals, guided by prices, promote society's overall good.
- **Circular flow:** households supply factors of production to firms in **factor markets** and buy goods and services in **product markets**; money flows in the opposite direction. (Extended with government and foreign sector in Unit 2.)

### 1.4.4 The Role of Government in a Market Economy
Governments intervene to:
1. **Provide public goods** (defense, roads, law enforcement), which markets under-supply because of free-riders.
2. **Correct externalities** (taxes on pollution, subsidies for education).
3. **Promote competition / regulate monopolies** (antitrust laws).
4. **Define and enforce property rights and contracts**; provide a legal system and money.
5. **Redistribute income** (progressive taxes, welfare, transfer payments).
6. **Stabilize the economy** (**fiscal policy**: taxes and spending; **monetary policy**: central bank interest rates and money supply) to reduce unemployment and inflation and encourage growth. *(This is the main macro role; see Units 3–5.)*

### 1.4.5 Government Failure
Government can also fail: **inefficient bureaucracy**, **rent-seeking and lobbying**, **poorly designed policies**, **time lags** in policy, **political motives**. Intervention should be judged by whether benefits outweigh costs.

### 1.4.6 Efficiency vs. Equity
- **Efficiency:** getting the most from limited resources (productive and allocative).
- **Equity:** fair distribution of income and opportunities.
- There is often a **trade-off** between them: policies that reduce inequality (high taxes, transfers) can reduce incentives; policies that maximize efficiency may widen inequality.

## 1.5 Supply, Demand and Market Equilibrium

### 1.5.1 Demand
- **Law of demand:** as price falls, **quantity demanded rises** (ceteris paribus) → **downward-sloping demand curve** (because of the substitution effect, income effect and diminishing marginal utility).
- **Change in quantity demanded:** movement along the curve, caused only by the **good's own price.**
- **Change in demand:** a shift of the curve, caused by non-price determinants:
  - **I**ncome (normal goods ↑, inferior goods ↓), **T**astes/preferences, **E**xpectations (future prices/income), **N**umber of buyers, **S**ubstitute and complement **P**rices.

### 1.5.2 Supply
- **Law of supply:** as price rises, **quantity supplied rises** → **upward-sloping supply curve** (higher price covers rising marginal cost and attracts more producers).
- **Change in quantity supplied:** movement along the curve (own price).
- **Change in supply:** shift of the curve from **non-price determinants**: **R**esource (input) prices, **O**ther goods' prices, **T**echnology, **T**axes and subsidies, **E**xpectations, **N**umber of sellers.

### 1.5.3 Market Equilibrium
**Equilibrium** occurs where **quantity demanded = quantity supplied**.
- **Equilibrium price (market-clearing price)** and **equilibrium quantity**.
- **Surplus (excess supply):** price above equilibrium → quantity supplied > quantity demanded → price falls.
- **Shortage (excess demand):** price below equilibrium → quantity demanded > quantity supplied → price rises.

**Numerical example:** Qd = 120 − 4P; Qs = 20 + 6P.
- 120 − 4P = 20 + 6P → 100 = 10P → **P\* = ₹10, Q\* = 80.**
- At **P = ₹14:** Qd = 64, Qs = 104 → **surplus of 40** → price falls.
- At **P = ₹6:** Qd = 96, Qs = 56 → **shortage of 40** → price rises.

### 1.5.4 Changes in Equilibrium
| Change | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| **Demand ↑** | ↑ | ↑ |
| **Demand ↓** | ↓ | ↓ |
| **Supply ↑** | ↓ | ↑ |
| **Supply ↓** | ↑ | ↓ |

**Both curves shift:** one effect is determinate and the other is **indeterminate**:
- D↑ and S↑ → Q↑ (price indeterminate).
- D↑ and S↓ → P↑ (quantity indeterminate).
- D↓ and S↑ → P↓ (quantity indeterminate).
- D↓ and S↓ → Q↓ (price indeterminate).

### 1.5.5 Price Controls (Brief)
- **Price ceiling:** legal maximum price. **Binding only if below equilibrium** → shortage, black markets, quality decline (e.g., rent control).
- **Price floor:** legal minimum price. **Binding only if above equilibrium** → surplus (e.g., minimum wage causing unemployment in a competitive labor market).

### 1.5.6 Why S&D Matters in Macroeconomics
The same supply-and-demand logic is later applied to **macro markets**:
- **Money market** (demand for and supply of money → nominal interest rate) — Unit 4.
- **Loanable funds market** (savers supply funds, borrowers demand them → real interest rate) — Unit 4.
- **Foreign exchange market** (demand for and supply of a currency → exchange rate) — Unit 6.
- **Aggregate demand and aggregate supply** (price level and real GDP) — Unit 3 (these are **not** simple market demand/supply curves, but equilibrium logic is the same).

### 1.5.7 Graphing Tips
- Label axes: **Price (P)** and **Quantity (Q)**; label curves **D, S**, and equilibrium **P\*, Q\***.
- When a curve shifts, draw the new curve and label it **D₂ or S₂**; show the new equilibrium **P₂, Q₂**.
- Never shift a curve for a change in the good's **own price**.

## Unit 1 Quick Reference

**Key formulas and rules**
- Opportunity cost = (units of the other good forgone) ÷ (units gained).
- Comparative advantage = **lower opportunity cost.**
- Terms of trade lie **between** the two opportunity costs.
- Equilibrium: **Qd = Qs.** Surplus: P above P\*. Shortage: P below P\*.
- MB = MC for optimal decisions.

**Key terms glossary:** macroeconomics, GDP, nominal, real, stock, flow, scarcity, opportunity cost, trade-off, factors of production, human capital, PPC, economic growth, potential output, absolute advantage, comparative advantage, terms of trade, traditional/command/market/mixed economy, invisible hand, public good, externality, government failure, equity, efficiency, demand, supply, equilibrium, surplus, shortage, price ceiling, price floor.

**Top exam traps**
1. **Scarcity ≠ shortage.**
2. **Capital ≠ money.**
3. **Recession = a point INSIDE the PPC** (not a shift of the PPC).
4. **Trade doesn't shift the PPC;** it allows consumption beyond it.
5. **Comparative advantage** decides trade, not absolute advantage.
6. **A price change moves along the curve; a non-price determinant shifts it.**
7. When both curves shift, one of P or Q is **indeterminate**.

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** Which of the following is a macroeconomic topic?
(A) The pricing of a pizza at one restaurant (B) The national unemployment rate (C) A firm's decision to hire a worker (D) The market for sneakers
**Answer: B.**

**2.** A recession is best represented on a PPC as:
(A) An outward shift (B) An inward shift (C) A point inside the curve (D) A point outside the curve
**Answer: C.**

**3.** Which would shift a country's PPC outward?
(A) A decrease in unemployment (B) A technological advance (C) A rise in consumer confidence (D) A fall in interest rates
**Answer: B.** (A is a movement toward the curve.)

**4.** Country X can produce 20 cars or 40 tractors; Country Y can produce 10 cars or 30 tractors. Which statement is correct?
(A) X has comparative advantage in tractors (B) Y has comparative advantage in tractors (C) X has comparative advantage in cars (D) Neither has comparative advantage
**Answer: B.** Opportunity cost of a tractor: X = 20/40 = 0.5 car; Y = 10/30 = 0.33 car → Y is lower, so Y has the comparative advantage in tractors (and X in cars).

**5.** Which of the following best describes a mixed economy?
(A) Government makes all decisions (B) Markets make all decisions (C) Market decisions combined with government intervention (D) Decisions made by tradition
**Answer: C.**

**6.** The price of coffee (a substitute for tea) rises. In the tea market, the equilibrium price and quantity will:
(A) Both fall (B) Both rise (C) Price rises, quantity falls (D) Price falls, quantity rises
**Answer: B.** Demand for tea increases.

**7.** At a price above equilibrium, there is:
(A) A shortage (B) A surplus (C) Equilibrium (D) A decrease in demand
**Answer: B.**

**8.** Which of the following is a normative statement?
(A) Unemployment fell to 4% last year (B) The government should raise taxes to reduce the deficit (C) Higher interest rates reduce investment (D) Inflation was 3% last year
**Answer: B.**

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** Country A can produce 100 units of consumer goods or 50 units of capital goods when fully employing its resources (assume a straight-line PPC).
(a) What is the opportunity cost of one unit of capital goods?
(b) Draw the PPC and label a point showing a recession.
(c) Explain the effect of a major improvement in technology that raises productivity in both sectors.
(d) Explain how a country that chooses more capital goods today will differ from one that chooses more consumer goods in the future.

**Answer:**
(a) 100/50 = **2 units of consumer goods** per unit of capital goods.
(b) PPC from (0, 100) to (50, 0); a recession is a point **inside** the PPC (e.g., 30 consumer, 15 capital).
(c) The PPC shifts **outward** (parallel), meaning greater potential output of both goods.
(d) The country with more capital goods invests more → **greater economic growth (bigger outward shift of the PPC)** in the future, but sacrifices current consumption.

**Q2.** The market for gasoline is in equilibrium. A war disrupts oil supplies, and at the same time consumer incomes rise (gasoline is a normal good).
(a) Show and explain the effect on the supply and demand curves.
(b) What happens to the equilibrium price and quantity?
(c) Explain why a price ceiling set below the new equilibrium price would cause a shortage.

**Answer:**
(a) Oil disruption → **supply decreases** (shifts left); higher income → **demand increases** (shifts right).
(b) **Price rises** for certain; **quantity is indeterminate** (depends on the relative size of the shifts).
(c) Below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied → **shortage**.
