## 4.1 Financial Assets

### 4.1.1 What is a Financial Asset?
A **financial asset** is a **claim** on the income or assets of another party (a promise to receive future payments). The person who holds it is a **saver/lender/investor**; the issuer is a **borrower**.

### 4.1.2 Main Types
| Asset | What it is | Return | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stock (equity/share)** | Ownership share in a corporation | Dividends + capital gains | **High** (price varies; no guaranteed payment) |
| **Bond** | **Loan** to a government or corporation; issuer promises **interest (coupon) + principal (face value) at maturity** | Interest + possible capital gain/loss | **Lower** than stocks (depends on issuer's creditworthiness) |
| **Loan (e.g., bank loan, mortgage)** | Direct borrowing from a lender | Interest | Default risk |
| **Bank deposit** | Funds placed in a bank | Interest (low) | **Very low** (deposit insurance) |

### 4.1.3 Characteristics of Financial Assets
1. **Return:** the gain from holding the asset (interest, dividends, capital gains).
2. **Risk:** uncertainty about returns or the possibility of loss. **Higher expected return generally requires accepting higher risk** (risk-return trade-off).
3. **Liquidity:** how easily and quickly an asset can be converted to cash **without losing value** (cash is the most liquid; real estate is illiquid).
4. **Time horizon (maturity).**
Rational savers trade off these characteristics; **diversification** (holding different assets) **reduces risk.**

### 4.1.4 Financial Intermediaries and Markets
- **Financial intermediaries** (banks, mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds) **channel savings to borrowers**, reduce **transaction costs**, **spread risk**, and **overcome information problems.**
- **Financial markets** (stock market, bond market, loanable funds market) bring savers and borrowers together directly.
- A well-functioning financial system turns **saving into investment**, which drives **economic growth.**

### 4.1.5 Time Value of Money and Present Value
A rupee today is worth **more** than a rupee in the future because it can be invested to earn interest.
- **Future value:** FV = PV × (1 + r)ᵗ
- **Present value:** **PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ** (r = interest rate, t = years)

**Example:** ₹1,000 to be received in 1 year, r = 5% → PV = 1,000 ÷ 1.05 = **₹952.38**. If r = 10% → PV = **₹909.09.**

### 4.1.6 Bond Prices and Interest Rates: an INVERSE Relationship
A bond's price is the **present value** of its future payments. When **market interest rates rise**, the present value of those fixed payments **falls**, so **bond prices fall**; when interest rates fall, bond prices rise.

- **Example (zero-coupon bond paying ₹1,000 in 1 year):** r = 5% → price = ₹952.38; r = 10% → price = ₹909.09 → **price falls as r rises.**
- **Example (perpetual bond paying ₹50/year):** r = 5% → price = 50 ÷ 0.05 = **₹1,000**; r = 10% → 50 ÷ 0.10 = **₹500.**
- Intuition: if new bonds pay higher interest, old bonds with lower fixed payments become less attractive, so their price must fall to offer a competitive yield.

> **Exam link:** when the central bank buys bonds, **bond prices rise and interest rates fall.**

## 4.2 Nominal vs. Real Interest Rates

### 4.2.1 Definitions
- **Nominal interest rate (i):** the interest rate **as stated**, not adjusted for inflation.
- **Real interest rate (r):** the nominal rate **adjusted for inflation**; measures the true increase in **purchasing power.**

### 4.2.2 Formulas (Fisher Equation)
**Real interest rate ≈ Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate**
**Nominal interest rate ≈ Real interest rate + Expected inflation rate**

*(Exact: 1 + r = (1 + i) ÷ (1 + π). The approximation is used on the AP exam.)*

**Examples:**
- Nominal = 8%, inflation = 3% → **real = 5%.**
- Real = 2%, expected inflation = 4% → nominal = **6%.**
- **Ex ante** (expected) real rate uses **expected** inflation; **ex post** (realized) real rate uses **actual** inflation. If inflation turns out higher than expected, the **ex post real rate is lower**: borrowers gain; lenders lose.

### 4.2.3 Why It Matters
- **Borrowers and lenders** make decisions based on the **real** rate.
- **Investment** depends on the **real interest rate**; the **money market** determines the **nominal** rate; the **loanable funds market** determines the **real** rate.
- A **negative real rate** occurs when inflation exceeds the nominal rate.

## 4.3 Definition, Measurement and Functions of Money

### 4.3.1 What is Money?
**Money** is any asset that is **generally accepted as payment** for goods and services and in settling debts. Money is **not** the same as wealth or income.

### 4.3.2 Functions of Money
1. **Medium of exchange:** used to buy and sell goods; solves the **double coincidence of wants** problem in barter.
2. **Unit of account:** a common measure for stating prices and values.
3. **Store of value:** holds purchasing power over time (inflation erodes it).
*(Also: standard of deferred payment.)*

### 4.3.3 Characteristics of Good Money
**Acceptability, durability, portability, divisibility, uniformity (fungibility), limited supply (scarcity).**

### 4.3.4 Types of Money
- **Commodity money:** has intrinsic value (gold, silver, cigarettes in prisoner-of-war camps).
- **Representative money:** paper backed by a commodity (gold certificates).
- **Fiat money:** has **no intrinsic value**; valuable because the **government declares it legal tender** and people trust it (today's currency). Value depends on the **confidence** in the issuer and the **limited supply**.

### 4.3.5 Measuring the Money Supply (Liquidity Ladder)
| Measure | Includes |
|---|---|
| **M1** | **Currency in circulation** (coins, notes held by the public) + **checkable (demand) deposits** + traveler's checks |
| **M2** | **M1 + savings deposits + small-denomination time deposits (CDs under $100,000) + retail money market mutual funds** |

- M1 is **most liquid**; M2 is **broader (less liquid).**
- **Not** part of the money supply: **credit cards** (a loan, not money), **stocks and bonds**, **bank reserves held by banks**, money held by the government/central bank, large time deposits, **gold/real estate.**
- *(In 2020 the U.S. Federal Reserve reclassified savings deposits into M1, but the AP exam's traditional definitions above are the ones to use.)*

### 4.3.6 Demand for Money
People hold money for:
1. **Transactions demand:** to buy goods and services; increases with **nominal GDP/price level**.
2. **Precautionary demand:** for emergencies.
3. **Asset (speculative) demand:** to hold as a store of value instead of bonds.

The **opportunity cost of holding money = the nominal interest rate** (interest forgone by not holding bonds). So:
- **Higher nominal interest rate → people hold less money → quantity of money demanded falls** (downward-sloping money demand curve).

## 4.4 Banking and the Expansion of the Money Supply

### 4.4.1 How Banks Operate
Banks accept **deposits** and make **loans**; they earn **profit** from the difference between the **interest on loans** and the **interest paid on deposits.** Banks keep only a **fraction of deposits as reserves** (**fractional-reserve banking**) and lend the rest.

### 4.4.2 Bank Balance Sheet (T-Account)
| **Assets** (what the bank owns/is owed) | **Liabilities** (what the bank owes) |
|---|---|
| **Reserves** (vault cash + deposits at the central bank) | **Deposits** (owed to depositors) |
| **Loans** | **Net worth (owner's equity)** |
| **Securities** (bonds) | |
**Assets = Liabilities + Net worth.**

### 4.4.3 Reserves
- **Required reserves = reserve requirement ratio (rr) × deposits.**
- **Excess reserves = total reserves − required reserves.**
- Banks can lend out **excess reserves** (and typically do, to earn interest).
- **Reserve requirement ratio (rr):** the **fraction of deposits** that must be held as reserves.
*(Note: the Federal Reserve set reserve requirement ratios to 0% in March 2020, but the reserve requirement remains a standard AP concept and monetary tool.)*

### 4.4.4 The Money Multiplier
**Money multiplier = 1 ÷ rr**
The **maximum** total change in the money supply from a change in reserves = **change in excess reserves (or new reserves) × money multiplier.**

**Assumptions for the maximum:** banks lend out all excess reserves; borrowers deposit all loan proceeds back into banks; people hold no extra cash (no currency drain).

### 4.4.5 Worked Example (rr = 10%)
Someone deposits **₹1,000 cash** in Bank A.

| Bank | New deposit | Required reserve (10%) | Excess reserve = new loan |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1,000 | 100 | **900** |
| B | 900 | 90 | **810** |
| C | 810 | 81 | **729** |
| … | … | … | … |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **1,000** | |

- Total deposits created = 1,000 ÷ 0.10 = **₹10,000**.
- **Careful (cash deposit):** the ₹1,000 of cash was **already in the money supply** (currency). It becomes deposits, so the **net increase in the money supply = 10,000 − 1,000 = ₹9,000**, which equals **excess reserves (900) × multiplier (10).**
- **If instead the central bank injects ₹1,000 of new reserves** (e.g., buys a bond from a bank), the money supply can rise by up to **₹1,000 × 10 = ₹10,000.**

| Situation | Maximum change in money supply |
|---|---|
| **Central bank adds ₹X of new reserves** | X × (1/rr) |
| **Public deposits ₹X of existing cash in a bank** | **Excess reserves created × (1/rr)** = X(1 − rr) × (1/rr) |
| **Bank has excess reserves of ₹E** | E × (1/rr) (total expansion of money supply through lending) |

**More examples:** rr = 20% → multiplier = 5; ₹500 of excess reserves → up to **₹2,500** of new money. rr = 25% → multiplier = 4.

### 4.4.6 Factors That Reduce the Actual Multiplier
- **Banks hold excess reserves** (don't lend everything).
- **Currency drain:** people keep some money as cash instead of depositing.
- **Weak loan demand** or credit constraints.
- A **lower rr increases** the multiplier; a **higher rr decreases** it.

### 4.4.7 Money Destruction
The process works in reverse: if reserves **fall** (or loans are repaid and not re-lent), the money supply **contracts** by a multiple.

### 4.4.8 Bank Runs and Deposit Insurance
- **Fractional-reserve banks cannot pay all depositors at once.** A **bank run** (panic withdrawals) can cause a bank to fail.
- **Deposit insurance (e.g., FDIC in the U.S., DICGC in India)** and the central bank acting as **lender of last resort** protect depositors and maintain confidence.

### 4.4.9 The Federal Funds Rate
The **federal funds rate** is the **interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves.** It is the central bank's main **policy target** in the U.S.

## 4.5 The Money Market

### 4.5.1 The Model
- **Vertical axis:** **nominal interest rate (i).** **Horizontal axis:** **quantity of money.**
- **Demand for money (MD):** **downward sloping** (higher interest rate → higher opportunity cost of holding money).
- **Supply of money (MS):** **vertical** (fixed by the central bank, **independent of the interest rate**).
- **Equilibrium nominal interest rate** is where **MD = MS.**
  - **Interest rate above equilibrium:** people hold more money than they want → they buy bonds → bond prices rise → interest rates fall.
  - **Below equilibrium:** people want more money than exists → they sell bonds → bond prices fall → interest rates rise.

### 4.5.2 Shifters of Money Demand
- **Price level ↑** or **real GDP ↑** (nominal income ↑) → people need more money for transactions → **MD shifts RIGHT** → **nominal interest rate ↑**.
- **Price level ↓** or **real GDP ↓** → MD shifts LEFT → interest rate ↓.
- **Changes in technology/financial innovation** (credit cards, ATMs, digital payments) → MD ↓ (shifts left).
- *A change in the interest rate itself = movement along MD.*

### 4.5.3 Shifters of Money Supply
- **Central bank actions** (open market operations, reserve requirement, discount rate).
- **MS ↑** (shifts right) → **interest rate ↓**; **MS ↓** (shifts left) → **interest rate ↑**.

### 4.5.4 Link to Aggregate Demand
- **Lower nominal interest rate** (and, with sticky prices, lower real rate) → **investment and interest-sensitive consumption ↑** (also **net exports ↑** through currency depreciation) → **AD shifts right.**
- This is the **transmission mechanism of monetary policy.**

## 4.6 Monetary Policy

### 4.6.1 Central Bank
The **central bank** (**Federal Reserve** in the U.S.; **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** in India; ECB in Europe) controls the **money supply and interest rates**, supervises banks and acts as **lender of last resort.**
**Dual mandate of the Fed:** **maximum employment** and **stable prices (≈2% inflation).** Central banks are usually **independent** of political pressure to avoid inflationary bias.

### 4.6.2 Tools of Monetary Policy
| Tool | Expansionary (increase money supply, lower interest rates) | Contractionary (decrease money supply, raise interest rates) |
|---|---|---|
| **Open market operations (OMO):** buying/selling government bonds | **Buy bonds** → pays banks → **reserves ↑** → MS ↑ → **federal funds rate ↓** | **Sell bonds** → banks pay → reserves ↓ → MS ↓ → rate ↑ |
| **Discount rate:** interest rate the central bank charges banks for loans | **Lower** | **Raise** |
| **Reserve requirement (rr):** minimum reserve ratio | **Lower** (multiplier ↑, excess reserves ↑) | **Raise** |
| **Interest on reserve balances (IORB) / overnight reverse repo:** rates the Fed pays banks/institutions for parking funds at the Fed | **Lower** (less incentive to hold reserves; banks lend) | **Raise** |
| **Target for federal funds rate** | **Lower target** | **Higher target** |

- **OMO is the most frequently used** traditional tool.
- **Quantitative easing (QE):** large-scale purchases of long-term securities to push down long-term interest rates when the policy rate is near zero.

> **In India:** the RBI mainly uses the **repo rate** (the rate at which it lends to banks), **reverse repo**, **CRR (cash reserve ratio)**, **SLR (statutory liquidity ratio)** and **open market operations.** The AP exam uses the Fed framework above.

### 4.6.3 The Transmission Mechanism
**Expansionary monetary policy (to fight a recession / recessionary gap):**
Central bank buys bonds → bank reserves ↑ → **money supply ↑** → **nominal interest rate ↓** (real rate ↓ in the short run) → **investment (and interest-sensitive consumption) ↑**; currency depreciates → **net exports ↑** → **AD shifts right** → **real GDP ↑, price level ↑, unemployment ↓.**

**Contractionary monetary policy (to fight inflation / inflationary gap):**
Central bank sells bonds → reserves ↓ → **money supply ↓** → **interest rate ↑** → **investment ↓**, currency appreciates → **net exports ↓** → **AD shifts left** → **price level ↓ (or inflation slows), real GDP ↓, unemployment ↑.**

### 4.6.4 Graph Steps for FRQs (Money Market + AD-AS)
1. Draw the **money market**: MD downward, MS vertical; equilibrium **i₁**.
2. Show the central bank action: **MS₁ → MS₂** (right for expansionary).
3. Show the new **i₂ < i₁**.
4. State: **investment ↑ → AD shifts right** in the AD-AS graph; label AD₂, PL₂, Y₂.

### 4.6.5 Quantity Theory of Money and Long-Run Neutrality
**Equation of exchange: M × V = P × Y**
(M = money supply, V = velocity of money (average number of times a unit of money is spent per period), P = price level, Y = real GDP.)
- If **V and Y are constant** (long run), a **% increase in M** causes the **same % increase in P** → **money growth leads to inflation** in the long run.
- **Money neutrality:** in the long run, changes in the money supply affect **nominal variables** (price level, nominal wages, nominal interest rates) but **not real variables** (real GDP, real interest rate, unemployment).
- **Short run:** because of **sticky prices/wages**, monetary policy **does** affect real GDP and unemployment.
- *Example:* if M grows 8%, real GDP grows 3% and V is constant, then inflation ≈ 8% − 3% = **5%.**

### 4.6.6 Effects of Monetary Policy on the Exchange Rate and Net Exports
Lower interest rates → **capital outflows** → **demand for the domestic currency falls** → **depreciation** → exports ↑, imports ↓ → **NX ↑ (AD ↑ further).** Higher rates have the opposite effect (preview of Unit 6).

### 4.6.7 Limitations of Monetary Policy
- **Time lags** (recognition, implementation, impact).
- **Liquidity trap / zero lower bound:** when interest rates are near 0, further reductions cannot stimulate the economy ("pushing on a string").
- **Weak loan demand or credit tightness** (banks won't lend; consumers/firms won't borrow).
- **Uncertain effects of expectations**, and **supply shocks** (cannot fix stagflation without trade-offs).
- Risk of **inflation** if too expansionary; **asset bubbles.**

### 4.6.8 Monetary vs. Fiscal Policy
| | **Monetary policy** | **Fiscal policy** |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | **Central bank** | **Government/legislature** |
| Tools | Money supply, interest rates | G and T |
| Speed | Quicker decisions (fewer political delays) | Slower (legislative lags) |
| Side effects | Influences interest rates, exchange rates, asset prices | Deficits/debt, crowding out |
| Targeting | Broad, affects the whole economy | Can be targeted at sectors/groups |

## 4.7 The Loanable Funds Market

### 4.7.1 The Model
The **loanable funds market** shows how **saving (supply of funds)** and **borrowing for investment (demand for funds)** determine the **real interest rate** — the **price of borrowing**.
- **Vertical axis:** **real interest rate (r).** **Horizontal axis:** **quantity of loanable funds.**
- **Supply of loanable funds:** **upward sloping** — a higher real interest rate gives savers **more incentive to save/lend**. **Sources:** household saving, business saving, **government budget surpluses (public saving)**, **foreign savings (capital inflows)**.
- **Demand for loanable funds:** **downward sloping** — a lower real interest rate makes **more investment projects profitable**. **Sources of demand:** **firms (investment)**, households (housing, durable goods), **government (borrowing to finance deficits)**, foreigners.
- **Equilibrium real interest rate:** where **quantity supplied = quantity demanded.**

### 4.7.2 Shifts in the Loanable Funds Market
| Change | Curve | Effect on real interest rate | Effect on investment/saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Government budget deficit ↑** (more borrowing) | **Demand ↑ (right)** | **r ↑** | **Crowding out:** private investment ↓ |
| **Budget surplus** or smaller deficit | Supply ↑ (or demand ↓) | r ↓ | Investment ↑ |
| **Increased private saving** (thriftier, saving incentives, tax breaks on saving) | **Supply ↑ (right)** | **r ↓** | Investment ↑ → future growth |
| **Increased expected profitability of investment, new technology** | **Demand ↑ (right)** | r ↑ | Investment ↑ (and saving ↑) |
| **Higher capital inflows from abroad** | Supply ↑ | r ↓ | Investment ↑ |
| **Decreased demand for investment** (pessimism) | Demand ↓ (left) | r ↓ | Investment ↓ |

### 4.7.3 Numerical Example (Crowding Out)
| Real interest rate (%) | Quantity demanded (private) | Quantity supplied |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 600 | 200 |
| 4 | 500 | 300 |
| **6** | **400** | **400** |
| 8 | 300 | 500 |
| 10 | 200 | 600 |

- **Initial equilibrium:** r = **6%**, funds = **400** (all private investment).
- **Government runs a deficit and borrows 100 at any interest rate** → total demand increases by 100 at each rate: 700, 600, 500, 400, 300.
- **New equilibrium:** QD = QS at **r = 7%** (QD = 450, QS = 450). Funds = 450, of which government = 100 → **private investment = 350** (down from 400).
- **Crowding out = 50** (private investment falls because of the higher interest rate; partial crowding out).

### 4.7.4 Loanable Funds vs. Money Market
| | **Money market** | **Loanable funds market** |
|---|---|---|
| Interest rate | **Nominal** | **Real** |
| Supply curve | Vertical (set by the central bank) | Upward sloping (saving) |
| Demand curve | Demand for **money** (liquidity) | Demand for **funds for investment/borrowing** |
| Time horizon | **Short run** (monetary policy) | **Long run** (saving, investment, growth, fiscal policy deficits) |
| Typical use in AP | **Monetary policy** analysis | **Crowding out, saving/investment, long-run growth** |

### 4.7.5 Why Saving and Investment Matter
- **Investment** raises the capital stock → **raises productivity and potential output** (LRAS right) → **long-run growth.**
- Policies that **raise national saving** (lower deficits, saving incentives) **lower the real interest rate and raise investment**, supporting long-run growth, but may reduce short-run consumption.
- **Crowding out** is the reason economists worry about large persistent deficits.

## Unit 4 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- **Real interest rate ≈ Nominal rate − Inflation.**
- **PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ.** Bond price ↔ interest rate **inverse**.
- **Money multiplier = 1 ÷ rr.**
- **Required reserves = rr × deposits; excess = reserves − required.**
- **Max ΔMS = Δ(excess) reserves × multiplier** (or new reserves × multiplier if added by the central bank).
- **M × V = P × Y.**

**Key terms glossary:** financial asset, stock, bond, liquidity, risk, return, present value, nominal/real interest rate, Fisher effect, money (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value), fiat money, M1, M2, fractional-reserve banking, reserves (required/excess), reserve requirement, money multiplier, federal funds rate, bank run, money market, money demand/supply, central bank, open market operations, discount rate, IORB, quantitative easing, expansionary/contractionary monetary policy, velocity, quantity theory, money neutrality, loanable funds, crowding out, liquidity trap.

**Top exam traps**
1. **Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions.**
2. **Credit cards are not money**; **M2 ⊃ M1**.
3. **The money supply curve is vertical**; the **money demand curve is downward sloping.**
4. **The money market uses the NOMINAL interest rate; loanable funds uses the REAL rate.**
5. **Central bank buys bonds → MS ↑ → i ↓ → AD ↑.**
6. **Multiplier = 1/rr** and applies to **excess reserves** for a deposit of existing cash.
7. **Government deficit → demand for loanable funds ↑ → real rate ↑ → crowding out.**
8. **Price level or real GDP increases shift money DEMAND right.**
9. Monetary policy is **neutral in the long run.**

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** If market interest rates rise, the price of existing bonds will:
(A) Rise (B) Fall (C) Stay the same (D) Double
**Answer: B.**

**2.** Which of the following is included in M2 but NOT in M1?
(A) Currency (B) Checkable deposits (C) Savings deposits (D) Traveler's checks
**Answer: C.**

**3.** The reserve requirement is 20%. A bank receives a ₹1,000 new deposit of cash from the public. The maximum amount the bank can lend is:
(A) ₹200 (B) ₹800 (C) ₹1,000 (D) ₹5,000
**Answer: B.** Required reserves = 200; excess = 800 (and the money multiplier is 5).

**4.** The central bank buys government bonds from banks. The most likely effects are:
(A) Money supply ↑, interest rates ↓ (B) Money supply ↓, interest rates ↑ (C) Money supply ↑, interest rates ↑ (D) No change
**Answer: A.**

**5.** Which would shift money demand to the right?
(A) A fall in the price level (B) A rise in the price level (C) Wider use of ATMs (D) A fall in real GDP
**Answer: B.**

**6.** Contractionary monetary policy is most likely to cause:
(A) Higher investment (B) AD to shift right (C) A higher nominal interest rate and AD to shift left (D) Lower unemployment
**Answer: C.**

**7.** An increase in the government's budget deficit will most likely cause, in the loanable funds market:
(A) A fall in the real interest rate (B) A rise in the real interest rate and crowding out of private investment (C) A shift in the supply of loanable funds to the right (D) No effect
**Answer: B.**

**8.** If the nominal interest rate is 9% and expected inflation is 4%, the expected real interest rate is:
(A) 13% (B) 5% (C) 4% (D) 9%
**Answer: B.**

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** The economy is in a recession with a recessionary gap. The reserve requirement is 10%.
(a) Name one open market operation the central bank should undertake and explain its effect on reserves.
(b) If the central bank buys ₹50 million of bonds from banks, what is the maximum change in the money supply?
(c) Draw a money market graph showing the effect on the nominal interest rate.
(d) Explain how the change in the interest rate affects AD, real GDP and the price level.

**Answer:**
(a) **Buy government bonds** → payment to banks raises their **reserves**.
(b) Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10; **ΔMS = 50 × 10 = ₹500 million (maximum).**
(c) MS shifts **right** (MS₁ → MS₂) with downward-sloping MD; **nominal interest rate falls (i₁ → i₂).**
(d) A lower interest rate **raises investment** (and net exports via depreciation) → **AD shifts right** → **real GDP rises, price level rises**, unemployment falls (closing the recessionary gap).

**Q2.** Suppose the government increases its budget deficit by borrowing in the loanable funds market.
(a) Show on a loanable funds graph the effect on the real interest rate and the quantity of loanable funds.
(b) Explain the concept of crowding out.
(c) Explain the long-run effect on economic growth.
(d) Now suppose households increase their saving. Show and explain the effect on the real interest rate and investment.

**Answer:**
(a) **Demand for loanable funds shifts right** → **real interest rate rises**, quantity of funds rises.
(b) Because the higher interest rate makes borrowing costlier, **private investment falls**, partly offsetting the deficit-financed government spending.
(c) Lower private investment reduces **capital accumulation**, so **LRAS grows more slowly** (lower long-run growth).
(d) **Supply shifts right** → **real interest rate falls**, **investment rises**, supporting future growth.
