## 3.1 Aggregate Demand (AD)

### 3.1.1 Definition
**Aggregate demand** shows the **total quantity of real GDP demanded** (total spending on domestically produced goods and services) at each **price level**, in a given period.
- **Vertical axis:** **price level (e.g., CPI or GDP deflator)**.
- **Horizontal axis:** **real GDP (Y)**.
- **AD = C + I + G + (X − M)**.

> **AD is NOT the sum of individual market demand curves.** It is a macro relationship between the **general price level** and total real output demanded.

### 3.1.2 Why Does AD Slope Downward? (Three Effects of a Price-Level Change)
When the **price level falls**, the quantity of real GDP demanded **rises** because:
1. **Wealth (real-balances) effect:** a lower price level raises the **real value of households' money and financial assets**, so they feel wealthier and **consume more**.
2. **Interest-rate effect:** a lower price level reduces the **demand for money**, lowering **interest rates**, which stimulates **investment and consumption on credit**.
3. **Exchange-rate (net export) effect:** a lower domestic price level makes **domestic goods cheaper relative to foreign goods** (and lowers interest rates, causing currency depreciation), so **exports rise and imports fall** → **net exports rise**.
*(The reverse holds for a rising price level.)*

> **Do not use the micro reasons (substitution/income effects)** to explain the AD slope.

### 3.1.3 Change in Quantity of Real GDP Demanded vs. Change in AD
- **Price-level change** → **movement along** the AD curve.
- **Change in a non-price determinant** → **shift** of the AD curve.

### 3.1.4 Determinants (Shifters) of AD
A **rightward shift (AD increase)** results from increases in **C, I, G, or NX** (at every price level):

| Component | Increases when... (AD shifts RIGHT) | Decreases when... (AD shifts LEFT) |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumption (C)** | Household **wealth ↑** (stock/housing prices), **consumer confidence/expectations ↑**, **taxes ↓** (higher disposable income), **household debt ↓**, **interest rates ↓** (affects durables) | Opposite |
| **Investment (I)** | **Real interest rate ↓**, **expected future profits ↑**, **business taxes ↓**, new **technology**, **excess capacity ↓**, business confidence ↑ | Opposite |
| **Government spending (G)** | **Government purchases ↑** (transfer payments act via C) | **G ↓** |
| **Net exports (NX)** | **Foreign income ↑** (more demand for exports), **domestic currency depreciates** (exports cheaper, imports dearer), **domestic prices fall relative to foreign** prices, foreign tariffs ↓ | Opposite |

*Example:* a stock-market boom raises wealth → C ↑ → AD shifts right; a recession abroad lowers exports → NX ↓ → AD shifts left.

### 3.1.5 The Consumption Function (Background for Multipliers)
- **Disposable income (Yd) = Income − Taxes (+ transfers).**
- Households **consume (C)** or **save (S)** their disposable income: **Yd = C + S.**
- **Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) = ΔC ÷ ΔYd** — the fraction of **each extra rupee** of disposable income that is **spent**.
- **Marginal propensity to save (MPS) = ΔS ÷ ΔYd.**
- **MPC + MPS = 1.**
- **Average propensity to consume (APC) = C ÷ Yd; APS = S ÷ Yd; APC + APS = 1.**
- **Consumption function:** **C = a + MPC × Yd** (a = autonomous consumption).

## 3.2 Multipliers

### 3.2.1 The Multiplier Effect
A **change in spending** (autonomous C, I, G or NX) has a **larger final effect on real GDP** because **one person's spending becomes another's income**, which is partly spent again, and so on.

### 3.2.2 Formulas
- **Spending (expenditure) multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS**
- **Tax multiplier = − MPC ÷ (1 − MPC) = − MPC ÷ MPS** (negative: a tax increase reduces GDP; a tax cut raises it)
- **Balanced-budget multiplier = 1** (equal increases in G and T raise GDP by the same amount: spending multiplier − |tax multiplier| = 1).
- **Change in real GDP = Multiplier × Initial change in spending (or taxes).**
- **Transfer-payment multiplier** = same as the tax multiplier (but positive for an increase in transfers).

> **Why is the tax multiplier smaller (in absolute value) than the spending multiplier?** A change in government spending changes AD by the **full amount** in round 1; a tax cut raises disposable income by the same amount, but only the **MPC share is spent** in round 1 (the rest is saved).

### 3.2.3 Worked Examples (MPC = 0.8)
- **MPS = 0.2; spending multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5; tax multiplier = −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4.**
- **Government spending ↑ ₹100:** ΔGDP = 5 × 100 = **+₹500**.
  *Round-by-round:* 100 + 80 + 64 + 51.2 + … = 100 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = **500**.
- **Tax cut of ₹100:** ΔGDP = −4 × (−100) = **+₹400**.
  *Round 1: consumption rises by 80 (not 100)* → 80 + 64 + 51.2 + … = 80 ÷ 0.2 = **400**.
- **Balanced budget:** ΔG = ₹100 and ΔT = +₹100 → +500 − 400 = **+₹100.**

**Higher MPC → larger multiplier.** MPC = 0.9 → multiplier 10; MPC = 0.5 → multiplier 2.

### 3.2.4 Real-World Limits of the Multiplier
The simple multiplier overstates the effect, because of:
- **Leakages:** saving, **taxes** (proportional), and **imports** (marginal propensity to import) reduce the amount re-spent.
- **Crowding out:** deficit financing may raise interest rates and reduce private investment.
- **Price-level changes:** in the AD-AS model, a rise in AD also **raises prices**, reducing the real output gain (the full horizontal shift of AD equals multiplier × ΔG, but the equilibrium change in real GDP is smaller if SRAS is upward sloping).
- **Expectations and time lags.**

## 3.3 Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

### 3.3.1 Definition
**SRAS** shows the **quantity of real GDP firms are willing to produce at each price level in the short run**, when **input prices (especially nominal wages) are sticky/fixed.** It slopes **upward**.

### 3.3.2 Why Does SRAS Slope Upward?
When the price level rises:
- Output prices rise while **nominal wages and other input prices are slow to adjust (sticky)** → **profit per unit rises** → firms **increase output**.
- **Sticky wages and prices** (contracts, menu costs, minimum wages, slow information) are the main reason.
- *(Also, firms may misperceive price changes and produce more.)*
In the **short run**, the economy can produce **above or below** its potential.

### 3.3.3 Determinants (Shifters) of SRAS
An **increase in SRAS (rightward shift)** occurs when the cost of production falls or productivity rises:

| Determinant | SRAS shifts RIGHT (increase) | SRAS shifts LEFT (decrease) |
|---|---|---|
| **Input (resource) prices**: wages, raw materials, energy | **Fall** (e.g., oil price drop) | **Rise** (**supply shock** like oil-price spike) |
| **Productivity/technology** | **Rise** | Fall |
| **Business taxes and regulations; subsidies** | Taxes ↓, subsidies ↑ | Taxes ↑, regulation ↑ |
| **Expectations of inflation** | Lower expected inflation (workers accept lower wage growth) | **Higher expected inflation** (wage demands rise) |
| **Quantity of resources** (labor force, capital) | Increase (also shifts LRAS) | Decrease |
| **Supply shocks** | Positive (good harvest) | Negative (natural disaster, war) |

> A **change in the price level** causes a **movement along** SRAS, not a shift.

## 3.4 Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)

### 3.4.1 Definition
**LRAS** is a **vertical line at the economy's potential (full-employment) output, Yf.**
- In the **long run**, **all prices and wages are flexible**; output depends only on **real factors** (the quantity and quality of resources and technology), not on the price level.
- **Potential GDP (Yf)** = output at the **natural rate of unemployment**; equals the **PPC** concept.
- LRAS is vertical because a higher price level raises nominal wages and costs proportionally in the long run, so **real wages and real output don't change.**

### 3.4.2 Determinants of LRAS (Shifters)
- **Quantity of resources:** labor force growth (population, immigration), capital accumulation (investment), discovery of natural resources.
- **Quality of resources:** **education and training (human capital)**, health.
- **Technological progress.**
- **Institutions:** stable property rights, rule of law, efficient government, open trade.
- **Natural rate of unemployment** (changes in structural factors) and demographic changes.
**LRAS shifts right = economic growth** (also shifts SRAS right). LRAS shifts left with a permanent loss of resources.

> **Changes in AD do NOT shift LRAS.**

## 3.5 Equilibrium in the AD-AS Model

### 3.5.1 Short-Run Macroeconomic Equilibrium
Occurs where **AD intersects SRAS**, determining the **equilibrium price level (PL\*)** and **real GDP (Y\*)**.

### 3.5.2 Long-Run Macroeconomic Equilibrium
Occurs where **AD = SRAS = LRAS**: real GDP = **potential GDP (Yf)**; unemployment = **natural rate**; no output gap.

### 3.5.3 Output Gaps
| | Recessionary gap | Inflationary gap |
|---|---|---|
| **Actual vs. potential** | **Y < Yf** | **Y > Yf** |
| **Unemployment** | **Above** natural rate (cyclical unemployment) | **Below** natural rate |
| **Price pressure** | Low/falling inflation | Rising inflation |
| **Output gap** | **Negative** | **Positive** |
| **Graph** | SR equilibrium is **left** of LRAS | SR equilibrium is **right** of LRAS |

**Output gap (%) = (Actual real GDP − Potential real GDP) ÷ Potential GDP × 100.**

## 3.6 Changes in the AD-AS Model in the Short Run

### 3.6.1 Demand Shocks (AD shifts)
| Shift | Price level | Real GDP | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AD increases** (right) | **↑** | **↑** | **↓** (inflationary gap if beyond Yf) |
| **AD decreases** (left) | **↓** | **↓** | **↑** (recessionary gap) |

*Examples:* AD ↑ — stock market boom, higher government spending, lower interest rates, a depreciating currency. AD ↓ — consumer pessimism, tax increase, falling investment, recession abroad.

### 3.6.2 Supply Shocks (SRAS shifts)
| Shift | Price level | Real GDP | Unemployment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SRAS increases** (right) | **↓** | **↑** | **↓** (e.g., technology advance, oil price drop) |
| **SRAS decreases** (left) | **↑** | **↓** | **↑** → **STAGFLATION** (e.g., oil price shock) |

**Stagflation** = **stagnant output (falling real GDP, rising unemployment) + inflation.** It's the classic result of a **negative supply shock** and creates a **policy dilemma**: expansionary policy would fight unemployment but worsen inflation, and vice versa.

### 3.6.3 Inflation Types in the AD-AS Model
- **Demand-pull inflation:** AD shifts right → higher price level and higher real GDP (output increases).
- **Cost-push inflation:** SRAS shifts left → higher price level and **lower** real GDP.

### 3.6.4 Both Curves Shift
One variable is determinate and the other is indeterminate (same as in micro). Example: AD ↑ and SRAS ↓ → price level definitely ↑; real GDP ambiguous.

### 3.6.5 Graphing Checklist (FRQs)
1. Label axes: **"Price level"** (vertical) and **"Real GDP"** (horizontal).
2. Draw and label **AD, SRAS, LRAS**; mark **Yf** (potential GDP) on the horizontal axis at LRAS.
3. Mark the initial equilibrium **PL₁, Y₁**.
4. Draw the shift(s) with new labels (**AD₂ / SRAS₂**) and the new equilibrium **PL₂, Y₂**.
5. State changes in price level, real GDP and unemployment; if Y₂ > Yf, say **"inflationary gap"**; if Y₂ < Yf, **"recessionary gap"**.

## 3.7 Long-Run Self-Adjustment

### 3.7.1 The Idea
If the economy is in a **gap**, **nominal wages and input prices eventually adjust**, shifting **SRAS** and returning the economy to **potential output (Yf)** **without government intervention** (though possibly slowly).

### 3.7.2 Recessionary Gap Self-Correction
1. Y < Yf; **unemployment > natural rate.**
2. Excess labor supply puts **downward pressure on nominal wages** (and other input prices).
3. **Production costs fall → SRAS shifts right.**
4. **Price level falls; real GDP rises back to Yf.**
5. New long-run equilibrium at **Yf with a lower price level** (AD unchanged).
*(Adjustment can be slow when wages are very sticky downward, motivating policy.)*

### 3.7.3 Inflationary Gap Self-Correction
1. Y > Yf; **unemployment < natural rate**; tight labor market.
2. **Nominal wages and input prices rise.**
3. **Production costs rise → SRAS shifts left.**
4. **Price level rises further; real GDP falls back to Yf.**
5. New long-run equilibrium at **Yf with a higher price level.**

### 3.7.4 Long-Run Neutrality
In the **long run**, changes in **AD affect only the price level**, **not real GDP or unemployment** (real output returns to potential). Only **supply-side changes (LRAS)** alter long-run real GDP. A temporary expansion beyond Yf merely creates inflation.

### 3.7.5 Policy Implication
- Without policy, the economy **self-corrects**, but the **adjustment may take years** (high unemployment meanwhile) → case for **active stabilization** (Keynesian view).
- **Classical / new classical view:** self-correction is fast; policy is unnecessary or harmful.
- **Keynesian view:** "In the long run we are all dead": wages are very sticky; use demand management.

## 3.8 Fiscal Policy

### 3.8.1 Definition
**Fiscal policy** = the government's use of **government spending (G)** and **taxes (T)** to influence aggregate demand and stabilize the economy. It is carried out by the legislature and the government (not the central bank).

### 3.8.2 Types
| | **Expansionary fiscal policy** | **Contractionary fiscal policy** |
|---|---|---|
| **Used when** | **Recessionary gap** (high unemployment) | **Inflationary gap** (high inflation) |
| **Tools** | **G ↑**, **T ↓**, **transfers ↑** | **G ↓**, **T ↑**, **transfers ↓** |
| **Effect on AD** | **Shifts right** (by multiplier × change) | **Shifts left** |
| **Budget effect** | Tends toward **deficit** | Tends toward **surplus** |
| **Result** | Price level ↑, real GDP ↑, unemployment ↓ | Price level ↓ (or slower inflation), real GDP ↓, unemployment ↑ |

### 3.8.3 How Big a Change Is Needed? (Closing the Gap)
**Required ΔG = GDP gap ÷ spending multiplier.**
**Required ΔT = GDP gap ÷ tax multiplier** (opposite sign).

**Example 1 (recession):** Yf = ₹1,000; actual Y = ₹900 (gap = −₹100); MPC = 0.8.
- Spending multiplier = 5 → **ΔG = +100 ÷ 5 = +₹20**.
- Tax multiplier = −4 → **ΔT = −100 ÷ 4 = −₹25** (cut taxes by ₹25).

**Example 2 (inflation):** Yf = ₹1,000; actual Y = ₹1,100 (gap = +₹100); MPC = 0.75.
- Spending multiplier = 4 → **ΔG = −100 ÷ 4 = −₹25**.
- Tax multiplier = −3 → **ΔT = +100 ÷ 3 = +₹33.3**.

> **Note:** this simple calculation assumes the **price level is fixed**; in the full AD-AS model, a smaller shift might suffice or the effect on real GDP may be smaller because the price level rises.

### 3.8.4 Budget Deficits, Surpluses and the Debt
- **Budget deficit:** G + transfers > T (in a given year). **Surplus:** T > G. **Balanced budget:** T = G.
- **National debt** = accumulated past deficits minus surpluses (a **stock**); the **deficit is a flow**.
- **Cyclical deficit:** arises from the business cycle (falling tax revenue, higher transfers in recessions). **Structural deficit:** exists even at full employment.
- Financing deficits: borrowing (selling bonds).

### 3.8.5 Crowding Out
When the government **borrows to finance a deficit**, it increases the **demand for loanable funds**, raising **real interest rates**, which **reduces private investment** (and interest-sensitive consumption) → **partially offsets** the expansionary effect of fiscal policy (**crowding out**). (Detailed in Unit 4 with the loanable funds market.)

### 3.8.6 Fiscal Policy Lags and Limitations
- **Recognition lag:** time to recognize that the economy has a problem.
- **Decision (legislative) lag:** time for politicians to agree on policy.
- **Implementation lag:** time to start spending/taxing changes.
- **Impact lag:** time for effects to work through the economy (multiplier rounds).
Other limitations: **crowding out**, **political motives** (political business cycle), **uncertain multipliers**, **inability to cut spending/raise taxes politically**, **debt burden**, **Ricardian equivalence** (households may save more expecting future taxes), **time inconsistency.**

### 3.8.7 Spending vs. Tax Changes
- **Government spending** has a **bigger multiplier** than an equal **tax cut** (because part of a tax cut is saved).
- **Targeting:** tax cuts for **high-MPC households** (low-income) have a larger effect than for high-income savers.
- Temporary tax cuts are spent less than permanent ones (people save part of temporary windfalls).

## 3.9 Automatic Stabilizers

### 3.9.1 Definition
**Automatic (built-in) stabilizers** are **features of the tax and transfer system that automatically** increase the budget deficit in recessions and the surplus in expansions, **without any new legislation**, smoothing the business cycle.

### 3.9.2 Examples
- **Progressive income taxes:** in a recession, incomes fall → tax revenue falls more than proportionally → disposable income cushioned; in a boom, tax revenue rises faster, restraining demand.
- **Unemployment insurance** and **welfare benefits:** payments rise in recessions (more claimants), fall in expansions.
- **Corporate taxes** (profits-based) and **sales taxes** also vary with activity.

### 3.9.3 Automatic vs. Discretionary Policy
| | **Automatic stabilizers** | **Discretionary fiscal policy** |
|---|---|---|
| Action needed | **None** (built in) | New legislation required |
| Lags | **No recognition/decision lag** | Substantial lags |
| Size | Limited, moderate | Can be large/targeted |
| Example | Rising unemployment benefits in a recession | A new stimulus package; a tax-rate change |

### 3.9.4 Effects
- **Recession:** tax revenue ↓, transfers ↑ → **deficit grows automatically** → supports AD.
- **Expansion:** tax revenue ↑, transfers ↓ → **deficit shrinks or surplus** → restrains inflationary pressure.
- Automatic stabilizers **reduce the size of fluctuations**, but **don't eliminate** them; they also mean the **actual budget balance is not a good measure of fiscal stance** (use the **structural/cyclically adjusted** balance).

## 3.10 Economic Growth in the AD-AS Model

### 3.10.1 Definition and Measurement
**Economic growth** = **sustained increase in an economy's potential output** (LRAS shifts right; PPC shifts outward), measured by the **rate of growth of real GDP** (or real GDP per capita).
- **Growth rate = (Real GDP₂ − Real GDP₁) ÷ Real GDP₁ × 100.**
- **Rule of 70:** years to double ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate.
- **Productivity:** output per worker (hour). Rising productivity is the main source of rising living standards.

### 3.10.2 Sources of Growth (Determinants)
1. **More physical capital** (investment in machines, factories, infrastructure) — **capital deepening.**
2. **More and better labor:** population growth, **human capital** (education, health).
3. **Technological progress** (R&D, innovation).
4. **Natural resources.**
5. **Institutions and policies:** property rights, rule of law, stable money, free trade, competition, political stability.
6. **Saving and investment rates**, openness to trade and foreign investment.

### 3.10.3 Growth in the AD-AS Graph
- **LRAS shifts right** (to Yf₂); **SRAS also shifts right** (lower costs/higher productivity).
- With AD constant: **price level falls**, real GDP rises. With AD also growing: **real GDP rises with stable or mildly rising prices**.
- **Long-run growth** is an issue of **supply-side** policies, not demand management.

### 3.10.4 Policies That Promote Growth
- **Invest in education, training, health** (human capital).
- **Encourage saving and investment** (tax incentives, stable financial system).
- **Support R&D and innovation** (patents, subsidies).
- **Invest in infrastructure.**
- **Free trade** and openness; **sound institutions**; **competition** and deregulation where efficient.
- **Trade-off:** more growth requires giving up **current consumption** (saving), similar to the PPC capital-goods trade-off.

### 3.10.5 Supply-Side Economics
Argues that **lower taxes (marginal rates) and deregulation** increase incentives to work, save and invest, shifting **LRAS (and SRAS) to the right.** Critics note possible **deficits** and inequality; the effect on revenue depends on the tax rate (**Laffer curve**).

## Unit 3 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- **AD = C + I + G + NX.**
- **MPC + MPS = 1.** **Spending multiplier = 1/(1 − MPC) = 1/MPS.**
- **Tax multiplier = −MPC/MPS.** **Balanced-budget multiplier = 1.**
- **ΔGDP = Multiplier × ΔSpending (or ΔTaxes).**
- **Required ΔG = Gap ÷ spending multiplier; required ΔT = Gap ÷ tax multiplier.**
- **Output gap = (Y − Yf) ÷ Yf.**
- **Growth rate = ΔY/Y × 100; Rule of 70.**

**Key terms glossary:** aggregate demand, wealth effect, interest-rate effect, exchange-rate effect, MPC, MPS, multiplier, aggregate supply (SRAS/LRAS), sticky wages, supply shock, stagflation, potential output, natural rate of unemployment, recessionary and inflationary gap, long-run self-adjustment, fiscal policy (expansionary/contractionary), budget deficit/surplus, national debt, crowding out, recognition/decision/implementation/impact lags, automatic stabilizers, progressive tax, economic growth, productivity, human capital, supply-side economics.

**Top exam traps**
1. **AD slopes downward because of wealth, interest-rate and exchange-rate effects**, not the micro substitution/income effects.
2. **Price-level change = movement along AD/SRAS**; other factors = shift.
3. **LRAS is vertical at Yf** and is unaffected by AD.
4. **A negative supply shock → stagflation** (P↑, Y↓, U↑).
5. **In the long run, AD changes only the price level**, not real GDP.
6. **Recessionary gap self-corrects with lower wages (SRAS shifts RIGHT)**; inflationary gap with higher wages (SRAS shifts LEFT).
7. **Tax multiplier magnitude = spending multiplier − 1.**
8. **Fiscal policy = G and T** (central bank actions are monetary policy).
9. **Expansionary fiscal policy → AD right**; **contractionary → AD left**.
10. When computing the needed change in G or T, **divide the gap by the multiplier**.

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** The aggregate demand curve slopes downward because a fall in the price level:
(A) Raises real wealth, lowers interest rates, and makes exports more competitive (B) Lowers production costs (C) Raises nominal wages (D) Reduces consumer confidence
**Answer: A.**

**2.** If the MPC is 0.75, the government spending multiplier is:
(A) 1.33 (B) 3 (C) 4 (D) 0.75
**Answer: C.** 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4.

**3.** Which of the following shifts SRAS to the right?
(A) A rise in nominal wages (B) A fall in oil prices (C) An increase in consumer confidence (D) An increase in government spending
**Answer: B.**

**4.** LRAS is vertical because, in the long run:
(A) Prices are fixed (B) Output is determined by resources and technology, not the price level (C) AD is perfectly elastic (D) Wages are sticky
**Answer: B.**

**5.** An economy is in a recessionary gap. In the absence of policy, the economy will return to full employment as:
(A) AD shifts left (B) SRAS shifts right as nominal wages fall (C) LRAS shifts left (D) Prices rise
**Answer: B.**

**6.** An oil-price shock that raises production costs is most likely to cause:
(A) Higher real GDP and lower prices (B) Lower real GDP and higher prices (stagflation) (C) Lower real GDP and lower prices (D) Higher real GDP and higher prices
**Answer: B.**

**7.** Which is an automatic stabilizer?
(A) A new tax cut passed by Congress (B) Unemployment insurance benefits (C) A central bank rate cut (D) A government infrastructure project
**Answer: B.**

**8.** The economy has a recessionary gap of ₹200 and the MPC is 0.75. By how much should government spending increase to close the gap?
(A) ₹50 (B) ₹150 (C) ₹200 (D) ₹800
**Answer: A.** Multiplier = 4; 200 ÷ 4 = ₹50.

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** Assume the economy is in long-run equilibrium. Consumer confidence suddenly falls sharply.
(a) Draw a correctly labeled AD-AS graph showing the initial long-run equilibrium (PL₁, Y₁ = Yf) and the short-run effect of the fall in confidence.
(b) State the effects on the price level, real GDP and unemployment.
(c) If the government wants to close the gap using government spending and the MPC is 0.8, and the gap is ₹60, calculate the required change in G.
(d) Explain what would happen to the price level and real GDP in the long run if the government does nothing.

**Answer:**
(a) Initial: AD₁ = SRAS₁ = LRAS at Yf. Fall in confidence → **C falls → AD shifts left (AD₂)** → new short-run equilibrium PL₂ < PL₁, Y₂ < Yf (recessionary gap).
(b) **Price level falls, real GDP falls, unemployment rises** above the natural rate.
(c) Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. **ΔG = 60 ÷ 5 = +₹12.**
(d) With high unemployment, nominal wages fall → **SRAS shifts right** → price level falls further and real GDP returns to **Yf**; the economy self-corrects at a **lower price level**.

**Q2.** A severe drought destroys a large share of the harvest (a negative supply shock) in an economy initially at long-run equilibrium.
(a) Show the effect on a correctly labeled AD-AS graph.
(b) Identify the effects on price level, real GDP and unemployment, and name the phenomenon.
(c) Explain why the central bank/government faces a dilemma.
(d) Explain how the economy returns to long-run equilibrium without policy.

**Answer:**
(a) **SRAS shifts left**: new equilibrium at higher price level, lower real GDP (Y₂ < Yf).
(b) **Price level ↑, real GDP ↓, unemployment ↑ → stagflation.**
(c) Expansionary policy (AD right) would fight unemployment but worsen inflation; contractionary policy would reduce inflation but worsen the recession.
(d) High unemployment lowers nominal wages and costs → **SRAS shifts back right** → price level falls and real GDP returns to Yf (assuming the shock is temporary).
