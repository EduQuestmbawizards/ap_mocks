## 3.1 The Production Function

### 3.1.1 Short Run vs. Long Run (Economic Definitions)
These are **not calendar periods**.
- **Short run:** a period in which **at least one input is fixed** (usually capital: plant size, machinery). Firms can change only variable inputs (labor, raw materials). Firms cannot enter or leave the industry in the short run.
- **Long run:** a period long enough for **all inputs to be variable** (firms can build new factories, change technology, enter or exit the industry). There are **no fixed costs** in the long run.

### 3.1.2 Fixed vs. Variable Inputs
- **Fixed inputs:** do not change with output (factory building, machinery lease).
- **Variable inputs:** change with output (labor hours, raw materials, electricity used).

### 3.1.3 The Production Function
The **production function** shows the relationship between the **quantity of inputs** and the **maximum output** that can be produced. In the short run, with capital fixed, we study how output changes as we add more of one variable input, **labor (L)**.

### 3.1.4 Total, Marginal and Average Product
- **Total Product (TP):** total output produced.
- **Marginal Product (MP):** the **additional output** from hiring **one more** unit of labor. **MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL.**
- **Average Product (AP):** output per worker. **AP = TP ÷ L.**

**Worked Example:**

| Labor (L) | Total Product (TP) | Marginal Product (MP) | Average Product (AP) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | — | — |
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10.0 |
| 2 | 30 | 20 | 15.0 |
| 3 | 60 | 30 | 20.0 |
| 4 | 78 | 18 | 19.5 |
| 5 | 92 | 14 | 18.4 |
| 6 | 102 | 10 | 17.0 |
| 7 | 108 | 6 | 15.4 |

### 3.1.5 Increasing and Diminishing Marginal Returns
- **Increasing marginal returns (workers 1–3):** MP rises. Reason: **specialization and division of labor**; workers do not waste time switching tasks, and the fixed capital is not yet fully used.
- **Diminishing marginal returns (from worker 4):** MP falls. Reason: workers share a **fixed** amount of capital (crowding, waiting for machines).
- **Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns:** *as more units of a variable input are added to a fixed amount of other inputs, the marginal product of the variable input eventually declines.* This is a **short-run** law. It is the root cause of the **U-shape of the cost curves** that follow.
- **Negative marginal returns:** MP < 0; additional workers reduce total output (too crowded).

### 3.1.6 Graph Relationships
- **TP** rises at an **increasing rate**, then at a **decreasing rate**, and reaches a maximum where **MP = 0**.
- **MP** rises, reaches a maximum (at the **inflection point of TP**), then falls.
- **AP** rises then falls. **MP intersects AP at the maximum of AP.** (Same mathematical rule as a marginal grade vs. a cumulative average.)
  - If MP > AP → AP is rising. If MP < AP → AP is falling.
  - In the example: MP of the 4th worker (18) < AP of 3 workers (20) → AP falls.
- **Law of diminishing returns** applies where MP starts to fall.

## 3.2 Short-Run Production Costs

### 3.2.1 Cost Concepts
- **Total Fixed Cost (TFC):** costs that **do not change** with output (rent, insurance, salaries of managers, interest on loans). Must be paid even at zero output. TFC is a **horizontal line**.
- **Total Variable Cost (TVC):** costs that **change with output** (wages of production workers, raw materials). TVC = 0 at zero output.
- **Total Cost (TC) = TFC + TVC.**
- **Marginal Cost (MC) = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ = ΔTVC ÷ ΔQ.** (Fixed cost does not change, so MC is determined only by variable cost.)
- **Average Fixed Cost (AFC) = TFC ÷ Q.** Always **falls** as Q rises ("spreading the overhead").
- **Average Variable Cost (AVC) = TVC ÷ Q.**
- **Average Total Cost (ATC) = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC.**

### 3.2.2 Cost Table (Worked Example)
TFC = ₹100 at every level of output.

| Q | TFC | TVC | TC | MC | AFC | AVC | ATC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100 | 0 | 100 | — | — | — | — |
| 1 | 100 | 50 | 150 | 50 | 100.00 | 50.00 | 150.00 |
| 2 | 100 | 90 | 190 | 40 | 50.00 | 45.00 | 95.00 |
| 3 | 100 | 125 | 225 | 35 | 33.33 | 41.67 | 75.00 |
| 4 | 100 | 165 | 265 | 40 | 25.00 | 41.25 | 66.25 |
| 5 | 100 | 215 | 315 | 50 | 20.00 | 43.00 | 63.00 |
| 6 | 100 | 275 | 375 | 60 | 16.67 | 45.83 | 62.50 |
| 7 | 100 | 345 | 445 | 70 | 14.29 | 49.29 | 63.57 |
| 8 | 100 | 430 | 530 | 85 | 12.50 | 53.75 | 66.25 |

*(Check: ATC = AFC + AVC. At Q = 6: 16.67 + 45.83 = 62.50 ✔.)*

### 3.2.3 Shapes of the Cost Curves
- **MC** is **U-shaped** (or "swoosh"): falls at first (increasing marginal returns), then rises (diminishing marginal returns).
- **AVC and ATC** are **U-shaped**.
- **AFC** slopes down continuously (rectangular hyperbola).
- **ATC lies above AVC**, and the **vertical gap between them = AFC**, which narrows as Q rises (but never closes).

### 3.2.4 Relationship Between Marginal and Average Curves
The **MC curve cuts the AVC and ATC curves at their minimum points**.
- When **MC < average**, the average is **falling**.
- When **MC > average**, the average is **rising**.
- When **MC = average**, the average is at its **minimum**.
In the table: MC at Q = 6 (60) is below ATC at Q = 5 (63), so ATC keeps falling up to Q = 6; MC at Q = 7 (70) is above ATC at Q = 6 (62.50), so ATC rises after that. **Minimum ATC ≈ ₹62.50 at Q = 6**; **minimum AVC ≈ ₹41.25 at Q = 4**. The **minimum of ATC occurs at a higher quantity than the minimum of AVC.**

### 3.2.5 Link Between Production and Cost
With a **constant wage (w)** for labor as the only variable input:
- **MC = w ÷ MP** → when MP rises, MC falls; when MP falls (diminishing returns), MC rises. **MC is the mirror image of MP.**
- **AVC = w ÷ AP** → AVC is lowest where AP is highest.
So diminishing marginal returns **cause** rising MC.

### 3.2.6 Shifts of Cost Curves
- A change in **fixed costs** (e.g., higher rent) shifts **AFC and ATC** (and TC) but **not MC or AVC**.
- A change in a **variable input price** (e.g., higher wage), or **technology**, shifts **MC, AVC and ATC**.
- A **per-unit tax** on output shifts MC, AVC and ATC up. A **lump-sum tax** shifts only ATC (fixed cost) and not MC.

## 3.3 Long-Run Production Costs

### 3.3.1 Long-Run Average Total Cost (LRATC)
In the long run all inputs are variable, so the firm can choose **any plant size**. Each plant size has its own **short-run ATC curve (SRATC)**. The **LRATC curve is the "envelope"**: the lower boundary of all possible SRATC curves, showing the **lowest possible average cost for each output level** when the firm can adjust plant size. LRATC is **U-shaped** (or "check mark").

### 3.3.2 Returns to Scale
- **Economies of scale:** LRATC **falls** as output rises (doubling all inputs more than doubles output).
  *Causes:* **specialization** of labor and management; **bulk purchasing** of inputs; ability to use **more efficient, large-scale machinery**; spreading **marketing, R&D and fixed overhead** over more units; cheaper financing.
- **Constant returns to scale:** LRATC is **flat** (doubling inputs doubles output). Firm size doesn't matter.
- **Diseconomies of scale:** LRATC **rises** as output rises (doubling inputs less than doubles output).
  *Causes:* **coordination and communication problems**, bureaucracy, difficulty monitoring workers, lower morale.
- **Minimum efficient scale (MES):** the **lowest output at which LRATC reaches its minimum.**

### 3.3.3 Important Distinctions
| Short-run concept | Long-run concept |
|---|---|
| **Diminishing marginal returns** (one variable input, others fixed) | **Economies/diseconomies of scale** (all inputs change) |
| Explains why **SR MC and ATC rise** | Explains why **LRATC falls then rises** |

> **Exam trap:** do not use "diminishing returns" to explain LRATC rising; use **diseconomies of scale**.

### 3.3.4 Industry Implications
- **Large MES relative to market demand** → few firms (natural monopoly in extreme cases).
- **Small MES relative to market** → many firms can survive (perfect competition).

## 3.4 Types of Profit

### 3.4.1 Revenue and Cost Definitions
- **Total Revenue (TR) = P × Q.**
- **Explicit costs:** actual payments (wages, rent, raw materials).
- **Implicit costs:** opportunity costs of the firm's own resources (owner's forgone salary, forgone interest on owner's capital, value of owner-supplied land/building).

### 3.4.2 Accounting Profit vs. Economic Profit
- **Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs.**
- **Economic profit = TR − (explicit costs + implicit costs)** = TR − economic costs.
- Economic profit is **always lower** than accounting profit when implicit costs are positive.

### 3.4.3 Normal Profit
**Normal profit** is the minimum return needed to keep the owner/entrepreneur in the business, i.e., the **implicit costs** (including the entrepreneur's opportunity cost) that are **included** in economic costs.
- **Economic profit = 0** means the firm earns **exactly normal profit** (accounting profit > 0 but equal to implicit costs). The firm has **no incentive to enter or leave**.
- **Economic profit > 0** → **above normal** → attracts entry.
- **Economic profit < 0** (economic loss) → **below normal** → causes exit in the long run.

### 3.4.4 Example
A bakery earns revenue ₹10,00,000. Explicit costs: ₹6,00,000. The owner gave up a job paying ₹5,00,000 (implicit cost).
- Accounting profit = ₹4,00,000. Economic profit = 10,00,000 − 6,00,000 − 5,00,000 = **−₹1,00,000** (economic loss). The owner would be better off taking the job.

## 3.5 Profit Maximization

### 3.5.1 Two Approaches
1. **Total approach:** choose the quantity where **TR − TC** is greatest.
2. **Marginal approach (the profit-maximizing rule):** **Produce the quantity where Marginal Revenue = Marginal Cost (MR = MC)**, with MC **rising** (cutting MC from below).

### 3.5.2 Why MR = MC?
- If **MR > MC**: the extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost → produce more.
- If **MR < MC**: the extra unit costs more than it brings in → produce less.
- Profit is maximized at the last unit where **MR ≥ MC**, i.e., at **MR = MC**.
This rule applies to **all market structures** (perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly).

### 3.5.3 Marginal Revenue
**MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.** For a **perfectly competitive firm**, the price is constant, so **MR = P = AR** (the firm's demand curve is horizontal). For other structures, MR < P (Unit 4).

### 3.5.4 Calculating Profit
**Profit = TR − TC = (P − ATC) × Q.**
On a graph: draw the **P = MR line**, find **Q\*** where it meets **MC** (rising part), then go **up/down to the ATC curve at Q\***. The **rectangle** between P and ATC with width Q\* is **economic profit** (if P > ATC) or **loss** (if P < ATC).

## 3.6 Firms' Short-Run Decisions to Produce and Shut Down

### 3.6.1 The Three Short-Run Situations (Perfect Competition, Price Taker)
At the profit-maximizing Q\* (where P = MC):

| Condition | Result | Short-run decision |
|---|---|---|
| **P > ATC** | **Economic profit** | Produce Q\* |
| **P = ATC** | **Zero economic profit** (normal profit, "break-even") | Produce Q\* |
| **AVC < P < ATC** | **Economic loss**, but TR covers all variable costs and part of fixed cost | **Keep producing** (loss is smaller than shutting down) |
| **P < AVC** | TR < TVC | **Shut down** (produce zero) |

### 3.6.2 The Shutdown Rule
**Shut down in the short run if P < minimum AVC** (or equivalently TR < TVC).
- If the firm **shuts down**, it loses its **fixed cost** (TFC). If it **produces at a loss** but P > AVC, it loses **less** than TFC, because revenue covers all variable costs and contributes towards fixed cost.
- **Fixed costs are sunk in the short run** and do not influence the decision.
- **Break-even price:** P = minimum ATC. **Shutdown price:** P = minimum AVC.

### 3.6.3 Worked Example (Using the Cost Table in 3.2.2)
| Market price | MR = MC quantity | TR | TC | Profit | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **₹70** | 7 (MC = 70) | 490 | 445 | **+45** | Produce 7 (profit) |
| **₹62.50** | ≈ 6 (MC(6) = 60 ≤ 62.5) | 375 | 375 | **0** | Produce 6, break even at min ATC |
| **₹55** | 5 (MC(5) = 50 ≤ 55 < 60) | 275 | 315 | **−40** | Produce 5 (loss < TFC of 100) because P > AVC (43) |
| **₹40** | 4 (MC = 40) | 160 | 265 | **−105** | **Shut down**: P < minimum AVC (41.25); producing loses ₹105 vs ₹100 if closed |

### 3.6.4 The Firm's Short-Run Supply Curve
For a perfectly competitive firm, the **short-run supply curve is the portion of the MC curve that lies above the minimum of AVC.** Below min AVC, quantity supplied is zero. **Market supply** = horizontal sum of all firms' supply curves.

## 3.7 Entry and Exit Decisions in the Long Run

### 3.7.1 Long-Run Adjustment (Perfect Competition)
In the long run, firms can **enter or exit** freely and **all inputs are variable**.
- **Economic profits** (P > ATC) → **new firms enter** → market **supply increases** (shifts right) → **market price falls** → profits shrink until economic profit = 0.
- **Economic losses** (P < ATC) → **firms exit** → market **supply decreases** → **price rises** → losses shrink until economic profit = 0.

### 3.7.2 Long-Run Equilibrium for a Perfectly Competitive Firm
In the long run:
- **P = MR = MC = minimum ATC** (also = minimum LRATC)
- **Economic profit = 0** (only normal profit)
- **No entry or exit**
- The firm produces at **minimum efficient scale**.

### 3.7.3 Adjustment Example: Increase in Demand
1. Market initially at long-run equilibrium: price = min ATC (P₁).
2. Demand increases → **price rises to P₂** (short run); firms earn **economic profits** and produce more (move up the MC curve).
3. Profits attract **entry** → market supply shifts right → price falls back down.
4. New **long-run equilibrium**: price returns to **min ATC** (if costs are unchanged), **industry output higher** (more firms), each firm back at its efficient scale, **zero economic profit**.

**For a decrease in demand:** price falls below ATC → **losses** → exit → supply shifts left → price returns to min ATC, output lower.

### 3.7.4 Long-Run Industry Supply (Types of Industries)
- **Constant-cost industry:** input prices do **not** change as industry expands → LR supply is **horizontal** at min ATC.
- **Increasing-cost industry:** input prices **rise** as the industry expands (competition for scarce inputs) → LR supply is **upward sloping** (new equilibrium price is higher).
- **Decreasing-cost industry:** costs fall as the industry expands (external economies) → LR supply is **downward sloping** (rare).

## 3.8 Perfect Competition

### 3.8.1 Characteristics
1. **Many small buyers and sellers**, so no single firm can influence the market price → **price takers**.
2. **Identical (homogeneous/standardized) products** → buyers see no difference.
3. **Free entry and exit** (no barriers to entry).
4. **Perfect information** about prices and quality.
5. **Perfect mobility of resources.**
*Examples (approximate):* wheat farming, some agricultural commodities, foreign-exchange markets.

### 3.8.2 Firm's Demand Curve
Because each firm is a **price taker**, it faces a **perfectly elastic (horizontal)** demand curve at the **market price**.
- **Demand = D = P = MR = AR** (all equal and horizontal).
- The **market** demand curve is **downward sloping**; the **firm's** demand is horizontal.

### 3.8.3 Short-Run Market and Firm Graphs
Draw **two side-by-side graphs**:
- **Market:** D and S determine **equilibrium price P\*** and Q\*.
- **Firm:** horizontal line at P\* (P = MR = D); **MC, ATC, AVC** curves; profit-max Q at **MR = MC**; show the **profit or loss rectangle**.

**Graph checklist for FRQs:**
1. Label axes: "Price/Cost (₹)" and "Quantity" on each graph.
2. Show MC, ATC (and AVC if needed) with MC intersecting ATC and AVC at their minimums.
3. Mark Q\* at MR = MC, then drop to ATC and mark the **profit/loss rectangle** with its vertices.

### 3.8.4 Efficiency in Long-Run Perfect Competition
- **Productive efficiency:** production at the **lowest possible ATC** (P = min ATC) → resources used at the lowest cost.
- **Allocative efficiency:** **P = MC** → marginal benefit to consumers (price) equals marginal cost of resources → the right quantity of the right goods; **total surplus is maximized**.
- Therefore perfect competition is the **benchmark of efficiency**, against which monopoly and other structures (Unit 4) are compared (they typically produce less output at a higher price, causing DWL).

### 3.8.5 Strengths and Limitations
- *Strengths:* efficient outcomes, low prices, no persistent economic profit; firms are pushed to minimize costs.
- *Limitations:* no product variety, little incentive for large-scale R&D, assumptions rarely met perfectly in the real world, does not address externalities or equity.

## Unit 3 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL; AP = TP ÷ L.
- TC = TFC + TVC; ATC = TC/Q = AFC + AVC; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ.
- MC = w ÷ MP; AVC = w ÷ AP.
- Economic profit = TR − explicit − implicit costs; Profit = (P − ATC) × Q.
- Rule: **MR = MC** (rising MC). Shutdown: **P < min AVC**. Break-even: **P = min ATC**.
- Perfect competition LR equilibrium: **P = MR = MC = min ATC**, economic profit = 0.

**Key terms glossary:** production function, fixed/variable inputs, short run, long run, total/marginal/average product, increasing and diminishing marginal returns, TFC, TVC, TC, AFC, AVC, ATC, MC, LRATC, economies of scale, diseconomies of scale, minimum efficient scale, explicit cost, implicit cost, accounting profit, economic profit, normal profit, marginal revenue, profit maximization, shutdown point, break-even point, price taker, perfect competition, productive efficiency, allocative efficiency, constant/increasing/decreasing-cost industry.

**Top exam traps**
1. Short-run cost shape = **diminishing marginal returns**; long-run shape = **economies/diseconomies of scale**.
2. **MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimums**, never AFC.
3. **Fixed costs are ignored** in the shutdown decision.
4. **Shutdown rule: P < min AVC** (not ATC).
5. Zero economic profit = **normal profit**, **not** a loss.
6. A **lump-sum tax** or change in fixed cost doesn't affect MC or the profit-maximizing output.
7. In the long run perfect competition always returns to **P = min ATC**.

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** The law of diminishing marginal returns explains why:
(A) ATC curve is always rising (B) MC eventually rises in the short run (C) LRATC rises (D) Firms leave the industry
**Answer: B.**

**2.** When marginal cost is below average total cost, average total cost is:
(A) Rising (B) Falling (C) At its minimum (D) Constant
**Answer: B.**

**3.** A firm's total revenue is ₹5,00,000, explicit costs ₹3,00,000, and implicit costs ₹2,50,000. Its economic profit is:
(A) ₹2,00,000 (B) ₹50,000 (C) −₹50,000 (D) 0
**Answer: C.** 5,00,000 − 5,50,000 = −₹50,000.

**4.** A perfectly competitive firm should shut down in the short run when:
(A) P < ATC (B) P < AVC (C) P < MC (D) TR < TFC
**Answer: B.**

**5.** In the long run, a perfectly competitive firm earns:
(A) Positive economic profit (B) Negative economic profit (C) Zero economic profit (D) Monopoly profit
**Answer: C.**

**6.** In a perfectly competitive industry, price is above ATC. Over time:
(A) Firms exit and price rises (B) Firms enter, supply increases, price falls (C) Demand falls (D) MC shifts up permanently
**Answer: B.**

**7.** A firm in perfect competition sells at ₹30; at its current output MC = ₹36, rising. The firm should:
(A) Increase output (B) Decrease output (C) Keep output unchanged (D) Shut down
**Answer: B.** MR (30) < MC (36) → produce less.

**8.** Which of the following causes the LRATC curve to rise at high output?
(A) Diminishing marginal returns (B) Diseconomies of scale (C) Spreading fixed costs (D) Specialization
**Answer: B.**

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** A perfectly competitive firm faces a market price of ₹50. Its marginal cost is ₹50 at 100 units, its ATC at 100 units is ₹42, and its AVC at 100 units is ₹35.
(a) What is the profit-maximizing quantity?
(b) Calculate economic profit.
(c) Describe what will happen in the market in the long run and the effect on this firm's profit.
(d) If the price instead fell to ₹30 (and the firm's min AVC is ₹35), what should the firm do? Why?

**Answer:**
(a) **100 units** (MR = P = MC = 50).
(b) Profit = (50 − 42) × 100 = **₹800**.
(c) Economic profit attracts **entry** → market supply shifts right → price falls until P = min ATC; the firm's profit falls to **zero**.
(d) **Shut down.** P (₹30) < AVC (₹35): revenue doesn't cover variable costs, so producing loses more than the fixed cost alone.

**Q2.** Explain, using diminishing marginal returns, why a firm's MC curve is eventually upward-sloping, and explain how an increase in the wage rate affects the firm's MC, AVC and ATC curves.

**Answer:** As more workers are added to fixed capital, each additional worker adds less output (MP falls). Since MC = w ÷ MP, a falling MP means each extra unit of output requires more labor and costs more → **MC rises**. A higher wage raises the variable cost of every unit, so **MC, AVC and ATC all shift up**; **AFC and TFC do not change**.
