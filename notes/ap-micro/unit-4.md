## 4.1 Introduction to Imperfect Competition

### 4.1.1 What is Imperfect Competition?
**Imperfect competition** describes any market structure that does not meet the conditions of perfect competition: sellers have some degree of **market power**, meaning the ability to influence the price of what they sell. The three imperfectly competitive structures are **monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly**.

### 4.1.2 Price Takers vs. Price Makers
- **Perfectly competitive firms are price takers:** the market sets the price; the firm's demand curve is horizontal.
- **Imperfectly competitive firms are price makers (price searchers):** they face a **downward-sloping demand curve**. To sell more, they must **lower the price**; to raise the price, they must accept a **lower quantity**.

### 4.1.3 Why Does MR Fall Below Price?
Because a price-making firm must lower the price on **all** units to sell one more unit, the **marginal revenue is less than the price**.
- **MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.** When the firm sells one more unit, it **gains** the price of the new unit but **loses** revenue on all the earlier units now sold at the lower price.
- For a **straight-line demand curve**, the **MR curve has the same vertical intercept but twice the slope** (it hits the horizontal axis at **half** the quantity of the demand curve).
- Relationship with elasticity: **MR = P × (1 − 1/|Ed|)**. So MR > 0 only when demand is **elastic** (|Ed| > 1); MR = 0 where demand is **unit elastic**; MR < 0 where demand is inelastic.
- **A profit-maximizing firm with market power never operates in the inelastic part of its demand curve** (it could raise price and revenue while lowering costs).

### 4.1.4 The Four Market Structures at a Glance

| Feature | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of firms** | Very many | Many | Few | One |
| **Product** | Identical | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | Unique (no close substitutes) |
| **Entry barriers** | None | Low/none | High | Very high/blocked |
| **Control over price** | None (price taker) | Some | Considerable (interdependent) | Considerable (price maker) |
| **Demand faced by the firm** | Horizontal | Downward sloping (fairly elastic) | Downward sloping | Market demand (downward sloping) |
| **MR vs. P** | MR = P | MR < P | MR < P | MR < P |
| **Long-run economic profit** | Zero | Zero | Possible | Possible |
| **Efficiency** | Productively and allocatively efficient | Neither | Usually neither | Neither |
| **Examples** | Wheat farming | Restaurants, salons, clothing | Airlines, soft drinks, mobile networks | Local water utility, patent-holding drug |

## 4.2 Monopoly

### 4.2.1 Definition and Characteristics
A **monopoly** is a market with a **single seller** of a product that has **no close substitutes**, protected by **barriers to entry**.
- The monopolist is a **price maker**.
- The firm's demand curve **is** the market demand curve (downward sloping).
- **MR < P** at every positive quantity.
- The monopolist **chooses a point on the demand curve**: it can set the price **or** the quantity, not both.
- **There is no supply curve** for a monopolist (the quantity it supplies depends on the demand curve's elasticity as well as costs).

### 4.2.2 Barriers to Entry (Sources of Monopoly)
1. **Control of a key resource** (e.g., De Beers and diamonds historically).
2. **Government-created barriers:** **patents** and copyrights (temporary monopolies to reward innovation), **licenses**, **franchises** (exclusive rights, e.g., a city's water utility).
3. **Economies of scale / natural monopoly:** a single firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms (electricity grids, water pipes, railways). The LRATC **keeps falling** over the entire range of market demand.
4. **Network effects:** the product is more valuable as more people use it (social networks, payment systems).
5. **High start-up costs, brand loyalty, or strategic actions** such as predatory pricing.

### 4.2.3 Profit Maximization for a Monopolist
**Rule: produce where MR = MC; then charge the price on the DEMAND curve at that quantity.**

**Step-by-step on a graph:**
1. Draw the **downward-sloping demand (D)** and the **MR curve** below it (twice as steep).
2. Draw **MC** and **ATC**.
3. Find **Q\*** where **MR = MC**.
4. Go **up to the demand curve** to find the **price P\*** (the highest price consumers will pay for Q\*).
5. Go down from the demand curve to the ATC curve at Q\*. **Profit = (P\* − ATC at Q\*) × Q\***, the rectangle between P\* and the ATC at Q\*.

**Common mistake:** reading the price off the MR or MC curve. **The price always comes from the demand curve.**

### 4.2.4 Worked Numerical Example
Demand: **P = 100 − Q**, so **MR = 100 − 2Q**. MC = ₹20 (constant). Total fixed cost = ₹400, so **ATC = 20 + 400/Q**.

| Q | P = 100 − Q | TR | MR = 100 − 2Q |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100 | 0 | 100 |
| 10 | 90 | 900 | 80 |
| 20 | 80 | 1,600 | 60 |
| 30 | 70 | 2,100 | 40 |
| 40 | 60 | 2,400 | 20 |
| 50 | 50 | 2,500 | 0 |

*(TR is maximized at Q = 50 where MR = 0, i.e., at the midpoint of the demand curve where demand is unit elastic.)*

**Monopoly equilibrium:**
- MR = MC → 100 − 2Q = 20 → **Q = 40**, **P = 100 − 40 = ₹60**.
- ATC at Q = 40: 20 + 400/40 = ₹30.
- **Profit = (60 − 30) × 40 = ₹1,200** (check: TR 2,400 − TC 1,200 = 1,200 ✔).

**Compare with perfect competition (P = MC):**
- 100 − Q = 20 → **Q = 80, P = ₹20**.

### 4.2.5 Monopoly vs. Perfect Competition

| | Perfect competition | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantity** | 80 | 40 (lower) |
| **Price** | ₹20 (= MC) | ₹60 (> MC) |
| **Consumer surplus** | ½ × 80 × 80 = ₹3,200 | ½ × 40 × 40 = ₹800 |
| **Profit (producer surplus above variable cost)** | 0 | (60 − 20) × 40 = ₹1,600 |
| **Total surplus** | 3,200 | 2,400 |
| **Deadweight loss** | 0 | **₹800** = ½ × (80 − 40) × (60 − 20) |

**Why a deadweight loss?** At the monopoly quantity, **P > MC**: the benefit consumers place on the last unit exceeds the cost of producing it, but the monopolist doesn't produce it because it would have to cut the price on all other units. **Allocative inefficiency** (P > MC). Also **productive inefficiency** (not producing at minimum ATC).

**Other points:**
- The monopolist can earn **positive economic profit in the long run** because **entry is blocked**.
- **Rent-seeking:** resources spent on lobbying/legal efforts to obtain/keep monopoly power are a **waste**.
- **Possible benefits:** economies of scale (lower cost than many small firms), funds for **R&D and innovation** (patents reward invention).
- **Monopolists in the long run** don't need to produce at the minimum of ATC and may suffer from "**X-inefficiency**" (lack of pressure to cut costs).

### 4.2.6 Natural Monopoly and Regulation
A **natural monopoly** has a LRATC curve that declines over the entire relevant range of demand. Government options:
1. **Unregulated monopoly:** MR = MC; high price, low output, DWL.
2. **Socially optimal price (P = MC), "marginal-cost pricing":** allocatively efficient, **but** because ATC is **above** MC when scale economies exist, the firm incurs a **loss** (it cannot cover fixed costs) → requires a **government subsidy** to remain in business.
   *Example:* P = MC = ₹20, Q = 80; ATC at 80 = 20 + 400/80 = ₹25 > 20 → loss of ₹5 × 80 = ₹400 (equal to the fixed cost).
3. **Fair-return price (P = ATC), "average-cost pricing":** the demand curve intersects ATC; the firm earns **zero economic profit (normal profit)**, no subsidy needed; **some DWL remains** (P > MC) but less than the unregulated case.
   *Example:* 100 − Q = 20 + 400/Q → Q ≈ 75, **P ≈ ₹25**.
4. **Other policies:** antitrust (breaking up or blocking mergers), public ownership, price caps, encouraging competition.

## 4.3 Price Discrimination

### 4.3.1 Definition
**Price discrimination** is charging **different prices to different customers for the same good or service** when the price differences are **not due to cost differences**. It lets a firm capture more consumer surplus and raise profit.

### 4.3.2 Necessary Conditions
1. The firm has **market power** (downward-sloping demand; it is a price maker).
2. The firm can **identify and separate** customers (or markets) with different willingness to pay/price elasticities.
3. The firm can **prevent resale** (arbitrage) between low-price and high-price buyers.
4. Customer groups have **different price elasticities of demand.**

### 4.3.3 Types
- **First-degree (perfect) price discrimination:** the firm charges **each consumer exactly the maximum they are willing to pay.**
- **Second-degree:** price depends on **quantity** purchased (bulk discounts, tiered pricing).
- **Third-degree:** the firm divides buyers into **groups** and charges each group a different price (student, senior, adult tickets; domestic vs. foreign; peak vs. off-peak; coupons).

### 4.3.4 Perfect Price Discrimination
- **Demand curve becomes the firm's MR curve** (each additional unit is sold at its own price without lowering earlier prices).
- The firm produces where **P = MC** (the same quantity as perfect competition → **no deadweight loss**; **allocatively efficient**).
- **Consumer surplus = 0**; the entire surplus goes to the producer.
- Using the example from 4.2.4: Q = 80; producer surplus (profit over variable cost) = ½ × 80 × 80 = ₹3,200; subtract fixed cost ₹400 → profit ₹2,800.

### 4.3.5 Third-Degree Price Discrimination (Two Markets)
**Rule:** in each market, produce until **MR = MC**, then charge the price on that market's demand curve. The group with **less elastic demand pays a higher price**.

**Example** (MC = ₹4 constant):
- **Adults:** P = 16 − Q → MR = 16 − 2Q = 4 → **Q = 6, P = ₹10**.
- **Students:** P = 10 − Q → MR = 10 − 2Q = 4 → **Q = 3, P = ₹7**.
- Elasticities: adults 10/6 = **1.67** (less elastic); students 7/3 = **2.33** (more elastic) → adults pay more ✔.
- Profit (over variable cost) = (10 − 4) × 6 + (7 − 4) × 3 = 36 + 9 = **₹45**, which is higher than charging one price to both.

### 4.3.6 Effects of Price Discrimination
- **Higher profit** for the firm; **less consumer surplus** for high-willingness-to-pay buyers.
- **Output increases** compared with a single-price monopoly (some buyers who would not buy at the single price now buy) → may reduce DWL.
- Some consumers (low-income, students) **benefit** by getting access to the product at lower prices.

## 4.4 Monopolistic Competition

### 4.4.1 Characteristics
1. **Many firms** (each is small; no single firm dominates).
2. **Differentiated products:** close but **imperfect substitutes** (different brands, styles, quality, location, service).
3. **Free (or easy) entry and exit** in the long run.
4. Firms have **some price-making power** because of product differentiation; the demand curve is **downward sloping but relatively elastic** (many close substitutes).
5. Heavy **non-price competition:** advertising, branding, packaging, quality, location, service.
*Examples:* restaurants, hair salons, clothing brands, coffee shops, boutiques.

### 4.4.2 Short-Run Equilibrium
The firm acts like a monopolist: **produce where MR = MC**, charge the price from its demand curve.
- If **P > ATC** → **economic profit**.
- If **P < ATC** → **economic loss** (shutdown rule still applies: shut down if P < AVC).

### 4.4.3 Long-Run Equilibrium
- **Economic profits attract entry** → new firms offer close substitutes → each existing firm's **demand shifts left** (smaller market share) and becomes **more elastic**.
- **Losses cause exit** → remaining firms' demand shifts right.
- Process stops when **economic profit = 0**: the **demand curve is tangent to the ATC curve** at the quantity where **MR = MC**.
- **Long-run result:** **P = ATC > MC**; economic profit = 0; the firm produces **to the left of the minimum ATC**.

### 4.4.4 Efficiency
- **Allocatively inefficient:** **P > MC** (too little output of the differentiated good).
- **Productively inefficient:** **P > minimum ATC** → **excess capacity** (the firm could produce more at a lower ATC; the gap between its actual output and the min-ATC output).
- **Benefit:** consumers gain **variety**, innovation and choice. Some of the inefficiency can be viewed as the "price of variety."
- **Advertising:** can inform consumers and encourage competition, but also raises costs and may create artificial differentiation.

### 4.4.5 Comparison with Perfect Competition (Long Run)
| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition |
|---|---|---|
| Economic profit | 0 | 0 |
| Price | = MC = min ATC | = ATC > MC |
| Output | Efficient scale | Less than efficient scale (excess capacity) |
| Variety | None | High |

## 4.5 Oligopoly and Game Theory

### 4.5.1 Characteristics of Oligopoly
1. **A few large firms** dominate the market.
2. **Interdependence:** each firm's decisions (price, output, advertising) significantly affect rivals, and each must **anticipate rivals' reactions**.
3. Products may be **identical** (steel, oil) or **differentiated** (cars, smartphones, airlines).
4. **High barriers to entry** (economies of scale, patents, high capital costs, brand loyalty, control of resources).
5. Firms have **price-making power**; economic profit **can persist** in the long run.
- **Concentration ratio:** the percentage of total market output/sales accounted for by the **largest n firms** (e.g., 4-firm concentration ratio). A high ratio (usually above ~60–80%) indicates oligopoly.

### 4.5.2 Collusion and Cartels
- **Collusion:** firms **cooperate** (explicitly or tacitly) to **restrict output and raise prices**, acting like a monopolist, to maximize joint profit.
- **Cartel:** a formal agreement among producers (e.g., OPEC). **Explicit collusion is illegal** in many countries (antitrust law).
- **Cartels tend to be unstable** because each member has an **incentive to cheat** (produce more than its quota and capture extra profit when others hold back).
- Factors that make collusion harder: more firms, differentiated products, unequal costs, a possibility of secret price cutting, strong antitrust enforcement.

### 4.5.3 Game Theory: Key Concepts
**Game theory** studies strategic behavior when each player's outcome depends on **the choices of others**.
- **Players:** the decision makers (firms).
- **Strategies:** the choices available (e.g., high price/low price; advertise/don't).
- **Payoff matrix:** a table showing each player's payoff for every combination of strategies.
- **Dominant strategy:** a strategy that is **best for a player regardless of what the other player does.**
- **Nash equilibrium:** a set of strategies where **no player can improve its payoff by unilaterally changing its own strategy**, given the other's strategy. (If a dominant strategy exists for every player, the combination of dominant strategies is a Nash equilibrium.)
- **Prisoner's dilemma:** a game in which each player's dominant strategy leads to an outcome that is **worse for both** than if they had cooperated.

### 4.5.4 Example: Pricing Game (Prisoner's Dilemma)
Payoffs are profits (₹ crores): (Firm A, Firm B).

| | **B: High Price** | **B: Low Price** |
|---|---|---|
| **A: High Price** | (10, 10) | (2, 14) |
| **A: Low Price** | (14, 2) | (5, 5) |

**Finding dominant strategies:**
- **Firm A:** if B charges High, A earns 10 (High) vs. 14 (Low) → **Low** is better. If B charges Low, A earns 2 (High) vs. 5 (Low) → **Low** is better. → **Low price is A's dominant strategy.**
- **Firm B:** by symmetry, **Low price is dominant.**
- **Nash equilibrium: (Low, Low) = (5, 5).**
- Both would be **better off at (High, High) = (10, 10)**, but each has an incentive to cheat. This explains why **collusion breaks down** in one-shot games.

**Repeated games:** if firms interact repeatedly, **threats of punishment** (e.g., "tit-for-tat") and the value of long-term cooperation can sustain collusion.

### 4.5.5 Example: A Game Without a Dominant Strategy
| | **B: Advertise** | **B: Don't advertise** |
|---|---|---|
| **A: Advertise** | (8, 8) | (12, 4) |
| **A: Don't advertise** | (4, 12) | (10, 10) |

- A: if B advertises → A prefers Advertise (8 vs 4); if B doesn't → Advertise (12 vs 10). So advertise is dominant for A; same for B. **Nash equilibrium: (Advertise, Advertise) = (8, 8)**, though (10, 10) would be better (another prisoner's dilemma: advertising wars).

*Method for finding Nash equilibria when no dominant strategies exist:* circle each player's **best response** to each of the other's choices; a cell with both payoffs circled is a Nash equilibrium (there may be 0, 1 or several).

### 4.5.6 Entry Deterrence and Other Strategies
- **Limit pricing / predatory pricing:** the incumbent sets a low price to discourage entry.
- **Excess capacity, advertising and product differentiation** can raise barriers.
- **Price leadership:** one dominant firm sets the price; others follow (a form of tacit collusion).

### 4.5.7 Efficiency and Policy
- Oligopolies typically produce **less output at higher prices than perfect competition**: **P > MC** (allocative inefficiency); may be productively inefficient too.
- **Antitrust policy** (blocking mergers, banning price-fixing) aims to preserve competition.
- Positive side: oligopoly firms can reap **economies of scale** and invest in **R&D**.

## Unit 4 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ; for linear demand P = a − bQ → MR = a − 2bQ.
- MR = P(1 − 1/|Ed|).
- Profit max (all structures): **MR = MC**.
- Monopoly price: from the **demand curve** at the MR = MC quantity.
- Profit = (P − ATC) × Q.
- Monopoly DWL = ½ × (Q_competitive − Q_monopoly) × (P_monopoly − MC) (for constant MC and linear demand).
- Monopolistic competition long run: **P = ATC > MC**.

**Key terms glossary:** market power, price maker, marginal revenue, monopoly, barriers to entry, patent, natural monopoly, deadweight loss, allocative/productive inefficiency, rent-seeking, price discrimination (first/second/third degree), monopolistic competition, product differentiation, non-price competition, excess capacity, oligopoly, interdependence, concentration ratio, collusion, cartel, game theory, payoff matrix, dominant strategy, Nash equilibrium, prisoner's dilemma.

**Top exam traps**
1. **Price from the demand curve, never from MR or MC.**
2. MR curve for a straight-line demand has **twice the slope**.
3. A monopolist never produces in the **inelastic** range.
4. Monopolistic competition long run = **zero economic profit but still P > MC** (inefficient).
5. **Nash equilibrium ≠ best joint outcome.**
6. Price discrimination requires **no resale** and different elasticities.
7. Perfect price discrimination has **no DWL**, but consumer surplus is **zero**.
8. A natural monopoly forced to charge P = MC will **lose money**.

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** For a monopolist, marginal revenue is:
(A) Equal to price (B) Greater than price (C) Less than price (D) Always negative
**Answer: C.**

**2.** A profit-maximizing monopolist produces the quantity where:
(A) P = MC (B) MR = MC (C) P = ATC (D) MR = P
**Answer: B.**

**3.** Compared with perfect competition, a monopoly typically produces:
(A) A higher output at a lower price (B) A lower output at a higher price (C) The same output at a higher price (D) A lower output at a lower price
**Answer: B.**

**4.** Which is required for successful price discrimination?
(A) Perfectly elastic demand (B) The ability to prevent resale (C) Many competitors (D) Identical elasticities among customers
**Answer: B.**

**5.** In the long run, a monopolistically competitive firm earns:
(A) Positive economic profit (B) Zero economic profit with P = ATC > MC (C) Zero economic profit with P = MC (D) Negative economic profit
**Answer: B.**

**6.** In a prisoner's dilemma, the Nash equilibrium is:
(A) The outcome with the highest total payoff (B) The outcome where both firms use their dominant strategies (C) Always cooperation (D) Never stable
**Answer: B.**

**7.** A cartel is difficult to maintain mainly because:
(A) Demand is perfectly elastic (B) Each member has an incentive to cheat on the quota (C) MC is zero (D) Firms are price takers
**Answer: B.**

**8.** A regulated natural monopoly is required to set P = MC. The most likely consequence is:
(A) Positive economic profit (B) An economic loss (C) Zero DWL and positive profit (D) A reduction in output
**Answer: B.** Because ATC > MC at that quantity, revenue doesn't cover total cost.

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** A monopolist faces the demand curve P = 100 − Q and has constant MC = ATC = ₹20 (no fixed cost).
(a) Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price.
(b) Calculate the monopolist's profit.
(c) Calculate consumer surplus.
(d) What quantity would a perfectly competitive industry produce? Calculate the deadweight loss caused by the monopoly.

**Answer:**
(a) MR = 100 − 2Q = 20 → **Q = 40**, **P = ₹60**.
(b) Profit = (60 − 20) × 40 = **₹1,600**.
(c) CS = ½ × 40 × (100 − 60) = **₹800**.
(d) Competitive: P = MC → Q = **80**. DWL = ½ × (80 − 40) × (60 − 20) = **₹800**.

**Q2.** Two firms, X and Y, each choose to "Cooperate" (keep price high) or "Defect" (cut price). Payoffs (X, Y): (Cooperate, Cooperate) = (6, 6); (Cooperate, Defect) = (1, 9); (Defect, Cooperate) = (9, 1); (Defect, Defect) = (3, 3).
(a) Identify each firm's dominant strategy.
(b) Identify the Nash equilibrium.
(c) Explain why the firms don't end up at (6, 6) in a one-time game, and one way they could sustain it.

**Answer:**
(a) For X: if Y cooperates, Defect gives 9 > 6; if Y defects, Defect gives 3 > 1 → **Defect** is dominant; same for Y.
(b) **(Defect, Defect) = (3, 3).**
(c) Each has an incentive to cheat, since cheating pays more whatever the other does. Cooperation can be sustained in a **repeated game** with credible punishment (e.g., tit-for-tat) or by enforceable agreements (although cartels are illegal in many countries).
