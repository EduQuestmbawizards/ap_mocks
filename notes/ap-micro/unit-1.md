## 1.1 Scarcity, Choice and Opportunity Cost

### 1.1.1 What is Economics?
Economics is the study of how individuals, businesses, governments and societies make choices when resources are limited but wants are unlimited. Everything in economics grows out of this single idea.

- **Microeconomics** studies the decisions of individual decision-makers: consumers, workers, firms, and individual markets (e.g., the market for pizza, the labor market for nurses).
- **Macroeconomics** studies the economy as a whole: GDP, inflation, unemployment, growth, national policy.
- **Positive economics** deals with objective, testable statements about "what is" (e.g., "A tax on cigarettes reduces smoking"). These can be proven true or false with data.
- **Normative economics** deals with value judgments about "what ought to be" (e.g., "The government should tax cigarettes more"). These cannot be proven true or false because they depend on opinion.

> **Exam tip:** Words like *should, ought, fair, better, worse* usually signal a normative statement. Words like *will, causes, increases, decreases* usually signal a positive statement.

### 1.1.2 Scarcity
**Scarcity** is the basic economic problem: human wants are unlimited, but the resources available to satisfy them are limited. Scarcity exists for every person and every nation, rich or poor. It is not the same as "shortage."

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Scarcity** | Permanent condition: limited resources vs. unlimited wants. Always exists. |
| **Shortage** | Temporary market condition: quantity demanded > quantity supplied at a given price. Can be fixed by price changes. |

Because of scarcity, every society must make choices, and every choice involves giving something up.

### 1.1.3 Needs vs. Wants
- **Needs:** goods and services required for survival (food, water, shelter, basic healthcare).
- **Wants:** goods and services we desire beyond survival; they are unlimited and keep changing as income and tastes change.
Economics treats both as demands on limited resources.

### 1.1.4 Economic Resources (Factors of Production)
Resources used to produce goods and services are called **factors of production**. There are four, each earning a specific payment (income):

| Factor | Definition | Examples | Payment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Land** | All natural resources (gifts of nature) | Soil, forests, water, oil, minerals, air | **Rent** |
| **Labor** | Human effort (physical and mental) used in production | Teachers, factory workers, doctors | **Wages** |
| **Capital** | Human-made goods used to produce other goods (physical capital) | Machines, tools, factories, computers, trucks | **Interest** |
| **Entrepreneurship** | Taking risk, organizing the other three factors, innovating | Founder starting a business | **Profit** |

Important points:
- **Capital in economics ≠ money.** Money (financial capital) is not a factor of production because it does not directly produce anything. Physical capital does.
- **Human capital** is the knowledge, skills and training of workers (education, experience). Investing in education increases human capital and productivity.
- **Consumer goods** are for immediate use (food, clothing). **Capital goods** are used to make other goods (a bakery's oven).
- Factors can be **specialized** (hard to move between uses) or **versatile**. This matters for the shape of the PPC (see 1.3).

### 1.1.5 The Three Basic Economic Questions
Every society, because of scarcity, must answer:
1. **What to produce?** (How many guns vs. butter, consumer vs. capital goods.)
2. **How to produce?** (Labor-intensive or capital-intensive methods; which technology.)
3. **For whom to produce?** (How output is distributed: by income, need, equal share, etc.)

Economic systems differ by how they answer these (see 1.2).

### 1.1.6 Opportunity Cost
**Opportunity cost** is the value of the **next best alternative** that is given up when a choice is made. It is the true cost of any decision.

Key points:
- It is **not** the sum of all alternatives given up; only the **single best** forgone alternative.
- Opportunity cost exists even when no money is paid. Spending 3 hours studying has an opportunity cost (e.g., the movie you could have watched).
- **Explicit costs** are out-of-pocket monetary payments (tuition, wages paid, rent paid).
- **Implicit costs** are the opportunity costs of using resources you already own (the salary you gave up to run your own business; the interest you could have earned on your own money). No cash leaves your pocket, but they are real costs.
- **Accounting cost = explicit costs only. Economic cost = explicit + implicit costs.** (This is used heavily in Unit 3.)
- **Trade-off:** giving up one thing to get another. Opportunity cost is the value of that trade-off.

**Example:** You have one free evening and ₹500. Your options ranked by value to you: (1) a movie (₹500), (2) dinner out (₹500), (3) buying a book (₹300). If you choose the movie, the opportunity cost is the **dinner** (the next best option), not the dinner and the book together.

### 1.1.7 Sunk Costs
A **sunk cost** is a cost already incurred that **cannot be recovered**. Rational decision makers **ignore sunk costs** because they do not change the future benefits or costs of a decision. Example: you buy a non-refundable ₹800 concert ticket but are very sick on the day. The ₹800 is gone whether you go or not; decide only on future costs and benefits.

### 1.1.8 Incentives
**Incentives** are rewards or penalties that motivate behavior. People respond to incentives. Higher price of a good → buy less; higher wage → more people want to work. Almost every model in microeconomics assumes people respond predictably to changes in incentives.

## 1.2 Resource Allocation and Economic Systems

### 1.2.1 What is an Economic System?
An **economic system** is the set of institutions and rules a society uses to decide **what, how and for whom** to produce. Systems differ in two main ways: who owns resources (private vs. government) and who makes decisions (individuals via markets vs. government planners).

### 1.2.2 Types of Economic Systems

**1. Traditional Economy**
- Decisions based on custom, culture, religion, and what was done in the past.
- Roles are inherited (a farmer's child becomes a farmer).
- Mostly subsistence agriculture, hunting, gathering; little change or growth.
- *Strength:* stable, predictable. *Weakness:* little innovation, low living standards, slow growth.

**2. Command (Centrally Planned) Economy**
- The government owns most resources and makes all major decisions (what, how, for whom), usually through central planning.
- *Strengths:* can mobilize resources toward national goals; can reduce income inequality; guarantees basic goods.
- *Weaknesses:* no price signals → inefficient allocation, shortages and surpluses; weak incentives; little innovation and consumer choice.
- Examples (historically): Soviet Union; today North Korea and Cuba are close to this model.

**3. Market (Capitalist / Free Market) Economy**
- Resources are **privately owned**; decisions are made by individuals and firms through **voluntary exchange** in markets. Prices coordinate everything.
- Key features: private property rights, self-interest, competition, freedom of enterprise and choice, limited government role, prices as signals.
- **Adam Smith's "Invisible Hand":** individuals pursuing their own self-interest unintentionally promote the society's overall good through the market.
- *Strengths:* efficiency, innovation, wide variety, rewards for productivity, responds quickly to consumers.
- *Weaknesses:* inequality, market failures (externalities, public goods), no care for those who cannot pay, possible monopolies, instability.

**4. Mixed Economy**
- Combines market decision-making with **government intervention** (regulation, taxes, public goods, safety nets). **All real-world economies are mixed**; they differ only in degree (the US leans more market; India has a larger government role; Sweden has a bigger welfare state).

| Feature | Traditional | Command | Market | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides? | Custom | Government | Individuals/firms | Both |
| Ownership | Often community | Mostly government | Private | Both |
| Efficiency | Low | Often low | High | Moderate–High |
| Equity | Varies | Often high (in theory) | Often lower | Moderate |
| Innovation | Low | Low | High | High |

### 1.2.3 Role of Property Rights and Prices
- **Property rights** (legal right to own, use, sell resources) give people the incentive to produce, save and invest; without them people would not invest or innovate.
- **Prices** act as **signals** (tell producers what consumers want), **incentives** (reward efficient producers), and **rationing devices** (decide who gets scarce goods).

### 1.2.4 Circular Flow Model
A simple model of how resources, goods and money move between households and firms.
- **Households** own the factors of production and are the consumers.
- **Firms** produce goods and services and hire factors.
- **Product (goods) market:** households buy goods from firms. Money flows to firms (revenue); goods flow to households.
- **Factor (resource) market:** firms hire land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship from households. Money flows to households (rent, wages, interest, profit); factors flow to firms.
- The model shows that one person's spending is another's income. Extended versions add government (taxes, transfers) and the foreign sector (exports, imports).

### 1.2.5 Efficiency vs. Equity
- **Productive efficiency:** producing at the **lowest possible cost** (on the PPC; minimum average total cost).
- **Allocative efficiency:** producing the combination of goods that society values most; **marginal benefit = marginal cost** (price = marginal cost in perfect competition).
- **Equity:** fairness in the distribution of output and income. There is often a **trade-off** between efficiency and equity (more redistribution may reduce incentives).

## 1.3 Production Possibilities Curve (PPC / PPF)

### 1.3.1 Definition and Purpose
The **Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)** is a graph showing the **maximum combinations of two goods** that an economy can produce when it uses **all its resources fully and efficiently**, given **its current technology and resources**.

It illustrates **scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency, and economic growth**.

**Assumptions of the model:**
1. Only two goods are produced.
2. Resources are fixed in quantity and quality (in the short run).
3. Technology is fixed.
4. Resources are fully employed (for points on the curve).

### 1.3.2 Reading Points on the PPC

| Location | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **On the curve** | Efficient. Full employment of all resources; **productive efficiency**. Producing more of one good requires producing less of the other. |
| **Inside the curve** | **Inefficient** or underutilization: unemployment, idle capacity, misallocated resources (e.g., a recession). Can produce more of both goods. |
| **Outside the curve** | **Unattainable** with current resources and technology. Becomes attainable only through growth (or trade). |

Moving from a point **inside** the curve **to the curve** costs nothing in terms of opportunity cost (more of one or both goods is gained). Moving **along** the curve always involves a trade-off.

### 1.3.3 Opportunity Cost on the PPC
The opportunity cost of one more unit of Good X = the units of Good Y given up (the **slope** of the PPC).

**Calculation (numerical example):**

| Point | Corn (tons) | Cars (units) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 100 |
| B | 20 | 90 |
| C | 40 | 70 |
| D | 60 | 40 |
| E | 80 | 0 |

- A → B: 20 tons of corn cost 10 cars → opportunity cost of 1 ton of corn = 0.5 car.
- B → C: 20 tons cost 20 cars → 1 car per ton.
- C → D: 20 tons cost 30 cars → 1.5 cars per ton.
- D → E: 20 tons cost 40 cars → 2 cars per ton.
Opportunity cost **rises** as more corn is produced → **law of increasing opportunity cost**.

### 1.3.4 Shapes of the PPC

**(a) Bowed-out (concave to the origin): increasing opportunity cost**
- Occurs because resources are **not perfectly adaptable** (specialized). Resources best suited to making cars (skilled auto workers, car factories) are poor at growing corn. When shifting resources, the first moved are the ones best suited to the new good; later ones are less suited and give up more of the first good for each unit gained.
- This is the **most common shape** in AP exam problems unless stated otherwise.

**(b) Straight line: constant opportunity cost**
- Occurs when resources are **perfectly substitutable** between the two goods. The slope is constant, so the opportunity cost is the same everywhere.

**(c) Bowed-in (convex): decreasing opportunity cost**
- Rare; indicates economies of scale/specialization benefits. Not usually tested.

### 1.3.5 Shifts of the PPC (Economic Growth and Decline)
A PPC **shifts outward** (economic growth) when:
1. **Increase in quantity of resources** (more labor through immigration or population growth; discovery of natural resources; more capital through investment).
2. **Increase in quality of resources** (education and training = human capital).
3. **Technological advancement** (new methods raise output per resource).

A PPC **shifts inward** (contraction) when resources are lost or destroyed: war, natural disaster, pandemic with lasting loss of labor, depletion of resources.

**Types of shifts:**
- **Parallel (both goods) outward shift:** a change that improves production of both goods (e.g., increase in labor force; general technology).
- **Pivot (one good only):** technology or resource that helps only one good. The intercept for that good moves; the other stays unchanged. (E.g., a new tractor technology increases corn output at the corn-axis intercept, while the car intercept stays the same.)

**What does NOT shift the PPC:**
- Moving along the curve (changing the mix of goods).
- Unemployment or recession → moves the economy **inside** the curve (does not shift the curve).
- Increase in demand or a change in tastes.
- A change in prices.

### 1.3.6 Economic Growth and Trade-offs Over Time
- Economies that devote more resources to **capital goods** today will have a **greater outward shift** in the future (they sacrifice current consumption for future growth). Economies that produce mostly **consumer goods** now grow more slowly.
- Example: Country A chooses 90% capital goods; Country B chooses 90% consumer goods. In 20 years, A's PPC is farther out.
- **Opportunity cost of growth:** current consumption given up.

### 1.3.7 Common Tests of the PPC on the AP Exam
- Identify the opportunity cost from a table or graph.
- Identify points as efficient/inefficient/unattainable.
- Determine the effect of an event (e.g., natural disaster, new technology, immigration, recession) on the PPC or on the economy's position.
- Compare shapes (straight vs. bowed-out) and explain why.

## 1.4 Comparative Advantage and Trade

### 1.4.1 Specialization and Trade
**Specialization:** producing a narrower range of goods than one consumes (concentrating on what you do best). **Trade:** voluntary exchange of goods or services. Specialization + trade lets people and countries **consume beyond their own PPC**.

### 1.4.2 Absolute Advantage
A producer has an **absolute advantage** if it can produce **more output with the same resources** (or the same output with **fewer** resources) than another producer. It is based on **productivity**.

### 1.4.3 Comparative Advantage
A producer has a **comparative advantage** if it can produce a good at a **lower opportunity cost** than another producer. **Trade is based on comparative advantage, NOT absolute advantage.** A country can have an absolute advantage in everything and still gain from trade because it cannot have a comparative advantage in everything.

### 1.4.4 How to Calculate Opportunity Cost

**Method 1: Output (what can be produced in the same time/with the same resources)**
Opportunity cost of Good A = (Max units of Good B) ÷ (Max units of Good A).

**Method 2: Input (time required per unit)**
Opportunity cost of Good A = (Time/units of input to make A) ÷ (Time/units of input to make B).

**Rule:** the producer with the **lower opportunity cost** has the comparative advantage in that good.

### 1.4.5 Worked Example (Output Method)
In one hour, assume:

| | Phones | Cars |
|---|---|---|
| **Country X** | 60 | 40 |
| **Country Y** | 20 | 20 |

**Absolute advantage:** X has an absolute advantage in **both** (60 > 20 phones; 40 > 20 cars).

**Opportunity costs:**

| | 1 Phone costs | 1 Car costs |
|---|---|---|
| **X** | 40/60 = **2/3 car** | 60/40 = **1.5 phones** |
| **Y** | 20/20 = **1 car** | 20/20 = **1 phone** |

**Comparative advantage:** X has the lower opportunity cost of a phone (2/3 < 1) → X specializes in **phones**. Y has the lower opportunity cost of a car (1 < 1.5) → Y specializes in **cars**.

### 1.4.6 Terms of Trade
The **terms of trade** is the exchange ratio at which two parties trade. For **both** to gain, the ratio must lie **between** the two opportunity costs.
- Price of 1 car (in phones) must be **between 1 and 1.5 phones**.
- Equivalently, price of 1 phone (in cars) must be **between 2/3 and 1 car**.
If the ratio equals one party's opportunity cost, that party gains nothing; outside the range, one party loses and will refuse to trade.

### 1.4.7 Showing Gains from Trade
Assume each country uses all its resources in one hour: after specialization, X makes 60 phones and Y makes 20 cars. They trade at 1.25 phones per car: **Y gives 8 cars, X gives 10 phones.**
- **X** ends with 50 phones + 8 cars. Without trade, to make 8 cars X would give up 12 phones (8 × 1.5), leaving 48 phones. Consuming **50 phones + 8 cars lies outside its PPC** → gain.
- **Y** ends with 12 cars + 10 phones = 22 units. Y's own PPC is 20 units total (1:1 trade-off), so 22 lies **outside** its PPC → gain.
Both countries now consume at points that were **unattainable** without trade.

### 1.4.8 Key Points on Trade
- Gains come from **lower opportunity cost** specialization, not from being "better at everything."
- Trade allows consumption **outside the PPC**, but trade does **not** shift the PPC itself.
- Larger gains occur when the **difference** in opportunity costs is **larger**.
- Costs: some domestic industries/workers are hurt (job losses), transition costs, dependence on others.
- With **constant opportunity cost**, complete specialization is possible. With **increasing opportunity cost**, specialization is usually partial.
- If opportunity costs are equal, there is no comparative advantage and no gains from trade.

### 1.4.9 Common Mistakes
1. Confusing absolute and comparative advantage.
2. Using output numbers directly instead of calculating opportunity cost.
3. Mixing the direction: in an **input (time)** table a **lower** number of hours = more productive, but the lower **opportunity cost** decides comparative advantage.
4. Setting the terms of trade outside the range.

## 1.5 Cost-Benefit Analysis and Marginal Analysis

### 1.5.1 Rational Behavior
Economists assume decision makers are **rational**: they weigh costs and benefits and choose the option that maximizes their satisfaction (consumers: utility; firms: profit; government: social welfare).

### 1.5.2 Marginal vs. Total
- **Marginal** means the **additional** or **extra** (the change from one more unit).
- **Total** means the sum of everything.
Decisions are made **at the margin**: "Should I do one more?" not "Should I do all or nothing?"

### 1.5.3 Marginal Benefit (MB) and Marginal Cost (MC)
- **Marginal Benefit (MB):** the additional benefit from one more unit. Generally **decreases** as more is consumed (**diminishing marginal benefit**; the 5th slice of pizza gives less satisfaction than the 1st).
- **Marginal Cost (MC):** the additional cost of one more unit. Generally **increases** as more is produced (due to increasing opportunity costs).

### 1.5.4 The Decision Rule
- If **MB > MC** → do more (net benefit is positive).
- If **MB < MC** → do less (net benefit is negative).
- **Optimal quantity:** do the activity until **MB = MC**. Net benefit is maximized here.

**Example:** Studying: hour 1 gives +20 marks, hour 2 +15, hour 3 +10, hour 4 +5. If each hour costs the equivalent of 8 marks (what you would earn elsewhere), study 3 hours (hour 3: 10 > 8) but stop before hour 4 (5 < 8).

### 1.5.5 Net Benefit and Total Analysis
**Net benefit = Total benefit − Total cost.** It is maximized where MB = MC. Check by calculating net benefit for each quantity and choosing the highest.

### 1.5.6 Cost-Benefit Analysis for Individuals, Firms and Governments
- **Individuals:** how many hours to work, whether to go to college.
- **Firms:** how many workers to hire, how many units to produce (profit maximized at MR = MC).
- **Governments:** whether to build a bridge, how much pollution to allow. Compare total social benefits and costs.

### 1.5.7 Role of Sunk Costs and Opportunity Cost
- Include **implicit costs** and **opportunity costs**.
- **Ignore sunk costs** (already paid and unrecoverable).
- Use **marginal** (future, additional) costs and benefits only.

## Unit 1 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- Opportunity cost (of Good A in terms of Good B) = Units of B given up ÷ Units of A gained.
- Economic cost = Explicit cost + Implicit cost.
- Net benefit = Total benefit − Total cost.
- Optimal decision: **MB = MC**.
- Terms of trade range: between the two countries' opportunity costs.

**Key terms glossary:** scarcity, shortage, factors of production, land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship, human capital, opportunity cost, explicit cost, implicit cost, sunk cost, incentive, trade-off, positive economics, normative economics, traditional / command / market / mixed economy, invisible hand, property rights, circular flow, productive efficiency, allocative efficiency, equity, PPC, law of increasing opportunity cost, economic growth, absolute advantage, comparative advantage, specialization, terms of trade, marginal benefit, marginal cost, net benefit.

**Top exam traps**
1. Scarcity ≠ shortage.
2. Capital ≠ money.
3. Opportunity cost = the **next best** option only.
4. Unemployment moves you **inside** the PPC; it does not shift it.
5. Comparative advantage = **lowest opportunity cost**; terms of trade lie **between** the opportunity costs.
6. Ignore sunk costs; include implicit costs.

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** Which of the following best describes scarcity?
(A) A temporary shortage of a good (B) Limited resources relative to unlimited wants (C) High prices for goods (D) A lack of money
**Answer: B.**

**2.** The opportunity cost of attending a concert that costs ₹1,000, instead of working 4 hours at ₹200/hr, is best measured as:
(A) ₹1,000 (B) ₹800 (C) The value of the next best alternative, including the forgone wages (D) ₹200
**Answer: C** (explicit ₹1,000 plus the forgone ₹800 wages, i.e., the next best use of that money and time).

**3.** A bowed-out PPC illustrates:
(A) Constant opportunity cost (B) Increasing opportunity cost (C) Decreasing opportunity cost (D) Unemployment
**Answer: B.**

**4.** Which event shifts a country's PPC outward?
(A) A recession (B) A technological advance (C) An increase in consumer demand (D) A rise in unemployment
**Answer: B.**

**5.** In one day, Maya can make 12 shirts or 6 bags; Ravi can make 8 shirts or 2 bags. Who has the comparative advantage in bags?
(A) Maya (B) Ravi (C) Neither (D) Both
**Answer: A.** Maya's opportunity cost of a bag = 12/6 = 2 shirts; Ravi's = 8/2 = 4 shirts. Maya's is lower.

**6.** A rational firm should expand production until:
(A) Total benefit is highest (B) MB = MC (C) MC = 0 (D) Total cost is lowest
**Answer: B.**

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** Country A can produce 30 tons of wheat or 15 tons of rice; Country B can produce 20 tons of wheat or 20 tons of rice (using all resources).
(a) Calculate the opportunity cost of 1 ton of rice for each country.
(b) Which country has the comparative advantage in rice? In wheat?
(c) Give a terms of trade (wheat per rice) that benefits both.
(d) Explain why the PPC does not shift after trade.

**Answer:**
(a) A: 30/15 = **2 wheat**; B: 20/20 = **1 wheat**.
(b) Rice: **B** (1 < 2). Wheat: opportunity cost of 1 wheat: A = 0.5 rice, B = 1 rice → **A**.
(c) Between 1 and 2 wheat per rice, for example **1.5 wheat per ton of rice**.
(d) Trade lets countries **consume** outside their PPC, but it does not change resources or technology, which determine the PPC's position.

**Q2.** A city is deciding how many hours to keep a public library open. The marginal benefit of the 8th hour is ₹400 and its marginal cost is ₹300; the 9th hour has MB ₹250 and MC ₹320.
(a) Should the library stay open for the 8th and 9th hour?
(b) What is the optimal number of hours?

**Answer:** (a) The 8th hour: yes (MB > MC). The 9th hour: no (MB < MC). (b) **8 hours**, the last hour where MB ≥ MC.
