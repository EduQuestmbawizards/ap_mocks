## 5.1 Introduction to Factor Markets

### 5.1.1 Product Markets vs. Factor Markets
Until now we studied **product (goods) markets**, where **firms are sellers** and **households are buyers**. In **factor (resource) markets**, the roles reverse:
- **Households (workers, landowners, savers, entrepreneurs) are the SELLERS** of factors of production.
- **Firms are the BUYERS (demanders)** of factors.

| Factor | Payment | Factor market |
|---|---|---|
| Labor | **Wage** | Labor market |
| Land (natural resources) | **Rent** | Land market |
| Capital | **Interest** (or rental price) | Capital market |
| Entrepreneurship | **Profit** | — |

The AP exam focuses mainly on the **labor market**; the logic applies to every factor.

### 5.1.2 Derived Demand
**Demand for a factor is a derived demand**: firms demand labor, machinery, or land **not for their own sake**, but because they help produce goods and services that **consumers** want. If demand for the product rises, the demand for the inputs used to make it rises too (e.g., more demand for cars → more demand for auto workers and steel).

### 5.1.3 Marginal Revenue Product (MRP)
**Marginal Revenue Product (MRP)** = the **additional revenue** a firm earns from hiring **one more unit of a factor** (e.g., one more worker).

**MRP = MP × MR** (marginal product of the factor × marginal revenue from the product)
Also: **MRP = ΔTR ÷ ΔL.**

- In a **perfectly competitive product market**, MR = P, so **MRP = MP × P**. This special case is called the **Value of Marginal Product (VMP)**.
- In an **imperfectly competitive product market** (monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly), **MR < P**, so **MRP < VMP** (MRP = MP × MR is smaller). Such firms hire **less labor** than competitive firms.

### 5.1.4 The Firm's Demand for a Factor = MRP Curve
Because of **diminishing marginal returns**, MP falls as more labor is hired → **MRP falls** → **the MRP curve slopes downward**. A firm's **demand curve for labor is its MRP curve** (above the point where it covers the wage).

### 5.1.5 Marginal Resource Cost (MRC)
**Marginal Resource Cost (MRC)** (also called **marginal factor cost, MFC**) = the **additional cost** of hiring one more unit of the factor = **ΔTotal cost of the factor ÷ ΔQuantity of the factor.** For labor, it is the **marginal cost of labor**.

### 5.1.6 The Profit-Maximizing Hiring Rule
**Hire workers up to the point where MRP = MRC.**
- If **MRP > MRC**: hiring one more worker adds more revenue than cost → hire more.
- If **MRP < MRC**: the worker adds more cost than revenue → hire fewer.

This is the factor-market version of **MR = MC**.

## 5.2 Changes in Factor Demand and Factor Supply

### 5.2.1 Determinants (Shifters) of Labor Demand (the MRP curve shifts)
1. **Change in the demand for (price of) the product:** higher product price (higher demand for the product) → MRP rises → labor demand shifts **right**.
2. **Change in productivity (MP) of labor:** better **technology, education/training (human capital)**, or **more capital** raises MP → MRP rises → labor demand shifts **right**.
3. **Changes in the prices of other resources:**
   - **Substitute inputs** (e.g., labor and robots): if the price of the substitute **falls**, firms may use more of it and **less labor** (substitution effect), but the lower costs may lead to higher output (**output effect**, which raises labor demand). **The net effect depends on which effect is larger**; if the question does not say, explain both effects.
   - **Complementary inputs** (e.g., labor and machines used together): if the price of the complement **falls**, firms use more of it and **demand for labor rises**.
4. **Number of firms (buyers)** in the market: more firms → market labor demand shifts right.

> **Remember:** a change in the **wage itself** causes a **movement along** the labor demand curve (change in quantity of labor demanded), **not** a shift.

### 5.2.2 Elasticity of Labor Demand
Labor demand is **more elastic** when:
- The **product demand** is more elastic (more price sensitivity in output).
- **Substitutes** for labor are easily available.
- **Labor cost is a large share** of total cost.
- The time horizon is **longer** (firms can adjust technology).

### 5.2.3 Labor Supply
The **labor supply curve** slopes **upward**: a higher wage attracts more workers (more people are willing to work, and existing workers supply more hours), because the **opportunity cost of leisure** rises.
*(A backward-bending individual labor supply can occur at very high wages because of the income effect, but the market supply still generally slopes upward.)*

### 5.2.4 Determinants (Shifters) of Labor Supply
1. **Number of qualified workers** (population growth, immigration, labor-force participation) → more workers shift supply **right**.
2. **Wages (and conditions) in alternative jobs:** if wages rise in other industries, workers leave → supply **left** in this one.
3. **Training/education requirements and costs:** greater training required → supply **left**.
4. **Non-wage job attributes:** benefits, safety, location, flexibility.
5. **Preferences for leisure/work, culture, social norms.**
6. **Government policies:** taxes on labor income, welfare payments, licensing requirements, work-visa policy.

### 5.2.5 Equilibrium in a Perfectly Competitive Labor Market
Market **labor demand (sum of all firms' MRP curves)** and **market labor supply** determine the **equilibrium wage (W\*)** and **equilibrium quantity of labor (L\*)**.
- **Shortage of labor** at wages below W\* → wages bid up.
- **Surplus of labor (unemployment)** at wages above W\* → downward pressure on wages.
- **Shifts:** demand ↑ → W ↑, L ↑; demand ↓ → W ↓, L ↓; supply ↑ → W ↓, L ↑; supply ↓ → W ↑, L ↓. If both shift, one of the changes is indeterminate (same logic as Unit 2).

### 5.2.6 Minimum Wage in a Competitive Labor Market
A **minimum wage above equilibrium** acts like a **price floor**: the quantity of labor supplied exceeds the quantity demanded → **unemployment (surplus of labor)**; workers who keep jobs earn more; DWL arises. A minimum wage **below equilibrium** has no effect (not binding).

### 5.2.7 Why Do Wages Differ? (Wage Differentials)
- **Human capital:** education, skills and experience raise MRP and wages.
- **Compensating differentials:** higher wages paid for **unpleasant, dangerous or risky** jobs.
- **Differences in productivity/MRP** across workers and industries.
- **Labor-market power** (unions, monopsony), **discrimination** and **licensing barriers.**

## 5.3 Profit-Maximizing Behavior in Perfectly Competitive Factor Markets

### 5.3.1 A Firm in a Perfectly Competitive Labor Market
A firm hiring in a **perfectly competitive labor market** is a **wage taker**: it can hire **any number of workers at the going market wage (W)** because it is only a small buyer.
- The **labor supply curve facing the firm is horizontal at W.**
- **MRC = W** (hiring one more worker costs exactly the market wage).
- **Hiring rule: hire until MRP = W.**

### 5.3.2 Worked Example (Competitive Product Market and Competitive Labor Market)
Product price = ₹10; wage = ₹50 per day.

| Workers (L) | MP (units) | MRP = MP × ₹10 | Wage (MRC) | MRP − MRC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12 | ₹120 | ₹50 | +70 |
| 2 | 10 | ₹100 | ₹50 | +50 |
| 3 | 8 | ₹80 | ₹50 | +30 |
| 4 | 6 | ₹60 | ₹50 | +10 |
| 5 | 4 | ₹40 | ₹50 | −10 |
| 6 | 2 | ₹20 | ₹50 | −30 |

**Hire 4 workers.** The 4th worker still adds ₹60 of revenue for ₹50 cost; the 5th adds only ₹40 for ₹50 cost.
- **If the wage rises to ₹70:** hire only **3** workers (MRP of the 3rd = 80 ≥ 70; the 4th = 60 < 70) → movement along the demand curve.
- **If the product price rises to ₹15:** MRP of the 5th worker = 4 × 15 = ₹60 ≥ 50 → hire **5** workers (labor demand shifts right).

**When product market is imperfect:** if MR is ₹6 (price ₹8) instead of ₹10, MRP of the 4th worker = 6 × 6 = ₹36 < ₹50 → hire fewer workers than the competitive firm.

### 5.3.3 Hiring More Than One Input: Least-Cost and Profit-Maximizing Combination
Firms use several inputs (labor L and capital K).

**Least-cost rule:** to produce a given output at the lowest cost, use the combination where the **marginal product per rupee spent is equal across inputs**:

**MP_L ÷ P_L = MP_K ÷ P_K**

*Example:* MP_L = 20, wage = ₹10 → 2 units per rupee; MP_K = 30, rental price = ₹20 → 1.5 per rupee. Labor gives **more output per rupee** → **use more labor and less capital** until the ratios equalize (as labor increases, MP_L falls).

**Profit-maximizing rule:** hire each input until **MRP of the input = its price**; for several inputs:

**MRP_L ÷ P_L = MRP_K ÷ P_K = 1**

(If the product market is perfectly competitive, MRP = MP × P.)

### 5.3.4 Other Factors: Capital and Land
- **Capital:** a firm hires/uses capital until **MRP_K = price of capital** (the rental rate or the interest cost of funds). **Lower interest rates** → more capital demanded (investment rises).
- **Land:** a firm rents land until MRP of land = rent. When the supply of land is **perfectly inelastic** (fixed), the payment is **economic rent**, determined solely by **demand**: higher demand for the land's output → higher rent. **Economic rent** = payment to a factor above the minimum needed to bring it into supply.

### 5.3.5 Market vs. Firm
- The **firm** takes the wage as given.
- The **market** wage is determined by market labor demand and supply.
- **Labor-market efficiency:** in a perfectly competitive labor market with perfectly competitive product markets, workers are paid their **marginal revenue product = value of marginal product** → resources are allocated efficiently.

## 5.4 Monopsony

### 5.4.1 Definition
A **monopsony** is a market with a **single buyer** of a factor (usually labor) — the buyer has **market power over the wage**. In general, a firm with market power as a buyer is a **wage (price) maker**.
*Examples:* a "company town" with one major employer, a large hospital hiring nurses in a small region, a big retailer in a small town, a professional sports league hiring players.
(Compare: **monopoly = single seller; monopsony = single buyer.**)

### 5.4.2 Why MRC > Wage
The monopsonist faces the **entire upward-sloping market labor supply curve**. To hire **one more** worker, it must **raise the wage**, and (typically) it must pay **that higher wage to all existing workers too**.
- The **cost of the extra worker = her wage + the raises given to all current workers.**
- So **MRC > wage** at every quantity; **the MRC curve lies above the labor supply curve** (and is steeper).

### 5.4.3 Profit-Maximizing Hiring for a Monopsonist
**Step 1:** Hire the quantity where **MRP = MRC** → quantity L\*.
**Step 2:** Pay the wage given by the **labor supply curve at L\*** (the lowest wage needed to attract L\* workers) → W\*.

> **The wage is read from the SUPPLY curve, not from MRC.** (Parallel to a monopolist reading the price from the demand curve.)

### 5.4.4 Numerical Example
- Labor supply (marginal worker's wage): **W = 10 + 2L**
- Total labor cost = W × L = 10L + 2L² → **MRC = 10 + 4L**
- Labor demand: **MRP = 58 − 2L**

**Competitive labor market (benchmark):** Supply = Demand: 10 + 2L = 58 − 2L → 4L = 48 → **L = 12, W = ₹34.**

**Monopsony:** MRP = MRC: 58 − 2L = 10 + 4L → 6L = 48 → **L = 8**; wage from supply: W = 10 + 2(8) = **₹26**.
- At L = 8, **MRP = 58 − 16 = ₹42**, so the workers' wage (₹26) is **₹16 below their MRP** (this gap is **exploitation**/monopsonistic markdown).

| | Competitive market | Monopsony |
|---|---|---|
| Employment | 12 | **8 (lower)** |
| Wage | ₹34 | **₹26 (lower)** |
| Wage vs. MRP | W = MRP | **W < MRP** |

**Deadweight loss:** the triangle between the supply and MRP (demand) curves for the units from L = 8 to L = 12 — workers who would be hired in a competitive market are not hired. **Allocatively inefficient** (MRP > wage at L = 8: the value of the extra output exceeds the worker's supply price).

### 5.4.5 Minimum Wage and Monopsony
**Key insight:** a **minimum wage can increase employment** in a monopsonistic labor market (unlike a competitive one).
- A minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage makes the **MRC flat at the minimum wage** (the firm no longer has to raise wages for existing workers to hire more) up to the point where the minimum wage meets the supply curve.
- *Example:* a minimum wage of **₹30** → the firm hires up to L = 10 (supply at ₹30: 10 + 2L = 30 → L = 10) since MRP at L = 10 (₹38) exceeds ₹30 → **employment rises from 8 to 10.**
- A minimum wage at the **competitive level (₹34)** → employment is **maximized at 12**.
- A minimum wage **above** the competitive wage (e.g., ₹42) → employment falls (MRP = 42 at L = 8), and a labor surplus appears (supply 16 vs. demand 8).

### 5.4.6 Monopsony vs. Imperfect Product Market
Do not confuse them:
- **Monopsony:** market power in the **factor (buying) market** → pays **W < MRP**, hires **fewer** than competitive.
- **Monopoly/imperfect product market:** market power in the **output (selling) market** → MR < P → MRP is lower → hires **fewer** workers, but pays the wage determined by a competitive labor market (if the labor market is competitive).
- A firm can be **both** a monopolist (product market) and a monopsonist (labor market): MRP = MP × MR and MRC > W → it hires even fewer workers and pays even lower wages.

### 5.4.7 Unions (Bilateral Monopoly) — Brief Note
When a **union** (monopoly seller of labor) faces a monopsonist, the wage and employment outcome is indeterminate and depends on bargaining power; a union can sometimes raise both wages and employment up to the competitive level.

## Unit 5 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- MRP = MP × MR (competitive product market: MRP = MP × P = VMP).
- MRC = ΔTotal factor cost ÷ ΔQuantity. Competitive factor market: MRC = W.
- **Hire where MRP = MRC.**
- Least-cost: MP_L/P_L = MP_K/P_K. Profit-max: MRP_L/P_L = MRP_K/P_K = 1.
- Monopsony: hire where MRP = MRC; **wage from the supply curve.**

**Key terms glossary:** factor market, derived demand, marginal revenue product (MRP), value of marginal product (VMP), marginal resource cost (MRC), wage taker, substitution effect/output effect (input), human capital, compensating differential, economic rent, monopsony, minimum wage, labor union.

**Top exam traps**
1. **Households SELL, firms BUY** in factor markets.
2. The labor demand curve is the **MRP curve**; a change in the **wage** moves along it.
3. Product price changes shift labor demand (via MRP).
4. A **monopsonist's wage comes from the supply curve** and is **below MRP**.
5. A minimum wage creates **unemployment in a competitive market** but can **raise employment in monopsony**.
6. Don't mix up **monopsony (one buyer)** and **monopoly (one seller)**.
7. In multi-input problems, compare **MP per rupee** (not MP alone).

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** The demand for labor is a derived demand because it depends on:
(A) The wage rate (B) The demand for the product the labor produces (C) Labor supply (D) The minimum wage
**Answer: B.**

**2.** A firm in a perfectly competitive labor market should hire workers until:
(A) MP = W (B) MRP = W (C) MR = MC of output (D) W = 0
**Answer: B.**

**3.** Which of the following shifts a firm's labor demand curve to the right?
(A) A decrease in the wage (B) An increase in the price of the product (C) An increase in the number of workers available (D) A decrease in labor productivity
**Answer: B.**

**4.** A monopsonist hiring labor will hire where MRP = MRC and pay a wage that is:
(A) Equal to MRC (B) Equal to MRP (C) Read from the labor supply curve, below MRP (D) Above MRP
**Answer: C.**

**5.** Compared with a competitive labor market, a monopsony hires:
(A) More workers at a higher wage (B) Fewer workers at a lower wage (C) The same workers at a lower wage (D) Fewer workers at a higher wage
**Answer: B.**

**6.** A firm's MP of labor is 10 and the price of the product is ₹6 (competitive product market). The wage is ₹50. The firm should:
(A) Hire more labor (B) Hire less labor (C) Keep hiring unchanged (D) Shut down
**Answer: A.** MRP = 10 × 6 = ₹60 > ₹50.

**7.** Last worker's MP_L/w = 4; last machine's MP_K/r = 6. To minimize cost the firm should:
(A) Use more labor and less capital (B) Use more capital and less labor (C) Change nothing (D) Cut output
**Answer: B.**

**8.** A binding minimum wage in a competitive labor market will cause:
(A) A shortage of workers (B) Unemployment (C) Higher employment (D) No change
**Answer: B.**

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** A firm sells its output in a perfectly competitive market at ₹20 per unit and hires labor in a perfectly competitive labor market at ₹100 per hour. The marginal product of the 5th, 6th, and 7th hours is 6, 5, and 4 units.
(a) Calculate MRP for the 5th, 6th and 7th hours.
(b) Assuming the MRP of the first four hours is above ₹100, how many hours should the firm hire?
(c) What happens to the firm's demand for labor if the price of output rises to ₹30?

**Answer:**
(a) MRP = MP × P: 5th = 6 × 20 = **₹120**; 6th = 5 × 20 = **₹100**; 7th = 4 × 20 = **₹80**.
(b) Hire while MRP ≥ ₹100: 5th (120) yes; 6th (100 = wage) yes (indifferent); 7th (80) no → **6 hours** (5 if the firm hires only when MRP strictly exceeds the wage).
(c) **Labor demand increases** (MRP curve shifts right) → at ₹30: 7th hour MRP = 4 × 30 = ₹120 > ₹100 → hires more.

**Q2.** A town has one large employer (a monopsonist) of nurses. Labor supply is W = 10 + 2L; MRC = 10 + 4L; MRP = 58 − 2L.
(a) Find employment and wage.
(b) Compare with the competitive outcome.
(c) Explain the effect of a ₹30 minimum wage.

**Answer:**
(a) 58 − 2L = 10 + 4L → **L = 8**, **W = 10 + 16 = ₹26**.
(b) Competitive: 10 + 2L = 58 − 2L → **L = 12, W = ₹34.** The monopsony hires fewer workers (8 vs. 12) at a lower wage (₹26 vs. ₹34); MRP at L = 8 is ₹42 > ₹26.
(c) A ₹30 minimum wage makes MRC flat at ₹30 until labor supply reaches L = 10, so the firm hires up to **L = 10** (MRP = 38 > 30): **employment rises** from 8 to 10 and wage rises to ₹30.
