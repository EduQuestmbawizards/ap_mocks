## 2.1 Demand

### 2.1.1 Definitions
- **Market:** any arrangement where buyers and sellers interact to exchange goods, services or resources.
- **Demand:** the relationship between the **price** of a good and the **quantity demanded** by consumers over a given period, holding everything else constant (**ceteris paribus**). Demand is the **whole schedule/curve**, not a single number.
- **Quantity demanded (Qd):** the amount consumers are willing and able to buy at **one specific price**. "Willing and able" matters: wanting something without the ability to pay is not demand.
- **Demand schedule:** a table of prices and quantities demanded. **Demand curve:** the graph of that schedule (price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis).

### 2.1.2 The Law of Demand
There is an **inverse** relationship between price and quantity demanded, ceteris paribus. As price rises, Qd falls; as price falls, Qd rises. The demand curve **slopes downward**.

**Why does the demand curve slope downward?**
1. **Substitution effect:** when a good's price rises, it becomes more expensive relative to substitutes, so consumers switch to other goods.
2. **Income (purchasing-power) effect:** when a good's price rises, the consumer's real purchasing power falls, so they can afford less of it.
3. **Diminishing marginal utility:** each additional unit gives less satisfaction, so consumers will pay less for additional units.

### 2.1.3 Market Demand
**Market demand** is the **horizontal sum** of all individual demand curves: at each price, add up every consumer's quantity demanded. More consumers → market demand shifts right.

### 2.1.4 Change in Quantity Demanded vs. Change in Demand

| | Change in **quantity demanded** | Change in **demand** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | A change in the good's **own price only** | A change in a **non-price determinant** |
| Graph | **Movement along** the same demand curve | **Shift** of the whole demand curve |
| Direction | Price ↑ → move up-left; Price ↓ → move down-right | Increase = shift **right**; Decrease = shift **left** |

> **Most common exam error:** saying "demand decreases" when only the price rose. A price change never shifts the curve of that same good.

### 2.1.5 Determinants of Demand (Shifters)
Memory aid: **I-T-E-N-S-P** (Income, Tastes, Expectations, Number of buyers, Substitutes/complements – Prices of related goods).

1. **Consumer income**
   - **Normal good:** demand rises when income rises (restaurant meals, branded clothing).
   - **Inferior good:** demand **falls** when income rises (instant noodles, used cars, bus travel).
2. **Tastes and preferences:** trends, advertising, health news. A favorable change shifts demand right.
3. **Consumer expectations:** about **future prices** (expected price rise → buy now → demand rises today) and **future income**.
4. **Number of buyers:** population growth, immigration, new market segments → demand rises.
5. **Prices of related goods:**
   - **Substitutes** (goods used in place of each other, e.g., tea and coffee). If the price of a substitute **rises**, demand for this good **rises**.
   - **Complements** (goods used together, e.g., cars and petrol). If the price of a complement **rises**, demand for this good **falls**.

**Direction summary:** a *favorable* change (more income for normal goods, better tastes, more buyers, higher price of a substitute, lower price of a complement, expected future price increase) → **demand shifts right (increase)**. The opposite → shifts left.

### 2.1.6 Exam Tips on Demand
- Always label axes (**Price**, **Quantity**) and the curves (**D, D₁, D₂**).
- Consider the exact words: "an increase in the price of beef" → affects demand for **chicken** (substitute), not beef's own demand curve.

## 2.2 Supply

### 2.2.1 Definitions
- **Supply:** the relationship between the **price** of a good and the **quantity supplied** by producers during a time period, ceteris paribus.
- **Quantity supplied (Qs):** the amount sellers are **willing and able** to sell at **one specific price**.

### 2.2.2 The Law of Supply
There is a **direct (positive)** relationship between price and quantity supplied. The supply curve **slopes upward**.

**Why?**
1. **Higher price = higher profit incentive**; existing firms produce more and new firms enter.
2. **Increasing marginal opportunity cost:** producing more requires pulling less-suitable resources or paying overtime. Firms must receive a **higher price** to cover the **higher marginal cost** (the supply curve of a firm is essentially its marginal cost curve above minimum AVC; see Unit 3).

### 2.2.3 Market Supply
Market supply is the **horizontal sum** of all individual firms' supply curves. More firms → market supply shifts right.

### 2.2.4 Change in Quantity Supplied vs. Change in Supply
- **Change in quantity supplied:** caused only by a change in the good's **own price** → **movement along** the supply curve.
- **Change in supply:** caused by a **non-price determinant** → **shift** of the supply curve. Increase in supply = shift **right** (down); decrease = shift **left** (up).

### 2.2.5 Determinants of Supply (Shifters)
Memory aid: **ROTTEN** — **R**esource prices, **O**ther goods' prices, **T**echnology, **T**axes/subsidies, **E**xpectations, **N**umber of sellers.

1. **Resource (input) prices:** wages, raw materials, rent. Higher input prices → higher costs → **supply decreases**.
2. **Prices of other goods the firm could produce:** if the price of a good the firm could produce instead rises, the firm shifts resources there, and supply of the original good falls (**substitutes in production**). Goods produced together (**joint products**, e.g., beef and leather) move together.
3. **Technology:** improvements lower the cost per unit → supply increases.
4. **Taxes and subsidies:** a per-unit **tax** raises costs → supply decreases; a **subsidy** lowers costs → supply increases.
5. **Producer expectations:** if firms expect higher future prices, they may withhold supply now (supply decreases today).
6. **Number of sellers:** more firms → supply increases.
7. *(Also:)* **Natural events/weather** (for agriculture) and **government regulation**.

> **Remember:** a supply increase is a shift to the **right**, which can also be read as a **downward** shift (each quantity can be supplied at a lower price). A supply decrease shifts **left/up**.

## 2.3 Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

### 2.3.1 What is Elasticity?
**Elasticity** measures how **responsive** quantity demanded (or supplied) is to a change in price (or income, or another good's price). It is a **unit-free** measure because it uses percentages.

### 2.3.2 Formula
**Price Elasticity of Demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price**

Use the **midpoint (arc) method** for accuracy and to avoid direction bias:

**% change in Q = (Q₂ − Q₁) ÷ [(Q₁ + Q₂)/2] × 100**
**% change in P = (P₂ − P₁) ÷ [(P₁ + P₂)/2] × 100**

Because of the law of demand, the sign is negative; economists **ignore the sign and use the absolute value**.

**Worked example:** Price falls from ₹10 to ₹8; quantity demanded rises from 100 to 130.
- %ΔQ = 30 ÷ 115 = 26.09%
- %ΔP = −2 ÷ 9 = −22.22%
- PED = 26.09 ÷ 22.22 = **1.17 → elastic**.

### 2.3.3 Interpreting the Value

| Absolute value of PED | Type | Meaning | Curve shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **> 1** | **Elastic** | Q changes by a **larger %** than price | Flatter |
| **< 1** | **Inelastic** | Q changes by a **smaller %** than price | Steeper |
| **= 1** | **Unit elastic** | Q changes by the **same %** as price | — |
| **= 0** | **Perfectly inelastic** | Q does not respond at all (e.g., life-saving insulin) | **Vertical** |
| **= ∞** | **Perfectly elastic** | Any price rise → Qd falls to zero | **Horizontal** |

> **Slope is not elasticity.** On a **straight-line** demand curve, slope is constant but elasticity **changes along the curve**: **elastic at high prices (upper left)**, **unit elastic at the midpoint**, **inelastic at low prices (lower right)**. The same is true for all linear demand curves, except the extreme vertical/horizontal cases.

### 2.3.4 Determinants of PED
1. **Availability of substitutes:** more and closer substitutes → **more elastic**. (Pepsi vs. Coke: elastic; salt: inelastic.)
2. **Necessity vs. luxury:** necessities → **inelastic** (medicine, basic food); luxuries → **elastic** (cruises, designer bags).
3. **Proportion of income spent:** bigger share of the budget → **more elastic** (a car) vs. small share → **inelastic** (a pack of matches).
4. **Time horizon:** demand is **more elastic in the long run** because consumers have time to find substitutes (e.g., switching from petrol to electric cars).
5. **How narrowly the market is defined:** "Pepsi" is more elastic than "soft drinks," which is more elastic than "beverages."

### 2.3.5 Total Revenue (TR) and the Total Revenue Test
**TR = Price × Quantity**.

| If demand is... | Price ↑ | Price ↓ |
|---|---|---|
| **Elastic** (>1) | TR **falls** | TR **rises** |
| **Inelastic** (<1) | TR **rises** | TR **falls** |
| **Unit elastic** (=1) | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |

- If price and total revenue move in **opposite** directions → **elastic**.
- If price and total revenue move in the **same** direction → **inelastic**.
- On a linear demand curve, **TR is maximized at the unit-elastic midpoint**.

**Check with the example:** price ₹10 → ₹8: TR rises from ₹1,000 to ₹1,040. Price fell and TR rose (opposite) → elastic ✔.

**Applications:** a firm facing inelastic demand can raise revenue by raising price (farmers, utilities, pharmaceuticals). A firm facing elastic demand should lower price to boost revenue (sales, discounts). Governments tax inelastic goods (tobacco, petrol) to raise revenue with little drop in quantity.

## 2.4 Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

### 2.4.1 Formula
**PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price** (midpoint method).

**Example:** Price rises from ₹5 to ₹6; quantity supplied rises from 40 to 46.
- %ΔQs = 6 ÷ 43 = 13.95%; %ΔP = 1 ÷ 5.5 = 18.18% → **PES = 0.77 → inelastic**.

### 2.4.2 Interpretation
- **PES > 1:** elastic supply. **PES < 1:** inelastic. **PES = 1:** unit elastic.
- **Perfectly inelastic supply (PES = 0):** vertical supply curve; quantity is fixed regardless of price (a famous painting, land in a city, tickets for a sold-out stadium).
- **Perfectly elastic supply (PES = ∞):** horizontal supply curve.
- On a straight-line supply curve that **starts at the origin**, PES = 1 everywhere. A straight-line supply curve intersecting the **price axis** is **elastic** (PES > 1); one intersecting the **quantity axis** is **inelastic** (PES < 1).

### 2.4.3 Determinants of PES
1. **Time (most important):** supply is **more elastic in the long run** (firms build new factories, new firms enter). **Very short run:** often perfectly inelastic.
2. **Availability of inputs / resource flexibility:** if inputs are easy to obtain or move, supply is elastic.
3. **Spare capacity and inventories:** firms with unused capacity or stored stock can increase output quickly.
4. **Mobility of factors:** how easily resources can switch between producing different goods.
5. **Production time:** goods that take a long time to produce (wine, crops) → inelastic in the short run.

## 2.5 Other Elasticities

### 2.5.1 Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (E_xy)
**E_xy = % change in quantity demanded of Good X ÷ % change in the price of Good Y**
- **Positive** → **substitutes** (price of tea ↑ → demand for coffee ↑).
- **Negative** → **complements** (price of printers ↑ → demand for ink cartridges ↓).
- **Zero** (or near zero) → **unrelated** goods.
- The **sign is kept** here (do not take the absolute value). The larger the absolute value, the stronger the relationship.

**Example:** Price of tea rises ₹10 → ₹12 (midpoint %: 2/11 = 18.18%); coffee's quantity demanded rises 50 → 56 (6/53 = 11.32%). E_xy = 11.32 ÷ 18.18 = **+0.62** → substitutes.

### 2.5.2 Income Elasticity of Demand (E_y)
**E_y = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in consumer income**
- **E_y > 0 → normal good.** If **E_y > 1** → **luxury** (income elastic); if **0 < E_y < 1** → **necessity** (income inelastic).
- **E_y < 0 → inferior good.**
- Sign is **kept**.

**Example:** Income rises ₹40,000 → ₹44,000 (4,000/42,000 = 9.52%); quantity rises 20 → 23 (3/21.5 = 13.95%). E_y = **1.47** → normal, luxury.

### 2.5.3 Summary Table

| Elasticity | Sign meaning | Key thresholds |
|---|---|---|
| Price elasticity of demand | Always negative (use absolute value) | 1 = unit elastic |
| Price elasticity of supply | Always positive | 1 = unit elastic |
| Cross-price | + substitutes, − complements | 0 = unrelated |
| Income | + normal, − inferior | >1 luxury, 0–1 necessity |

## 2.6 Market Equilibrium, Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus

### 2.6.1 Market Equilibrium
**Equilibrium** is where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied (**Qd = Qs**) — the point where the demand and supply curves intersect.
- **Equilibrium price (market-clearing price):** the price at which there is **no shortage and no surplus**.
- **Equilibrium quantity:** the quantity bought and sold at that price.
- At equilibrium there is **no tendency to change** (unless something shifts D or S).

### 2.6.2 Worked Numerical Example
Demand: **Qd = 100 − 2P**. Supply: **Qs = −20 + 4P**.
- Set Qd = Qs: 100 − 2P = −20 + 4P → 120 = 6P → **P* = 20**, **Q* = 100 − 40 = 60**.
- Inverse functions (needed for graphing): demand **P = 50 − 0.5Q** (vertical intercept 50); supply **P = (Q + 20)/4 = 5 + 0.25Q** (vertical intercept 5).

### 2.6.3 Consumer Surplus (CS)
**Consumer surplus** = the difference between what consumers are **willing to pay** and what they **actually pay**. Graphically: the area **below the demand curve and above the market price**.
- Example: CS = ½ × base × height = ½ × 60 × (50 − 20) = **900**.
- Lower price → more CS; higher price → less CS.

### 2.6.4 Producer Surplus (PS)
**Producer surplus** = the difference between the **price producers receive** and the **minimum price they would accept** (their marginal cost). Graphically: the area **above the supply curve and below the market price**.
- Example: PS = ½ × 60 × (20 − 5) = **450**.

### 2.6.5 Total Surplus and Efficiency
**Total (social) surplus = CS + PS** = 900 + 450 = **1,350**.
- At competitive equilibrium, **total surplus is maximized**; the market is **allocatively efficient** (marginal benefit = marginal cost) and produces the **socially optimal quantity**.
- Any quantity **below or above** equilibrium reduces total surplus; the lost surplus is the **deadweight loss (DWL)**.

## 2.7 Disequilibrium and Changes in Equilibrium

### 2.7.1 Disequilibrium
- **Surplus (excess supply):** price **above** equilibrium → Qs > Qd. Sellers have unsold goods → they **cut price** → price falls toward equilibrium.
- **Shortage (excess demand):** price **below** equilibrium → Qd > Qs. Buyers compete → **price is bid up** → price rises toward equilibrium.
- Markets move to equilibrium automatically through price adjustments (unless prevented by price controls).

### 2.7.2 Single Curve Shifts (The Four Rules)

| Change | Equilibrium Price | Equilibrium Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| **Demand increases** (right) | ↑ | ↑ |
| **Demand decreases** (left) | ↓ | ↓ |
| **Supply increases** (right) | ↓ | ↑ |
| **Supply decreases** (left) | ↑ | ↓ |

### 2.7.3 Simultaneous Shifts of Both Curves
When both D and S shift, **one variable is determinate and the other is indeterminate** (depends on the relative size of the shifts):

| Demand | Supply | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| ↑ | ↑ | **?** (depends) | **↑** |
| ↑ | ↓ | **↑** | **?** |
| ↓ | ↑ | **↓** | **?** |
| ↓ | ↓ | **?** | **↓** |

### 2.7.4 Steps to Solve Shift Questions
1. Identify the **market** (be specific: "market for electric cars").
2. Decide whether the event affects **demand, supply, or both**, and in which direction.
3. Draw the **original** D and S with equilibrium P₁, Q₁.
4. **Shift** the correct curve(s) and label the new equilibrium P₂, Q₂.
5. State the changes in price and quantity.

**Related markets example:** The price of petrol rises sharply. In the market for **cars (complement)**, demand falls → P↓, Q↓. In the market for **electric cars (substitute for petrol cars)**, demand rises → P↑, Q↑. In the market for **bus travel**, demand rises.

## 2.8 Price Ceilings and Price Floors

### 2.8.1 Price Ceiling
A **price ceiling** is a legal **maximum** price. Typical examples: rent control, caps on medicine prices.
- **Binding only if set BELOW equilibrium price.** A ceiling above equilibrium has no effect.
- **Effects:** Qd > Qs → **shortage**; quantity traded = quantity supplied (smaller). Consequences: waiting lines, **black markets**, **non-price rationing** (favoritism, first-come-first-served), lower quality, underinvestment in maintenance (e.g., housing).
- **Welfare:** some consumers gain (those who get the good at a lower price); some lose (those who cannot buy). **Deadweight loss** exists.

**Numerical example (using Qd = 100 − 2P; Qs = −20 + 4P):** ceiling at **₹15** (below ₹20).
- Qd = 70, Qs = 40 → **shortage of 30**; quantity traded = **40**.
- Willingness to pay for the 40th unit is P = 50 − 0.5(40) = 30. CS = triangle ½ × 40 × (50 − 30) = 400, plus rectangle 40 × (30 − 15) = 600 → **1,000**.
- PS = ½ × 40 × (15 − 5) = **200**. Total surplus = 1,200.
- **DWL = 1,350 − 1,200 = 150** (the triangle between Q = 40 and Q = 60: ½ × 20 × 15).

### 2.8.2 Price Floor
A **price floor** is a legal **minimum** price. Examples: minimum wage, agricultural support prices (MSP).
- **Binding only if set ABOVE equilibrium.**
- **Effects:** Qs > Qd → **surplus**; quantity traded = quantity demanded (smaller). Consequences: unsold goods/unemployment (in labor market, a minimum wage above equilibrium creates unemployment), government may need to **buy the surplus** (cost to taxpayers), producers may waste resources.
- **Welfare:** producers who can still sell benefit; consumers pay more; DWL.

**Numerical example:** floor at **₹25** (above ₹20).
- Qd = 50, Qs = 80 → **surplus of 30**; quantity traded = **50**.
- DWL = ½ × (60 − 50) × (25 − 17.5) = **37.5** (supply price at Q = 50 is 17.5).

### 2.8.3 Key Graph Features for FRQs
- Draw the horizontal control line; **label** it "price ceiling/floor".
- **Shortage/surplus** = horizontal distance between Qd and Qs **at the controlled price**.
- Quantity actually exchanged is the **smaller** of Qd and Qs (the "short side").
- **DWL triangle** lies between the quantity traded and the equilibrium quantity, bounded by the demand and supply curves.

## 2.9 Tax Incidence and Deadweight Loss

### 2.9.1 Per-Unit Tax
A **per-unit (excise) tax** is a fixed amount per unit sold. If imposed on **sellers**, **supply shifts up (left) by the amount of the tax**. If imposed on **buyers**, demand shifts down by the tax. The **economic result is identical** either way.

**Key results of a tax:**
- **Price paid by buyers (P_b)** rises; **price received by sellers (P_s)** falls; **P_b − P_s = tax**.
- **Quantity falls** (below the pre-tax equilibrium).
- **Tax revenue = tax per unit × new quantity.**
- **Deadweight loss (DWL):** the triangle of lost surplus on the units no longer traded; the tax **distorts** the market away from efficiency.

### 2.9.2 Tax Incidence
**Tax incidence** = who actually bears the burden of the tax. The **legal (statutory) incidence** (who writes the cheque) does not determine the **economic incidence**. The burden is shared according to **relative elasticities**:
- **The less elastic side (the one with fewer alternatives) bears more of the tax.**
- If **demand is less elastic than supply** → **buyers** bear more.
- If **supply is less elastic than demand** → **sellers** bear more.
- Perfectly inelastic demand → buyers bear all. Perfectly elastic demand → sellers bear all.

**Numerical example (same market, tax = ₹6 on sellers):**
- New supply: Qs = −20 + 4(P_b − 6) = 4P_b − 44. Set equal to Qd = 100 − 2P_b → 144 = 6P_b → **P_b = 24**, **Q = 52**; sellers receive **P_s = 18**.
- Buyers' burden = 24 − 20 = **₹4**; sellers' burden = 20 − 18 = **₹2** (demand is steeper, i.e., less elastic → buyers bear more).
- **Tax revenue** = 6 × 52 = **₹312**.
- **DWL** = ½ × 6 × (60 − 52) = **₹24**.

### 2.9.3 Elasticity and DWL
**The more elastic demand and/or supply, the greater the fall in quantity, so the larger the deadweight loss** from a given tax. Taxes on inelastic goods are more efficient (smaller DWL).

### 2.9.4 Subsidies
A **per-unit subsidy** is a payment from the government to producers (or consumers) per unit.
- Supply shifts **down (right)** by the subsidy amount.
- **Price paid by buyers falls**, **price received by sellers rises** (above buyers' price), and **quantity rises** above the efficient level.
- **Cost to government** = subsidy per unit × new quantity.
- **DWL (overproduction):** units are produced where marginal cost > marginal benefit.
- **Incidence** again depends on relative elasticities: the **less elastic side** captures more of the subsidy benefit.

## 2.10 International Trade and Public Policy

### 2.10.1 Domestic Market with Trade (Small Country Model)
A **small country** is a **price taker** in the world market: it faces the **world price (P_w)** and can import or export any amount at that price.

**Case 1: P_w < domestic equilibrium price → country IMPORTS**
- Domestic price falls to **P_w**.
- Domestic quantity demanded (Qd) rises; domestic quantity supplied (Qs) falls.
- **Imports = Qd − Qs** (at P_w).
- **Consumer surplus ↑; producer surplus ↓**; **net gain** to society (CS gain > PS loss).

**Case 2: P_w > domestic equilibrium price → country EXPORTS**
- Domestic price rises to **P_w**.
- Qs rises; Qd falls. **Exports = Qs − Qd**.
- **Producer surplus ↑; consumer surplus ↓**; **net gain** to society.

### 2.10.2 Tariffs
A **tariff** is a **tax on imported goods**.
- The domestic price rises to **P_w + tariff**.
- **Imports fall**; domestic production rises; domestic quantity demanded falls.
- **Consumer surplus ↓**, **producer surplus ↑**, **government revenue ↑** (tariff × imports remaining).
- **Net effect:** **DWL** of two triangles: (1) **overproduction** by less-efficient domestic firms (production distortion) and (2) **underconsumption** by consumers (consumption distortion).

### 2.10.3 Quotas
A **quota** is a **legal limit on the quantity** of a good that can be imported.
- Has **similar effects to a tariff**: raises the domestic price, reduces imports, boosts domestic producers, harms consumers.
- **Difference:** with a quota there is **no tariff revenue to the government**; the gap between domestic and world prices (**quota rent**) goes to **license holders / importers / foreign exporters** (unless the government auctions the licenses).
- An **import ban** is a quota of zero.

### 2.10.4 Why Do Governments Restrict Trade?
Reasons often cited: protect domestic jobs and **infant industries**, national security, retaliation, raise revenue. Economists emphasize that restrictions usually produce **net losses** (DWL) for the economy and **concentrated benefits / dispersed costs**.

## Unit 2 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP (midpoint). PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP.
- E_xy = %ΔQ of X ÷ %ΔP of Y. E_y = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔIncome.
- TR = P × Q. CS = area under D above P. PS = area above S below P.
- Total surplus = CS + PS. Tax revenue = tax × quantity after tax.
- Shortage/surplus = |Qd − Qs| at the controlled price.

**Key terms glossary:** demand, quantity demanded, law of demand, substitution effect, income effect, normal good, inferior good, substitutes, complements, supply, law of supply, elasticity, midpoint method, elastic/inelastic/unit elastic, total revenue test, cross-price elasticity, income elasticity, equilibrium, surplus, shortage, consumer surplus, producer surplus, total surplus, deadweight loss, price ceiling, price floor, tax incidence, subsidy, world price, tariff, quota.

**Top exam traps**
1. Price change = **movement along** the curve; non-price factor = **shift**.
2. Elasticity ≠ slope.
3. Use **absolute value** for PED/PES but **keep the sign** for cross-price and income elasticity.
4. A price ceiling is binding only **below** equilibrium; a floor only **above**.
5. Both curves shift → one of P or Q is **indeterminate**.
6. The **less elastic side bears more** of a tax.
7. A quota does not give the government revenue (a tariff does).

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** Which of the following will shift the demand curve for pizza to the right?
(A) A fall in the price of pizza (B) A fall in the price of burgers (C) A rise in the price of burgers (D) A fall in the price of cheese
**Answer: C.** Burgers are a substitute; a higher burger price raises pizza demand. (A is a movement along; D shifts supply.)

**2.** If the price of a good rises 10% and total revenue falls, demand is:
(A) Inelastic (B) Unit elastic (C) Elastic (D) Perfectly inelastic
**Answer: C.** Price and TR move in opposite directions.

**3.** The cross-price elasticity between two goods is −1.8. The goods are:
(A) Substitutes (B) Complements (C) Unrelated (D) Inferior
**Answer: B.**

**4.** A drought reduces wheat production and, at the same time, consumers' incomes rise (wheat is a normal good). In the wheat market:
(A) Price rises, quantity indeterminate (B) Price falls, quantity rises (C) Price indeterminate, quantity falls (D) Price and quantity both fall
**Answer: A.** Supply ↓ and demand ↑ → price ↑ for sure, quantity indeterminate.

**5.** A binding price floor in a market leads to:
(A) A shortage (B) A surplus (C) No change (D) A lower price
**Answer: B.**

**6.** A per-unit tax is placed on a good with very inelastic demand and elastic supply. Who bears most of the tax?
(A) Sellers (B) Buyers (C) Equally shared (D) The government
**Answer: B.**

**7.** A country that is a price taker in the world market removes a tariff on steel. Which results?
(A) Domestic price rises (B) Imports rise (C) Domestic producer surplus rises (D) Consumer surplus falls
**Answer: B.** The domestic price falls to the world price, so imports rise, CS rises, PS falls.

**8.** On a straight-line downward-sloping demand curve, demand is unit elastic:
(A) At the vertical intercept (B) At the midpoint (C) At the horizontal intercept (D) Everywhere
**Answer: B.**

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** The market for wireless earbuds is in equilibrium.
(a) Draw a correctly labeled supply and demand graph showing equilibrium price and quantity.
(b) Suppose the price of smartphones (a complement) falls. Show and explain the effect on the earbud market.
(c) At the same time, a new technology lowers the cost of producing earbuds. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity? Explain which is determinate.
(d) If demand for earbuds is elastic, what happens to total revenue of earbud sellers after a price decrease?

**Answer:**
(a) D slopes down, S slopes up; label P*, Q* at the intersection, axes "Price" and "Quantity of earbuds."
(b) Cheaper smartphones → **demand for earbuds increases** (complements) → D shifts right → P ↑, Q ↑.
(c) Technology → **supply increases** (S shifts right). Combined with the demand increase: **quantity rises for sure (determinate)**; **price is indeterminate** (depends on whether the demand or supply shift is bigger).
(d) Elastic demand: a price decrease **increases** total revenue.

**Q2.** The government imposes a binding price ceiling on apartment rents.
(a) Explain how the ceiling affects the quantity of apartments demanded and supplied.
(b) Identify one effect on the quality of apartments.
(c) Show the deadweight loss on a graph.
(d) Is the DWL larger in the short run or the long run? Why?

**Answer:**
(a) Rent below equilibrium → Qd rises, Qs falls → **shortage**.
(b) Landlords reduce maintenance because tenants queue for scarce units → **quality falls**.
(c) DWL is the triangle between the quantity traded (Qs at the ceiling) and the equilibrium quantity, bounded by D and S.
(d) **Long run:** both demand and supply are more elastic over time, so the quantity traded falls by more and the DWL is larger.
