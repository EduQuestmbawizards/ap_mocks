## 6.1 Socially Efficient and Inefficient Market Outcomes

### 6.1.1 Efficiency Recap
A market outcome is **socially (allocatively) efficient** when it **maximizes total (social) surplus** = consumer surplus + producer surplus (+ any effects on third parties). This occurs when **marginal social benefit (MSB) = marginal social cost (MSC)**.

In a perfectly competitive market with **no externalities**, the equilibrium (where private marginal benefit = private marginal cost) is automatically socially efficient: the demand curve shows **marginal benefit** to consumers and the supply curve shows **marginal cost** to producers.

### 6.1.2 Market Failure
**Market failure** occurs when a market, left on its own, **fails to allocate resources efficiently** (the quantity produced is **not** where MSB = MSC), creating **deadweight loss (DWL)**. Main sources:
1. **Externalities** (spillover costs or benefits to third parties).
2. **Public goods** (non-rival and non-excludable; the free-rider problem).
3. **Market power** (monopoly, oligopoly; P > MC; see Unit 4).
4. **Imperfect or asymmetric information** (e.g., **adverse selection**: sellers know more about quality than buyers; **moral hazard**: people take more risk when someone else bears the cost).
5. **Common resources** (overuse; "tragedy of the commons").

### 6.1.3 Key Definitions
- **Marginal private benefit (MPB):** benefit to the buyer of one more unit (= the demand curve in an unregulated market).
- **Marginal private cost (MPC):** cost to the seller of one more unit (= the supply curve).
- **Marginal external benefit (MEB) / cost (MEC):** benefit/cost imposed on third parties for one more unit.
- **MSB = MPB + MEB.** **MSC = MPC + MEC.**
- **Socially optimal quantity:** where **MSB = MSC**.
- **Deadweight loss:** the loss of total surplus from producing a quantity other than the socially optimal quantity.

### 6.1.4 Deadweight Loss Rule
- **Overproduction** (Q_market > Q_optimal): units are produced where **MSC > MSB** (cost to society exceeds benefit) → DWL.
- **Underproduction** (Q_market < Q_optimal): units not produced where **MSB > MSC** (potential gains lost) → DWL.
The DWL is the **triangle** between the MSB and MSC curves from the market quantity to the optimal quantity.

## 6.2 Externalities

### 6.2.1 Definition
An **externality** is a **cost or benefit that falls on a third party** (someone who is not the buyer or seller) and is **not reflected in the market price.** Externalities cause the market to produce the **wrong quantity**.

### 6.2.2 Negative Externalities (External Costs)
Examples: pollution from factories, traffic congestion, second-hand smoke, noise, antibiotic overuse.
- The firm ignores the **external cost**, so **MSC > MPC** (the social cost curve lies **above** the supply curve).
- **Market produces too much** (overproduction) at **too low a price**: Q_market > Q_optimal.
- **DWL** arises from units where MSC > MSB.

### 6.2.3 Positive Externalities (External Benefits)
Examples: education, vaccinations, basic research, beekeeping next to an orchard, public parks, home gardens that improve the street's look.
- Buyers ignore the **external benefit**, so **MSB > MPB** (the social benefit curve lies **above** the demand curve).
- **Market produces too little** (underproduction): Q_market < Q_optimal.
- **DWL** arises from units where MSB > MSC that are not produced.

### 6.2.4 Numerical Example: Negative Externality
- **MPB (demand): P = 60 − Q**
- **MPC (supply): P = 10 + Q**
- **External cost = ₹10 per unit** → **MSC = 20 + Q**

| | Market | Social optimum |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | MPB = MPC: 60 − Q = 10 + Q | MSB = MSC: 60 − Q = 20 + Q |
| Quantity | **Q = 25** | **Q = 20** |
| Price | **₹35** | **₹40** |

**DWL** = ½ × (25 − 20) × (MSC − MSB at Q = 25) = ½ × 5 × (45 − 35) = **₹25**.

### 6.2.5 Numerical Example: Positive Externality
- **MPB: P = 50 − Q**; **MPC: P = 10 + Q**; **external benefit = ₹10 per unit** → **MSB = 60 − Q**.
- Market: 50 − Q = 10 + Q → **Q = 20**, P = ₹30.
- Social optimum: 60 − Q = 10 + Q → **Q = 25**, P = ₹35.
- **DWL** = ½ × (25 − 20) × (MSB − MSC at Q = 20) = ½ × 5 × (40 − 30) = **₹25**.

### 6.2.6 Solutions to Negative Externalities
1. **Pigouvian (corrective) tax:** a per-unit tax **equal to the marginal external cost** at the optimal quantity. It **internalizes the externality**: the supply curve shifts up by the tax, so the market equilibrium moves to the socially optimal quantity.
   *Example:* a ₹10 tax → new supply P = 20 + Q = MSC → **Q = 20**; buyers pay ₹40, sellers receive ₹30; tax revenue = 10 × 20 = ₹200; **DWL eliminated**.
2. **Regulation / command-and-control:** direct limits on emissions, mandated technology, bans.
3. **Tradable permits (cap-and-trade):** government sets an overall cap on pollution and issues permits that firms can buy and sell; firms that can cut pollution cheaply sell permits to those with high abatement costs → pollution is reduced at the **lowest total cost**.
4. **Property rights and the Coase theorem:** **if property rights are clearly defined and transaction costs are low, private bargaining can solve the externality** regardless of who initially holds the rights (it fails when many parties are involved or transaction costs are high).
5. **Social pressure, information campaigns, liability laws.**

> **The optimal level of pollution is NOT zero.** Cleaning up has increasing marginal costs (marginal abatement cost). The optimal level is where the **marginal benefit of abating** (reduced damage) **= marginal cost of abating.**

### 6.2.7 Solutions to Positive Externalities
1. **Subsidy** to buyers or sellers equal to the **marginal external benefit** → shifts demand (or supply) so the market produces the socially optimal quantity.
   *Example:* a ₹10 subsidy to buyers → effective demand P = 60 − Q = MSB → **Q = 25**; **DWL eliminated**; cost to government = 10 × 25 = ₹250.
2. **Government provision** (public schools, public parks).
3. **Mandates** (compulsory vaccination or schooling), **tax credits**, **patents** (to reward innovators).

### 6.2.8 Common Mistakes
- **Do not shift the wrong curve.** A negative production externality shifts the **MSC (supply-side social cost)** above supply; a positive consumption externality shifts **MSB** above demand.
- **Negative consumption externalities** (e.g., smoking) → **MSB < MPB** (social benefit curve **below** demand) → overconsumption.
- **Positive production externalities** (e.g., beekeeping) → **MSC < MPC** → underproduction.
- The Pigouvian tax equals the **external** cost per unit **at the optimum**, not the total external cost.

## 6.3 Public and Private Goods

### 6.3.1 Two Key Characteristics
- **Rival in consumption:** one person's use **reduces** the amount available to others.
- **Excludable:** it is possible to **prevent** non-payers from using the good.

### 6.3.2 The Four Types of Goods

| | **Excludable** | **Non-excludable** |
|---|---|---|
| **Rival** | **Private goods** (food, clothing, cars, a pizza) | **Common resources** (fish in the ocean, clean air, public pasture) |
| **Non-rival** | **Club (natural monopoly) goods** (cable TV, toll roads, Netflix, uncrowded gym) | **Public goods** (national defense, lighthouse, street lights, flood control) |

### 6.3.3 Public Goods and the Free-Rider Problem
- **Public goods** are **non-rival** and **non-excludable**.
- **Free-rider problem:** since people can't be excluded, each has an **incentive to enjoy the good without paying**. If everyone free-rides, private firms cannot profit from providing it → the market provides **too little (or none)** → **market failure**.
- **Solution:** the **government provides** the good and finances it through **taxes**.

### 6.3.4 Optimal Quantity of a Public Good
Because the good is non-rival, **everyone consumes the same quantity**, so the **market demand (marginal social benefit) is the VERTICAL sum** of individual demand curves (add the prices each person is willing to pay at each quantity) — unlike private goods, where we sum **horizontally**.

**Rule:** provide the quantity where **MSB (vertical sum) = MC**.

**Example:** Person A: P = 10 − Q; Person B: P = 6 − Q. **MSB = 16 − 2Q** (valid up to Q = 6). If MC = ₹8: 16 − 2Q = 8 → **Q = 4**. At Q = 4, A is willing to pay 6 and B 2 → total ₹8 = MC ✔.

### 6.3.5 Common Resources and the Tragedy of the Commons
- A **common resource** is **rival but non-excludable**. Each user ignores the cost their use imposes on others → **overuse and depletion** (overfishing, overgrazing, congestion, groundwater depletion). This is a **negative externality**.
- **Solutions:** **quotas/catch limits**, **taxes or fees**, **tradable permits**, **privatization / assigning property rights**, community management agreements.

### 6.3.6 Club Goods and Quasi-Public Goods
- **Club goods**: non-rival (up to capacity) but excludable (e.g., subscription TV). Often supplied by **natural monopolies**; can be regulated.
- **Quasi-public (merit) goods**: private goods with **large positive externalities** (education, vaccines, healthcare) that governments subsidize or provide.

### 6.3.7 Cost-Benefit Analysis for Public Projects
Governments should undertake a public project if the **total social benefits exceed the total social costs**, and expand it until **MSB = MSC**. Sunk costs are ignored.

## 6.4 The Effects of Government Intervention in Different Market Structures

### 6.4.1 Recap: Intervention in Competitive Markets (Unit 2)
- **Price ceiling (binding)** → shortage and DWL.
- **Price floor (binding)** → surplus and DWL.
- **Per-unit tax** → higher P_b, lower P_s, lower Q, DWL, revenue; burden by elasticity.
- **Subsidy** → higher Q, DWL (when no externality exists), cost to government.
- **Exceptions:** when externalities exist, a **well-designed tax or subsidy can improve** efficiency.

### 6.4.2 Government Intervention in a Monopoly
Use the monopoly from Unit 4: **P = 100 − Q**, **MR = 100 − 2Q**, **MC = ₹20**, **TFC = ₹400** (**ATC = 20 + 400/Q**). Unregulated: **Q = 40, P = ₹60, profit = ₹1,200.**

**(a) Lump-sum (fixed) tax** (e.g., ₹500): increases fixed cost only; **MC and MR do not change** → **price and output do not change**; profit falls (to ₹700).

**(b) Per-unit tax** (e.g., ₹10): raises MC to ₹30 → MR = MC: 100 − 2Q = 30 → **Q = 35, P = ₹65** (price rises by only ₹5; output falls). The monopolist passes on part of the tax. If the monopoly produces an **externality**, such a tax could be helpful.

**(c) Price ceiling on a monopoly** — **opposite to competitive markets: it can INCREASE output.**
- With a ceiling at the competitive price (₹20 = MC), the firm's MR becomes **flat at ₹20** up to Q = 80 → it produces **Q = 80 at P = ₹20** (efficient), but profit = −₹400 (a loss equal to the fixed cost; a natural monopoly would need a subsidy).
- With a ceiling at **₹40**: MR = ₹40 up to Q = 60 (where demand = ₹40), MC = 20 < 40 → the firm produces **Q = 60 at P = ₹40**: **price falls from ₹60 to ₹40, output rises from 40 to 60**, profit = (40 − ATC(60) = 26.67) × 60 ≈ **₹800** (still positive).
- A ceiling set **above the unregulated price (₹60)** has no effect.

**(d) Regulation of natural monopoly** (Unit 4): **P = MC** (needs a subsidy), **P = ATC** (fair return; zero economic profit), or **public ownership**.

**(e) Subsidy to a monopoly:** raises output, but may worsen inequality by increasing monopoly profit; may be justified if there is a positive externality.

**(f) Antitrust policy:** prevent mergers that reduce competition, break up monopolies, prohibit price-fixing, regulate predatory behavior.

### 6.4.3 Government Intervention in Monopolistic Competition and Oligopoly
- **Antitrust laws** limit collusion and mergers (cartels are illegal in many countries).
- **Information rules** (truth in advertising, labeling) reduce asymmetric information.
- **Regulation of entry barriers** (limiting excessive patent lives, licensing).
- **Taxes/subsidies** can correct externalities from advertising or product design.

### 6.4.4 Government Intervention in Factor Markets
- **Minimum wage:** unemployment in competitive labor markets; may increase employment in monopsony (Unit 5).
- **Taxes on labor:** raise firms' cost, lower employment.
- **Education/training subsidies:** raise human capital and MRP.

### 6.4.5 Government Failure
Government intervention can fail too: **lobbying and rent-seeking**, **imperfect information** to set the correct tax, **political incentives**, **administrative costs**, **unintended consequences**. The existence of market failure does not automatically justify intervention; the benefits should exceed the costs.

## 6.5 Inequality

### 6.5.1 Income Distribution and Poverty
- **Income inequality:** unequal distribution of income among households. Often measured by dividing households into **quintiles** (five groups, 20% each) and comparing the share of total income each group earns.
- **Poverty line:** an income level below which a household is considered poor.
- Causes of inequality: differences in **human capital**, **ability and effort**, **inheritance and wealth**, **discrimination**, **market power**, **technology and globalization**, **luck**, **family structure** and **institutions**.

### 6.5.2 The Lorenz Curve
The **Lorenz curve** plots the **cumulative percentage of households** (horizontal axis, from poorest to richest) against the **cumulative percentage of total income** (vertical axis).
- The **45° line** is the **line of perfect equality** (the bottom 20% receive 20% of income, etc.).
- The **farther the Lorenz curve bows away from the line of equality, the greater the inequality.**
- The Lorenz curve always lies **on or below** the line of equality and passes through (0,0) and (100,100).

### 6.5.3 The Gini Coefficient
**Gini coefficient = Area A ÷ (Area A + Area B)**
where **A** = area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve; **B** = area below the Lorenz curve (A + B = the triangle under the equality line = 0.5).
- **Range 0 to 1:** **0 = perfect equality; 1 = perfect inequality** (one household has all the income).
- **Higher Gini = greater inequality.**

**Example:** household quintiles' income shares (poorest to richest): 5%, 10%, 15%, 25%, 45%.
- Cumulative shares: 5%, 15%, 30%, 55%, 100% → Lorenz points (20, 5), (40, 15), (60, 30), (80, 55), (100, 100).
- Area B (trapezoids) ≈ 0.2 × [(0 + 0.05)/2 + (0.05 + 0.15)/2 + (0.15 + 0.30)/2 + (0.30 + 0.55)/2 + (0.55 + 1)/2] = 0.2 × 1.55 = **0.31**.
- A = 0.5 − 0.31 = 0.19 → **Gini ≈ 0.19 ÷ 0.5 = 0.38.**

### 6.5.4 Tax Structures
- **Progressive tax:** the **average tax rate rises as income rises** (the rich pay a higher percentage). Example: income tax with higher rates on higher brackets.
- **Proportional (flat) tax:** the **same percentage** for all incomes.
- **Regressive tax:** the **average tax rate falls as income rises** (the poor pay a higher percentage of income). Example: sales tax on necessities, payroll tax capped at a ceiling, cigarette taxes.
- **Average tax rate = total tax ÷ income. Marginal tax rate = ΔTax ÷ ΔIncome** (the rate paid on the **next** rupee earned).
- *Example:* Income ₹20,000 → tax ₹2,000 (10%); income ₹1,00,000 → tax ₹30,000 (30%) → **progressive**.

### 6.5.5 Principles of Taxation
- **Ability-to-pay principle:** those with higher incomes should pay more (supports progressive taxes).
- **Benefits-received principle:** people who benefit from a government service should pay for it (fuel taxes for roads, user fees).

### 6.5.6 Policies to Reduce Inequality
- **Progressive taxation** and **transfer payments** (welfare, unemployment benefits, food assistance, negative income tax / earned income tax credit).
- **Public provision of education, health care** (raises human capital).
- **Minimum wage laws** (mixed effects).
- **Anti-discrimination laws.**

### 6.5.7 The Equity–Efficiency Trade-off
Redistribution can **reduce incentives to work, save and invest** (higher taxes reduce the reward for effort; benefits may reduce work incentives) and cost administrative resources (**"leaky bucket"** analogy: when you carry money from rich to poor, some leaks away). Yet it increases **equity** and may raise social welfare; society must decide how much efficiency to sacrifice for fairness. This is a **normative** judgment.

## Unit 6 Quick Reference

**Key formulas**
- MSB = MPB + MEB; MSC = MPC + MEC. **Optimal: MSB = MSC.**
- Pigouvian tax (or subsidy) = **marginal external cost (or benefit) at the optimum.**
- Public good: **MSB = vertical sum of individual demand**; provide where MSB = MC.
- Gini = A ÷ (A + B), between 0 and 1.
- Average tax rate = Tax ÷ Income; Marginal tax rate = ΔTax ÷ ΔIncome.

**Key terms glossary:** market failure, social surplus, marginal social benefit/cost, externality (positive/negative), internalize, Pigouvian tax, subsidy, cap-and-trade, Coase theorem, public good, private good, common resource, club good, rival, excludable, free-rider problem, tragedy of the commons, asymmetric information, adverse selection, moral hazard, antitrust, government failure, Lorenz curve, Gini coefficient, progressive/regressive/proportional tax, equity-efficiency trade-off.

**Top exam traps**
1. **Negative externality → overproduction; positive externality → underproduction.**
2. The social cost/benefit curve shifts **relative to the private curve**, not the other way round.
3. The corrective tax equals the **external cost per unit**, not the full price.
4. Public goods: **vertical** summation; private goods: **horizontal.**
5. A **price ceiling on a monopoly** can **raise output** (unlike in competition).
6. A **lump-sum tax** on a monopolist does **not** change P or Q.
7. Optimal pollution is **not zero**.
8. **Higher Gini = more inequality** (not more equality).

## Practice Questions

### Multiple Choice
**1.** A factory's production creates air pollution that harms nearby residents. Compared with the socially optimal quantity, the market quantity is:
(A) Too low (B) Too high (C) Efficient (D) Zero
**Answer: B.** Ignoring the external cost, MSC > MPC → overproduction.

**2.** The appropriate per-unit tax to correct a negative externality equals:
(A) The market price (B) The marginal private cost (C) The marginal external cost at the optimal quantity (D) Total pollution damage
**Answer: C.**

**3.** A good that is non-rival and non-excludable is a:
(A) Private good (B) Public good (C) Common resource (D) Club good
**Answer: B.**

**4.** The free-rider problem leads to:
(A) Overproduction of public goods (B) Underprovision of public goods by the private market (C) A surplus of private goods (D) A shortage of common resources
**Answer: B.**

**5.** Which of these best illustrates the tragedy of the commons?
(A) A private firm overcharges for its product (B) Overfishing in international waters (C) Underproduction of vaccines (D) A cartel restricting output
**Answer: B.**

**6.** A monopoly faces a binding price ceiling set below its unregulated price but above its MC and ATC. Compared with no regulation, output will:
(A) Fall (B) Rise (C) Stay the same (D) Fall to zero
**Answer: B.**

**7.** Moving from a regressive tax to a progressive tax will, most likely:
(A) Increase the Gini coefficient (B) Reduce income inequality after taxes (C) Reduce the equity (D) Not change the Lorenz curve
**Answer: B.**

**8.** If the Lorenz curve lies exactly on the 45° line, the Gini coefficient is:
(A) 1 (B) 0.5 (C) 0 (D) Undefined
**Answer: C.**

### Free Response (FRQ style)
**Q1.** A paper mill sells paper in a perfectly competitive market and dumps waste into a river, imposing a cost on downstream fishermen.
(a) Draw a correctly labeled graph showing the market equilibrium, the socially optimal quantity, and the deadweight loss.
(b) Explain why the market quantity exceeds the socially optimal quantity.
(c) Describe one policy that could achieve the optimal quantity and show its effect on the graph.
(d) Explain why the optimal quantity of pollution is unlikely to be zero.

**Answer:**
(a) Demand (MPB = MSB), supply (MPC), and a higher **MSC curve** above supply. Market equilibrium at MPB = MPC (Q_m); optimum at MSB = MSC (Q\*, where Q\* < Q_m). DWL = triangle between Q\* and Q_m bounded by MSC above and MSB below.
(b) The firm ignores the external cost, so its private cost is lower than the social cost → it supplies more at each price → overproduction.
(c) A **Pigouvian tax per unit equal to MEC at Q\*** shifts the supply curve up to MSC; the new equilibrium is at Q\* and DWL disappears. (Alternatives: tradable permits, regulation.)
(d) Reducing pollution has increasing marginal abatement costs; at some point, the marginal cost of further cleanup exceeds the marginal benefit.

**Q2.** Two neighbors value a street light. Neighbor 1's demand is P = 12 − Q and neighbor 2's demand is P = 8 − Q. The marginal cost of each light is ₹10.
(a) Derive the marginal social benefit.
(b) Find the optimal number of lights.
(c) Explain why a private firm would not supply the optimal number.

**Answer:**
(a) Vertical sum: **MSB = 20 − 2Q** (valid up to Q = 8).
(b) 20 − 2Q = 10 → **Q = 5**.
(c) Street lights are non-rival and non-excludable, so each neighbor has an incentive to **free-ride**; a private firm cannot collect payment from all beneficiaries, so it would supply too few (or none).
